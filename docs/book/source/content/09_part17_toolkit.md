PART XVII — THE AKURA TOOLKIT

PARTINTRO: This final Part turns the ideas of the book into tools a coach can use on Monday morning. It gives AKURA pace and race-equivalence charts, explains how to adjust them for Chennai heat and hill-station altitude, and sets out three roadmaps: a 24-week marathon roadmap, a long-term athlete development roadmap, and the research roadmap that will take the AKURA Endurance Index from prototype to validated tool. ## STORY: Three Minutes Eight Seconds

Valencia, 7 December 2025. The Spanish city's marathon course is famous for being flat and fast, and on this morning Thonakal Gopi is running the race of his life. He crosses the line in 2:12:23. It is a personal best. It is also 23 seconds outside the national record of 2:12:00 that Shivnath Singh set at Jalandhar in 1978, a record that has outlived almost every runner who ever chased it.

Take a calculator and divide 2:12:23 by 42.195. The answer is about 3:08 per kilometre. Not one fast kilometre: forty-two of them, one after another, each covered in about three minutes and eight seconds. Most club runners in India would struggle to hold that pace for one lap of a track. Gopi held it for more than two hours.

This is how coaches think about races. Not as a single time, but as a pace, a rhythm, a number per kilometre that can be trained, tested and planned. It is exactly how this final Part of the book thinks too.

### From Wayanad

Gopi comes from Sulthan Bathery in Wayanad, Kerala, a hill town about 1,000 metres above sea level. Picture the district in the monsoon: rain drumming on the pepper vines, red earth roads running between paddy fields and forest. His parents were farmers who grew rice and ginger. He joined the Army through the sports quota and became a Havildar in the Artillery regiment, attached to the Army Sports Institute in Pune.

His events were the 10,000 m and the marathon. Under Army coach Surendra Singh Bhandari he spent what ESPN described as a ten-month training stint at a high-altitude facility in Ooty before the 2016 Rio Olympics. In Rio he finished 25th in 2:15:25, then a personal best, one second ahead of his Army team-mate Kheta Ram.

### Champion of Asia

The following year, at the Asian Marathon Championship in Dongguan, China, Gopi won gold in 2:15:48. No Indian had won the title before him. The next Indian to win it was Man Singh, in Hong Kong in 2024.

The wins kept coming at home. In January 2018 he won the Indian elite race at the Mumbai Marathon in 2:16:51, with Nitendra Singh Rawat second in 2:16:54. In Seoul in 2019 he ran 2:13:39, a new personal best. At Mumbai in 2023 he was again the first Indian man, in 2:16:41.

Then came Valencia. For that race he went back to the Nilgiris, spending four months training at Ooty, while Bengaluru had been his base for the previous six years. Afterwards he said simply: "I think the high altitude at Ooty helped my performance."

Four months after Valencia, in April 2026, he ran the Rotterdam Marathon and finished 23rd in 2:13:16, well inside the Athletics Federation of India's Asian Games standard of 2:15:04. In the same race, Sawan Barwal ran 2:11:58 on his marathon debut and finally broke Shivnath Singh's record, by two seconds. The old mark that Gopi had missed by 23 seconds in December fell to a younger man in April. That is how records usually fall: not to one hero but to a generation pushing on the same door.

### A career as a pace chart

Now do what this Part asks every coach to do. Turn Gopi's results into paces.

Rio 2016, 2:15:25: about 3:13 per kilometre. Dongguan 2017, 2:15:48: also about 3:13. Seoul 2019, 2:13:39: about 3:10. Valencia 2025, 2:12:23: about 3:08. Rotterdam 2026, 2:13:16: just under 3:10.

Across nine years, the average pace moved by about five seconds per kilometre. Five seconds sounds like nothing; across 42 kilometres it is roughly three and a half minutes. For an elite marathoner, that is the difference between being a good national runner and knocking on the door of a 48-year-old record. The improvement did not arrive in a single leap. It came in small steps, separated by years, with results that went up and down along the way. A coach who looked only at Rotterdam, a slower run than Valencia, might have called it a decline. A coach who looked at the whole line would see a runner still holding paces that, in 2016, had been beyond him.

Chart 1 in the next chapter uses a well-known formula, Riegel's endurance equation, to translate a 5K time into equivalent performances at longer distances. Run the arithmetic backwards from Gopi's 2:12:23 and the model says it corresponds to a 5K of roughly 13:48. That is not Gopi's actual 5K time; it is simply what the formula implies. It is also well off the top of the chart, which begins at 15:00, because the chart is built for club runners and developing athletes, not for Asian champions. Even for elites, though, the logic is the same. A marathon pace is a fraction of what the athlete can do over shorter distances, and how large that fraction is depends on durability, economy, fuelling and heat.

### A career as a roadmap

Gopi's preparations also read like roadmaps. Ten months at Ooty before Rio. Four months at Ooty before Valencia, followed four months later by Rotterdam. Sawan Barwal's group, according to The Bridge, followed a similar rhythm: about four months at Ooty and Wellington from November to February, then pre-competition work in Bengaluru. Each is a sequence of phases: a long base, a specific block, a taper, a race and then a review before the next cycle begins.

The 24-week marathon roadmap in Chapter 87 is a shorter, simpler version of the same idea, built for runners who have jobs, families and one target race a year. The athlete development roadmap in Chapter 88 is the long version, the one that describes how a boy from a Wayanad farm becomes an Army runner, then an Olympian, then champion of Asia. And the research roadmap in Chapter 89 is about making sure that the next time an Indian runner spends four months at Ooty and runs a personal best, we know more precisely what the mountain contributed.

### What the charts are for

A pace chart will not make anyone a champion. Gopi's 3:08 per kilometre rests on years of Army training, altitude blocks, coaches, races won and races lost. But charts do something important: they make training honest. They turn a vague intention such as "run easy" or "do some tempo" into a number that can be checked against heart rate, effort and the weather, and then corrected.

Charts also need adjusting, and in India that usually means heat. A pace that is comfortable on a cool December morning in the Nilgiris can be dangerous on a humid May morning in Chennai. Chapter 86 explains how to let the pace fall while the effort stays the same, and why a heart-rate ceiling and a stop rule matter more than any number on a chart. Altitude changes the arithmetic too: the first days in Ooty are slower, and a runner who chases sea-level splits up there is fighting the mountain rather than learning from it. An elite marathoner and a first-time half-marathoner face the same physics. The difference is only in the numbers. The principle, that the body sets the pace and the chart is only the starting point, applies to both.

Somewhere in Kerala tonight, a young runner will look at a race result and wonder what it means. The tools in this Part are built to answer that question, on an ordinary Monday morning, for an ordinary athlete with extraordinary hopes. We start with the charts.

## 85. The AKURA Pace Charts

Rotterdam, 12 April 2026. Sawan Barwal, a soldier from Mandi district in Himachal Pradesh, is running his first marathon. He finishes 20th in 2:11:58, and a national record that had stood since Shivnath Singh ran 2:12:00 at Jalandhar in 1978 is gone by two seconds. Divide his time by 42.195 and you get the number every coach instinctively reaches for: about 3:08 per kilometre, held for more than two hours. In September, at the Asian Games in Nagoya, he lowers the record to 2:11:37 and wins silver. "There was only one thing in my mind: I have to go till the finishing line and how I can maintain the pace," he told the Tribune. Maintain the pace. For a national record-holder and for a beginner in Anna Nagar alike, training starts with the same question: what pace, and why that one?

Every coach eventually needs a simple table: "My athlete just ran this race. What paces should they train at?" Pace charts such as Jack Daniels' VDOT tables have helped a generation of runners answer that question. AKURA's charts serve the same practical purpose, but they are built from a transparent, openly stated model so that any coach can check the arithmetic, and they are designed to be adjusted by the environment and readiness layers described in earlier chapters.

### How the charts are calculated

The charts start from one recent, honest race result, ideally a 5K run on a flat course in cool conditions. From that anchor, equivalent performances at other distances are estimated with Riegel's well-known endurance formula: predicted time = known time × (new distance ÷ known distance) raised to the power 1.06. The same relationship is used to estimate the pace an athlete could hold for a given duration, which gives the training paces.

- **Threshold pace** is the estimated pace the athlete could race for about 60 minutes. It should feel controlled-hard.
- **Power pace** is the estimated pace for an all-out effort of about 11 minutes, used for VO₂max-type intervals of 2–5 minutes.
- **Speed** is given as a 400 m repetition time, based on the pace for an all-out effort of about 5 minutes, used with full recovery.
- **Marathon pace** is the Riegel marathon prediction divided by 42.195 km.
- **Recovery, Aerobic and Endurance** paces are set as percentages of threshold speed: Recovery slower than 70%, Aerobic 70–80%, and Endurance 80–88%.

KEY: AKURA pace charts turn one recent race into a full set of training paces using a published, transparent formula. They are a starting point: heart rate, effort, heat and readiness decide the final pace on the day.

### Using the charts safely

A chart is only as good as its anchor. An old race, a downhill course, a race run on a cool December morning or a time trial run while unwell will all give misleading paces. Where the athlete has no recent race, use a 5K time trial on a flat measured course, run early in the morning, after two easy days.

The easy paces are deliberately wide. On hot days, after poor sleep or in the first days at altitude, athletes should run towards the slow end of the range or slower. Easy running is defined by effort and heart rate first; the pace column is a guide, not a target.

## 86. Adjusting Paces for Heat and Altitude

Bengaluru, April 2026. The TCS World 10K is run in hot, humid conditions, and the Indian women's race is won by Sanjivani Jadhav in 35:01, her third straight title. Her own event record, set in 2018, is 33:38. Nobody can say from the result sheet how much of that 83-second gap belongs to the weather and how much to fitness, tactics or the eight years between the two races. That uncertainty is exactly the problem a coach faces every summer in Chennai. A runner who covered an aerobic loop at 5:30 per kilometre in January covers the same loop at 6:00 in May and comes home convinced she is losing fitness. Often she is not. She is paying the price of running in heat, and that price is paid in pace. The question is how to set training paces that respect it.

A pace chart assumes a cool, still, sea-level day. Chennai in May is not that day, and neither is the first week in Ooty. This chapter gives AKURA's working adjustments. They are practical coaching guidance drawn from the physiology in Parts III and IV, not validated equations, and they are always overruled by heart rate, perceived effort and warning signs.

### Heat and humidity

Marathon results slow progressively as environmental heat stress rises, and slower runners, who are exposed for longer, tend to be affected more. In humid heat, sweat evaporates poorly, so heart rate climbs at the same pace. The practical response is to hold effort, not pace.

|WBGT (°C)|Easy and endurance runs|Threshold and interval sessions|Races and long runs|
|Below 18|Use chart paces|Use chart paces|Use chart paces|
|18–23|Run by heart rate; often 2–4% slower|Slow by about 2–4%, or shorten reps|Expect a small slowdown; drink to plan|
|23–28|Run by heart rate; often 5–8% slower|Move to cooler hours, cut volume, slow by 5% or more|Reduce goals; heat-acclimated athletes only|
|Above 28|Short, very easy, or move indoors|Postpone|Follow medical and event guidance; consider not racing|

On a humid Chennai evening, a 5:30/km aerobic run might become 5:45–6:00/km at the same heart rate. That is not lost fitness; it is the price of thermoregulation. The AEI records the environment so that the slower pace is interpreted correctly.

KEY: In heat, hold the effort and let the pace fall. A heart-rate ceiling and a stop rule protect the athlete better than any pace target.

### Altitude

At the moderate altitudes of Ooty and Kodaikanal, oxygen pressure is roughly a quarter lower than at sea level. Easy running feels harder and fast intervals are noticeably slower in the first days.

|Camp phase|Easy running|Threshold|Power and speed|
|Days 1–4|By effort only; expect clearly slower paces|Avoid|Avoid|
|Days 5–10|By effort; paces begin to return|By effort; typically a few per cent slower than sea-level pace|Short reps with long recovery; do not chase sea-level times|
|Days 11 onwards|Close to normal effort|By heart rate and RPE|Consider descending to lower ground for key fast sessions|
|Return to sea level|Normal|Normal after a few days|Retest before racing|

COACH: Write two numbers on every hot-weather or altitude session plan: the heart-rate ceiling and the stop rule. For example: "Easy 50 minutes, heart rate below 150; stop and cool down if dizzy, confused, nauseous or if sweating stops." The pace is recorded afterwards, not chased during the run.

## 87. The 24-Week Marathon Roadmap

The record Sawan Barwal broke in Rotterdam was built on a calendar. According to The Bridge, his group in Reliance Foundation's Project 2:09, coached by Ajith Markose, spent about four months, from November to February, at Ooty and Wellington in the Nilgiris, then moved to Bengaluru for pre-competition work. "For Sawan, most of the time he was staying in high altitude," the report quoted. The Tribune described a load of about 200 kilometres a week, with 40–42 km long runs on alternate weeks. Those numbers belong to a professional athlete training full-time, and no club runner should copy them. What any runner can copy is the structure: a long base in one place, a sharper block in another, a deliberate sequence of phases, each with its own purpose, all pointing at a single race date circled months in advance.

A marathon is built over months, not weeks. The AKURA marathon roadmap divides 24 weeks into five phases, each with a clear purpose, a key session type and a planned retest. The roadmap is a map, not a timetable: each phase ends only when the athlete has absorbed it, and the AEI trend decides whether to move on, repeat or reduce.

FIG: f31_marathon_roadmap.png | The AKURA 24-week marathon roadmap: five phases, four testing points, a planned heat block for a warm-weather race and a progressive long run.

### The five phases

|Weeks|Phase|Main purpose|Key sessions|Test at end|
|1–6|Foundation|Consistency, aerobic volume, strength habit|Aerobic runs, strides, strength twice a week|5K time trial; easy pace at fixed HR|
|7–12|Aerobic build|Raise volume and long-run length|Long run, Endurance runs, one Threshold session|Threshold test; long-run decoupling|
|13–18|Specific strength|Sustain higher fractions of capacity|Threshold, marathon-pace blocks in long runs|Half-marathon or 15K race|
|19–21|Race-specific|Rehearse race pace, fuelling and heat plan|Long runs with marathon-pace finish; heat sessions if race is warm|Final long run review|
|22–24|Taper and race|Arrive fresh, keep sharpness|Reduced volume, short marathon-pace efforts|Race|

### Progression rules

- Increase the long run gradually, typically by 2–3 km every one to two weeks, with a lighter week every third or fourth week.
- Add only one new stress at a time: volume, intensity, heat or hills.
- If AEI-Readiness is repeatedly low, or pace-HR decoupling on long runs worsens, hold the current week rather than progressing.
- Practise race-day fuelling in every long run from week 13 onwards.
- For a warm race, schedule a controlled 10–14 day heat-acclimation block in weeks 19–21, as described in Chapter 20, not in the final week.

KEY: The roadmap moves forward only when the athlete has absorbed the current phase. Testing points turn the plan into a feedback loop.

COACH: Put the four testing weeks into the calendar on day one and treat them as fixed appointments. The results are what allow you to adjust the plan with confidence rather than guessing.

### An example: a Chennai club marathoner

Consider an illustrative athlete with a 25:00 5K preparing for a January marathon in Chennai. Chart 2 suggests Aerobic runs around 6:34–7:30/km, Threshold around 5:15/km and marathon pace around 5:41/km. Because the Foundation and Aerobic phases fall in the monsoon and early winter, most sessions are run early in the morning and by heart rate. The heat block is unnecessary for a cool January start but essential if the athlete is racing a warm event. The half-marathon test in week 18 is used to re-anchor paces for the final phase.

## 88. The Athlete Development Roadmap

"I truly learned about competitive running only after joining the army," Gulveer Singh has said. He grew up in a farming family in Aligarh district, Uttar Pradesh, and joined the Army through the sports quota in 2018. Seven years later he ran 27:00.22 for 10,000 m, a national record, and in 2026 he won three Asian Games medals. Avinash Sable, from a farming family in Beed district, Maharashtra, joined the Army after Class 12 and took up running through Army cross country in 2015. An Army coach moved him to the steeplechase in 2017, and in Paris in 2024 he became the first Indian to reach the Olympic final in the event. Both stories unfold in stages: finding the sport, training consistently, receiving structured coaching, then racing internationally with specialist support. The order of those stages mattered as much as the speed at which they were climbed.

A world-class endurance athlete is built over many years. The AKURA development roadmap describes five stages, from a school or beginner runner to a performance athlete. Each stage has its own training priorities and, just as importantly, its own level of measurement. Measuring too much too early can be as harmful as measuring too little too late.

FIG: f32_development_roadmap.png | The AKURA athlete development roadmap: five stages in which training load and measurement depth grow together.

|Stage|Typical athlete|Training priority|Measurement level|
|1 Foundation|School runner or adult beginner|Enjoyment, running skill, all-round athleticism|Race times, simple logs, wellbeing|
|2 Aerobic development|Regular club runner|Consistent aerobic volume, strength basics|Pace-HR, sleep, RPE, 5K tests|
|3 Structured training|Competitive club or college runner|Threshold work, long runs, planned progression|AEI-Fitness and Readiness, heat logging|
|4 Performance|State or national-level athlete|Specific preparation, heat and altitude blocks|Lab tests, blood markers with clinician|
|5 High performance|International-level athlete|Individualised, multidisciplinary support|Full AEI profile with medical team|

### Moving between stages

Athletes move up when they can absorb the current stage consistently for months, not when they run one good race. Age matters too: young athletes should build broad athletic ability and enjoyment before specialising in high volume. For adults who start late, the stages still apply, but progress is limited more by tissue tolerance and life load than by physiology.

KEY: Training load and measurement should grow together. Each stage asks a different question, and the AEI adds detail only when the athlete is ready to use it.

COACH: Write each athlete's stage on their AEI card. It prevents two common mistakes: giving a beginner a laboratory test battery they do not need, and coaching a national-level athlete with nothing more than a stopwatch.

CHECK: Which stage is each of your athletes at? What single step would move each of them to the next stage?

## 89. The AEI Research Roadmap

In 1991, a team of physiologists published a study in the Japanese Journal of Physiology on 67 Bengali female athletes from different sports, tested on a cycle ergometer. The long-distance runners had the highest relative VO₂max in the group: 43.0 ml/kg/min. Four years later, Saltin and colleagues reported Kenyan senior runners averaging about 79.9 ml/kg/min at sea level. The comparison is unfair in many ways: different decades, different athletes, different equipment, different levels of competition. That is precisely the point. Published physiological data on Indian endurance runners are scarce, scattered and hard to compare with anything. In April 2025 India inaugurated its National Centre of Sports Science and Research in New Delhi. The laboratories now exist. What is still missing is a shared method: consistent data, reliable tests, and models that are checked against reality before anyone is asked to trust them.

The AKURA Endurance Index is presented in this book as Prototype Edition 0.1. The research roadmap sets out how it can become a validated tool. Each phase has a clear output and a clear test, and the index should not be used for high-stakes decisions until the later phases are complete.

### What will count as success

The AEI will be judged by the tests set out in Chapter 68. Does it predict outcomes better than race performance alone? Does it help coaches make better decisions, reduce avoidable overload and identify useful interventions? If a domain adds nothing, it should be removed. If the index works in Chennai but not in Delhi or Kodaikanal, the model must be revised.

KEY: AEI becomes a validated index only by passing each research phase in order. Until then, report the domains separately, with their confidence.

COACH: Coaches can contribute from day one. Every standardised test, logged session, environmental note and reasoned override on an AEI card becomes part of the evidence base that will validate or correct the index.
