PART XII — DATA SCIENCE

PARTINTRO: An index is only as honest as the mathematics underneath it. Part XII moves from physiology to the data science that turns measurements into an AKURA Endurance Index™ score. It explains why weights must be learned rather than guessed, how variables measured in different units can be placed on a common scale, how missing data should be handled without inventing certainty, why every score needs a confidence layer, and how AEI must be validated before anyone relies on it. The theme throughout is restraint: a research system earns trust by showing what it does not know as clearly as what it does.

## 64. AEI Scoring: Do Not Guess the Weights

The most dangerous part of building an athlete score is not the physiology or the software. It is the moment someone assigns attractive numbers to unvalidated concepts. A formula that gives 20% to VO₂max, 15% to running economy and 10% to heart rate variability looks scientific on a slide. It has decimal places, it adds to 100 and it can be coded in an afternoon. Yet unless those percentages were derived from data, they are arbitrary opinions dressed as measurement. This chapter explains why AEI v0.1 begins life as a data schema and research prototype, and how its weights should eventually be learned or justified.

### Why guessed weights are a problem

A weight is a claim about how much a variable matters for an outcome. If VO₂max carries twice the weight of economy, the index is asserting that a unit change in one is twice as important as a unit change in the other. That assertion may be roughly true for some athletes, distances and conditions, and badly wrong for others. A marathoner with a moderate VO₂max and excellent economy, as in Case Study B, would be systematically under-rated by a formula that over-weights aerobic ceiling.

Guessed weights also hide collinearity. VO₂max, lactate threshold and recent race performance are strongly related. Adding all three with separate weights can quietly count the same underlying fitness several times, inflating the apparent precision of the score. Finally, guessed weights cannot be falsified. If nobody tested them, nobody can say whether a different set would have predicted outcomes better.

KEY: A weight in an index is a scientific claim about importance. If it has not been estimated from data or justified from strong evidence, it should be labelled as a provisional assumption, not presented as a finding.

### Start with a schema, not a formula

The honest first version of AEI is a structured way of collecting and storing variables: performance results, physiological tests, training history, environment, recovery signals and context. Each field has a defined unit, a measurement method, a timestamp and a quality flag. This schema allows data to accumulate consistently across athletes and seasons. Only once enough well-described data exist can weights be estimated with any confidence.

During the prototype phase AEI can still display components separately, such as an aerobic capacity profile, an economy profile and a durability profile, without collapsing them into a single number whose weighting is invented. Coaches often find a transparent profile more useful than a composite score anyway.

### Candidate modelling methods

Several statistical families can learn weights from data. Each answers a slightly different question and carries its own risks. The choice should follow the research question and the size and structure of the dataset.

|Method|Typical use in AEI|Strength|Main risk|
|Linear regression|Predict race time from test variables|Transparent, interpretable coefficients|Overfits with many correlated inputs|
|Regularised regression (ridge, lasso, elastic net)|Select and shrink many candidate predictors|Handles collinearity; reduces overfitting|Shrunk coefficients harder to explain; tuning needed|
|Mixed-effects models|Repeated measures within athletes over time|Separates between-athlete and within-athlete effects|Needs careful specification and adequate repeats|
|Survival analysis|Time to injury or illness|Uses censored data; models risk over time|Outcome definitions and competing risks complicate it|
|Machine learning (e.g. tree ensembles)|Non-linear patterns in large datasets|Captures interactions automatically|Opaque; overfits small samples without strict testing|

Regression methods and regularisation, discussed in standard texts on prediction modelling, are usually the right starting point for a modest cohort because their behaviour is well understood. Mixed-effects models suit athlete monitoring particularly well, because each athlete contributes many observations and individuals differ in their baselines. Survival analysis is the natural home for injury outcomes, where some athletes are followed without ever being injured and must still contribute information. Machine learning can find patterns that simpler models miss, but only with strict out-of-sample testing on data the model never saw during training.

COACH: If a platform shows you a single fitness score, ask two questions: where did the weights come from, and on what data were they tested? If the answer is "expert judgement" alone, treat the number as a conversation starter, not a verdict. Look at the underlying components before changing training.

### Out-of-sample testing is non-negotiable

Any model will fit the data it was trained on. The real test is how it performs on new athletes, new seasons or new events. Cross-validation, temporal hold-out sets and, ideally, a completely separate external cohort are the tools that separate a genuine signal from a pattern the model memorised. In sports science, where samples are often small, this discipline matters even more. A model that explains 80% of variance in 40 athletes and 20% in the next 40 has taught us mainly about the first group.

Weights can also be justified without full estimation, for example by using published effect sizes or by fixing weights in advance and then testing whether the resulting index predicts outcomes. The critical requirement is that the justification is written down, the test is prospective and the result is reported whether or not it flatters the index.

CHECK: Which variables in your current athlete dashboards are combined into a single score? Can you say how each weight was chosen, and has anyone tested whether a different weighting would predict performance or injury better?

## 65. Normalisation

VO₂max is measured in millilitres per kilogram per minute, running economy in millilitres per kilogram per kilometre, HRV in milliseconds, sleep in hours and subjective fatigue on a rating scale. These variables use different units and ranges. Adding them directly would let whichever variable has the largest numbers dominate the result. A normalisation layer is therefore required before any variables are combined. The question is not whether to normalise but which method fits the research question.

### The main options

|Method|Formula in words|Best suited to|Caution|
|Z-score|(value minus group mean) divided by group standard deviation|Comparing athletes within a well-described cohort|Sensitive to outliers; assumes a meaningful reference group|
|Percentile rank|Percentage of the reference group scoring at or below the value|Communicating position to athletes and coaches|Loses information about distance between athletes|
|Robust scaling|(value minus median) divided by interquartile range|Skewed data or data with outliers|Less familiar; still needs a reference group|
|Athlete-baseline deviation|(today's value minus athlete's rolling baseline) divided by athlete's own typical variation|Readiness and day-to-day monitoring|Needs enough history to establish a stable baseline|

Each method answers a different question. A z-score says how unusual an athlete is relative to a group. A percentile says where the athlete sits in a ranking. Robust scaling does the same as a z-score but resists distortion by a few extreme values, such as a sensor glitch that reports a resting heart rate of 20. Athlete-baseline deviation asks something entirely different: is this athlete unusual relative to themselves?

### Population comparison versus self-comparison

For readiness, deviation from the athlete's own baseline is often more useful than comparison with a population. Resting heart rate and HRV vary widely between healthy individuals, so a value that is low for one athlete may be normal for another. Plews and colleagues, writing on HRV monitoring in endurance athletes in Sports Medicine in 2013, argued for rolling averages and individual reference ranges rather than single-day values judged against group norms. The same logic applies to sleep duration, subjective wellness and morning body mass.

For performance, age- and sex-specific reference data may be relevant, provided the dataset supports it. A 5 km time can reasonably be expressed relative to athletes of the same sex and age band. But the reference group must be appropriate. Norms drawn from a different climate, altitude, competitive level or measurement protocol may mislead more than they inform.

KEY: Normalise to the question. Compare athletes with a relevant population when asking about capacity; compare the athlete with their own history when asking about readiness.

### Practical details that matter

The reference period for a baseline must be defined. A seven-day rolling average responds quickly but is noisy; a 28-day or 60-day window is steadier but slower to reflect genuine change. Many monitoring systems show both. The typical variation used in the denominator should also be the athlete's own, often expressed as a standard deviation or coefficient of variation across the baseline window, so that a change is judged against normal day-to-day noise.

Direction matters as well. For some variables higher is better (VO₂max), for others lower is better (resting heart rate), and for some the relationship is U-shaped (body mass for a given athlete). The normalisation layer should record direction explicitly so that combined scores do not accidentally reward a harmful change.

Finally, normalisation should be versioned. If the reference cohort grows or the baseline window changes, historical scores will shift. Storing the method and reference set alongside each score keeps the record reproducible.

COACH: For daily decisions, look at how far today's values sit from the athlete's own recent normal, not how they compare with teammates. Keep at least three to four weeks of morning data before trusting a baseline, and be cautious during travel, illness or major training changes when the baseline itself is moving.

### A worked example

Consider an athlete whose morning resting heart rate over the past 28 days has averaged 52 beats per minute with a typical day-to-day variation of about 2 beats. Today it reads 57. Against a population, 57 is unremarkable and a z-score would barely register. Against the athlete's own baseline, it is 2.5 typical variations above normal, a change worth noticing, particularly if sleep was short or the athlete reports feeling unwell. Now consider the same athlete's 10 km time. Here the population comparison is useful: it tells the coach where the athlete stands relative to others of the same sex and age, which helps set realistic race goals. The two variables sit in the same system but are normalised differently because they answer different questions.

Mixing methods inside one composite score is possible, but it must be deliberate and documented. A composite built partly from percentiles and partly from baseline deviations will behave in ways that are hard to interpret unless each component's scale is shown separately alongside the total.

CHECK: For each variable you monitor, do you know whether it is being compared with a population or with the athlete's own history? Is that the right comparison for the decision you are making?

## 66. Missing Data

Real athletes do not produce perfect datasets. Watches fail mid-run, sleep data disappear after a firmware update, blood tests happen only a few times a year and athletes forget to enter RPE after a hard session. Any system that works with real training data must decide what to do with the gaps. The wrong answer, and the most common one in consumer products, is to fill gaps silently with "normal" values. The AKURA principle is simple: missingness must be stored explicitly, and confidence should fall when critical variables are absent.

### Why missing data are not neutral

A missing value is itself information. If an athlete stops logging wellness scores during a period of heavy fatigue or personal stress, the gap is related to the very thing the system is trying to detect. Replacing it with the athlete's average would make a difficult week look ordinary. Statisticians describe three broad mechanisms, formalised by Rubin and developed in Little and Rubin's Statistical Analysis with Missing Data.

|Type|Meaning|Running example|Implication|
|MCAR (missing completely at random)|Missingness unrelated to any data, observed or not|Watch battery dies on a random day|Complete-case analysis unbiased but loses power|
|MAR (missing at random)|Missingness explained by other observed variables|HR strap not worn on easy days, which are logged as easy|Can be handled with models or imputation using observed data|
|MNAR (missing not at random)|Missingness depends on the unobserved value itself|Athlete skips wellness entry when feeling worst|Bias likely; needs sensitivity analysis and explicit flags|

In practice these categories overlap and cannot always be distinguished from the data alone. The safe assumption for readiness data is that some missingness is informative.

KEY: Missing is not the same as normal. A system that converts blanks into averages manufactures reassurance exactly when it may be least justified.

### How AKURA should handle gaps

First, every record should distinguish between a value of zero, a value that was not measured and a value that was measured but rejected for quality reasons. These are three different states and should never share a code.

Second, the index should know which variables are critical for each output. A readiness assessment without any recovery signal is fundamentally weaker than one missing only a secondary variable. When critical inputs are absent, the confidence tier drops (see Chapter 67) or the output is withheld.

Third, where statistical imputation is used for research, it should be principled. Multiple imputation, which creates several plausible completed datasets and combines the results, reflects uncertainty in a way that single-value filling does not. It works best under MAR assumptions and should be accompanied by sensitivity analyses that ask how conclusions would change if data were MNAR.

Fourth, the pattern of missingness should itself be monitored. A sudden drop in logging compliance can be an early behavioural signal worth a conversation, regardless of what the numbers would have said.

### No data, no false precision

The useful rule is: no data, no false precision. If an athlete has not completed a threshold test for eight months, the system should not report their threshold pace to the nearest second as though it were measured last week. Older values can be carried forward, but they should be labelled with their age and should contribute less confidence than fresh data. Occasional measurements such as blood markers should be displayed with their collection date, not blended silently into a daily score.

COACH: Make data entry easy and explain why it matters, but never punish an athlete for gaps. When logging stops, ask how the athlete is rather than assuming nothing has changed. A two-minute conversation often recovers more information than an imputation model.

### Designing for fewer gaps

Good system design reduces missingness before statistics are needed. Short, mobile-friendly wellness questions completed in under a minute are more likely to be answered than long forms. Automatic syncing reduces manual entry. Reminders timed to the athlete's routine, such as after the morning run rather than at a fixed clock time, improve compliance. Some data are naturally intermittent: a blood panel every three to six months or a laboratory test twice a season. These should be planned as scheduled measurements with defined validity windows rather than treated as irregular gaps.

It also helps to decide in advance the minimum dataset required for each output. For example, a readiness assessment might require at least one objective recovery signal and one subjective report from the past 24 hours. If that minimum is not met, the system says so plainly rather than producing a number. Writing these rules down before analysis prevents quiet, after-the-fact decisions about which gaps are acceptable.

## 67. Confidence and Uncertainty

Every AEI output should carry a confidence layer. Two athletes can receive the same score for very different reasons. One score may rest on a race completed last month, complete heart rate data from dozens of sessions and repeated laboratory tests. Another may rest on a race from two years ago, a handful of watch-estimated values and no recovery information at all. Displaying both numbers in the same bold font, with no indication of how much evidence stands behind them, misleads the coach and the athlete. This chapter describes how AKURA should communicate uncertainty honestly.

### Sources of uncertainty

Uncertainty in an athlete score comes from several places. Measurement error arises because every device and test has limited precision; a laboratory VO₂max test is more precise than a wrist-based estimate, but neither is exact. Biological variation means that even a perfect instrument would record different values on different days because the athlete genuinely changes with sleep, hydration, heat and time of day. Data age matters because fitness drifts, so a value measured months ago describes a slightly different athlete. Model uncertainty exists because the weights and structure of the index are themselves estimates (Chapter 64), and completeness matters because missing inputs reduce what can be known (Chapter 66).

Hopkins and colleagues, in their 2009 progressive statistics guidance in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise, encouraged sports scientists to report uncertainty with confidence limits and to interpret changes against the smallest worthwhile effect rather than relying on bare point estimates or p-values alone. The same attitude belongs in an athlete-facing interface.

### Confidence tiers

The interface does not need to show statistical intervals to every user. It can show "high confidence", "moderate confidence" or "limited confidence" without pretending that the underlying physiology is known exactly. The criteria for each tier should be explicit and consistent.

|Tier|Recency|Completeness|Measurement quality|Typical use|
|High|Key tests or races within about 8 weeks|All critical variables present; long monitoring history|Validated tests; clean sensor data|Planning training zones and race targets|
|Moderate|Key data 2–6 months old|Most critical variables present; some gaps|Mix of lab and field or device estimates|General guidance with periodic review|
|Limited|Older data or first weeks on the system|Critical variables missing|Mainly device estimates or self-report|Orientation only; prioritise retesting|

The thresholds in this table are illustrative starting points for the prototype, not validated cut-offs. They should be refined as the dataset grows and as reliability studies show how quickly each variable loses relevance.

KEY: A score without a confidence label invites over-interpretation. The confidence layer tells the coach how hard to lean on the number.

### Showing uncertainty without confusing people

Several design choices help. A score can be shown with a range rather than a single point, for example "AEI-Fitness 71 (likely range 67–75)". Changes can be classified as "likely real", "possibly real" or "within normal variation" relative to the athlete's typical error. The age of each input can be displayed next to it. And the system can recommend the single most useful next measurement to raise confidence, such as a time trial or a submaximal heart rate test.

The limited-confidence tier should never be hidden or treated as a failure. For a new athlete it is simply the honest starting point. As data accumulate, the tier should rise, and the athlete should see why.

COACH: Treat a limited-confidence score as a prompt to gather data, not as a basis for major decisions. When confidence is moderate, make conservative adjustments and re-check within a few weeks. Reserve precise pace targets for high-confidence profiles.

### Research system versus motivational score

This confidence layer is one of the major differences between a research system and a motivational score. Motivational scores are designed to engage: they change often, reward activity and rarely admit ignorance. A research system is designed to be correct over time. It will sometimes say "we do not know yet", and that admission is part of its value. Athletes are generally more comfortable with honest uncertainty than product designers assume, particularly when the system also explains how to reduce it.

### Confidence as a guide to action

The confidence tier should change what the system recommends, not only how the score looks. At high confidence, the system can suggest precise training paces and race targets. At moderate confidence, it can suggest ranges and recommend a check-in test within a defined period. At limited confidence, it should focus on data collection: a standardised submaximal run, a recent race or a few weeks of consistent morning monitoring. In this way, uncertainty becomes a practical plan rather than a disclaimer. Over a season, a coach should see confidence rise as the athlete's record fills in, and should notice quickly if it falls because of injury, device changes or lapses in logging.

## 68. Validation Plan

An index is not validated because it looks sensible, because experienced coaches like it or because it correlates with race times. AEI should be validated prospectively, with a plan written before the data are collected. The plan should start with a cohort large enough to estimate reliability, then test whether the index predicts outcomes beyond simple race performance. This chapter sets out the types of evidence required and the hardest question AEI must answer.

### The validation ladder

Validation is a sequence of questions, each building on the one before. If an index is not reliable, it cannot be valid; if it is not predictive, calibration hardly matters; if it works only in the development cohort, it is not yet ready for general use.

|Validation type|Question asked|Example AEI test|
|Test–retest reliability|Does the score repeat under stable conditions?|Two assessments a week apart with no training change|
|Convergent validity|Does it agree with established measures of the same construct?|AEI-Fitness versus laboratory VO₂max and threshold|
|Predictive validity|Does it forecast future outcomes?|Baseline AEI predicts race time or injury over the next season|
|Calibration|Do predicted values match observed values across the range?|Predicted 10 km times versus actual times in each score band|
|Subgroup analysis|Does it perform similarly across groups?|Women and men, age bands, climates, training levels|
|External validation|Does it work in a new, independent cohort?|Different clubs, cities or countries using the same protocol|

Reliability studies typically report the typical error of measurement and intraclass correlation, following approaches described by Hopkins in Sports Medicine in 2000 and by Atkinson and Nevill in 1998. Knowing the typical error allows every later change in AEI to be judged against measurement noise.

Calibration deserves special attention because it is often ignored. A model can rank athletes correctly while systematically over-predicting performance for everyone. Good discrimination with poor calibration can still mislead individual decisions, so both must be reported.

KEY: The easy question is whether AEI correlates with performance. The hard question is whether its extra layers improve decisions or outcomes beyond what coaches already use.

### The incremental-value question

AEI almost certainly will correlate with running performance if performance is one of its inputs. That correlation proves little. The research question that justifies AKURA is incremental: do the physiological, environmental and readiness layers add predictive or practical value beyond a recent race time and a simple training log?

This can be tested in several ways. Nested models can compare a baseline model containing only race performance with an extended model adding AEI components, reporting the change in prediction error on held-out data. A stronger design compares outcomes in groups coached with and without AEI-guided decisions, ideally with randomisation where ethically and practically feasible. Outcomes should include not just performance but also injury, illness, training availability and athlete-reported experience.

COACH: Be willing to find out that part of the index does not help. If a component adds complexity without improving decisions, removing it is a scientific success, not a failure. Share validation results with your athletes; it builds trust.

### Designing a credible study

A credible validation plan pre-registers its hypotheses, primary outcomes and analysis methods. It defines inclusion criteria, the minimum follow-up period and the handling of missing data. It reports results using established guidance for prediction models, such as the TRIPOD statement. It publishes negative as well as positive findings. And it separates the people who build the model from the data used to test it wherever possible.

For AKURA in India, a realistic path is a staged one: a reliability pilot with a few dozen athletes, a development cohort spanning multiple cities and climates, and then external validation with partner clubs and institutions. Each stage should be reported openly so that coaches can judge the strength of the evidence for themselves.

### Common pitfalls

Several traps recur in the validation of athlete indices. Circularity occurs when the outcome being predicted is also an input, so the index appears to predict performance simply because it contains performance. Leakage occurs when information from the test period accidentally enters model development, for example when normalisation uses statistics from the whole dataset including future data. Small samples produce unstable estimates that shrink dramatically in new cohorts. Selective reporting, where only favourable outcomes are published, distorts the evidence base. And survivorship bias arises when athletes who drop out through injury or loss of motivation disappear from the analysis, leaving a healthier and more successful group that flatters the index. A written protocol, independent analysis and transparent reporting are the main defences.

## 69. The Athlete Is the Laboratory

Wearables, blood tests and laboratory equipment are tools. The athlete remains the system being studied. This sounds obvious, yet much of modern endurance technology quietly reverses the relationship: the device becomes the authority and the athlete becomes a source of data for it. The AKURA model starts from the opposite position. Every measurement exists to understand one specific person better, and every interpretation must return to that person's experience, context and goals.

QUOTE: The athlete’s body is the laboratory; data is the language. — Kura

### Measure, train, recover, retest, interpret

The best AKURA workflow is iterative. The coach measures the athlete's current state, prescribes training, allows recovery, retests under comparable conditions and interprets what changed. Then the cycle begins again. No single test or season provides the full picture; understanding builds through repetition.

Each step has its own discipline. Measurement must be standardised so that a later retest is comparable: same time of day, similar weather where possible, similar preceding training load, same protocol. Training must be recorded accurately enough that its effect can be linked to the result. Recovery must be long enough that the retest reflects adaptation rather than residual fatigue. Interpretation must consider measurement error before declaring an improvement or a decline.

KEY: One athlete measured carefully over many cycles can teach a coach more about that athlete than any population average. The workflow is a loop, not a line.

### Individual response and the n-of-1 mindset

Group studies describe average responses. Individual athletes vary around those averages, sometimes widely. Hecksteden and colleagues, writing in the Journal of Applied Physiology in 2015, discussed how apparent individual differences in training response must be separated from measurement error and day-to-day variation before they are interpreted as true differences. For a coach, the lesson is twofold: individual responses are real and worth understanding, but a single before-and-after comparison is rarely enough to establish them.

Treating the athlete as a laboratory means adopting an n-of-1 mindset. Changes are introduced deliberately, preferably one at a time. Outcomes are measured repeatedly. Alternative explanations, such as a hotter test day, a new pair of shoes or a stressful work week, are recorded and considered. Over months, the coach builds a personal evidence base for that athlete: how they respond to heat, to volume increases, to altitude, to intensity blocks.

### Know why each measurement exists

The coach should know why each measurement exists. Every test, device reading and questionnaire should answer a question that could change a decision. If nobody can say what action would follow from a particular result, the measurement may be adding burden without adding value.

A simple audit helps. For each variable collected, write down the question it answers, the decision it could influence and how often it genuinely needs to be measured. Many athletes are over-measured in some areas, such as daily GPS pace data, and under-measured in others, such as periodic standardised submaximal tests or honest subjective wellness reports.

COACH: Before adding any new test or device, write one sentence: "If this result is X, I will do Y." If you cannot complete the sentence, postpone the measurement. Schedule retests at fixed points in the training cycle so that comparisons are fair.

### The athlete as a partner

The laboratory metaphor has a limit. Unlike a laboratory specimen, the athlete observes, interprets and chooses. Subjective reports of fatigue, mood and soreness have been shown in systematic review evidence to respond to training load at least as sensitively as many objective markers. The athlete's own understanding of their body is part of the data. A good AKURA workflow explains results to athletes in plain language and invites their interpretation, so that data becomes a shared language rather than a verdict delivered from outside.

### Standardising the laboratory

A laboratory is useful only when conditions are controlled. For the athlete-as-laboratory model, this means agreeing a small set of standard conditions for retests: the same course or treadmill, a similar time of day, a similar warm-up, comparable footwear, and a note of temperature and humidity. In India, where seasonal heat and humidity can change dramatically between months, recording environmental conditions is essential; a slower retest in May may reflect weather rather than lost fitness. Where perfect standardisation is impossible, the next best step is to record the differences so that they can be considered when interpreting the result.

## 70. AEI + AISRi

AEI and a daily readiness system should answer different questions. AEI asks: "What is the athlete's current capacity and profile?" AISRi asks: "Given today's recovery and risk signals, what training should be expressed today?" Keeping these questions separate is one of the central design decisions in the AKURA model. It prevents the common mistake of using one number for both fitness and readiness.

### Two questions, two timescales

Capacity changes slowly. Aerobic fitness, economy and durability build over weeks and months and decline over similar timescales. Readiness changes quickly. Sleep, illness, travel, heat, emotional stress and the previous day's training can alter readiness within hours. A single score trying to represent both will either be too slow to catch a poor night's sleep or too jumpy to reflect genuine fitness.

|AEI (capacity)|AISRi (readiness)|
|What can this athlete do?|What should this athlete do today?|
|Changes over weeks to months|Changes day to day|
|Built from races, tests, training history|Built from sleep, HR, HRV, wellness, recent load|
|Compared with population and own history|Compared mainly with the athlete's own baseline|
|Guides zones, targets and season planning|Guides session modification and risk management|
|Updated after retests and races|Updated every morning or before key sessions|
|Error: over-rating a detrained athlete|Error: ignoring a warning sign on a key day|

### Why one number fails

A strong athlete can be poorly recovered. A lower-fitness athlete can be very ready for today's easy run. If both situations are compressed into one number, the coach loses the information needed to respond correctly. Case Study E in Part XI illustrated the first pattern: high AEI-Fitness with elevated resting heart rate, poor sleep and high fatigue. The right response there was a modified session, not a reduction in the athlete's assessed capacity.

The reverse error is equally common. A beginner with high readiness should not be given a session designed for a far fitter athlete simply because they feel fresh. Readiness tells the coach how much of the athlete's capacity can safely be expressed today; it does not raise the ceiling.

KEY: Capacity is the size of the engine; readiness is how much of it can be used today. Measure them separately, then combine them in the decision, not in the score.

### How the two layers interact

In practice the coach uses both. AEI sets the framework: training zones, weekly structure, realistic race targets. AISRi modulates the daily expression of that framework: proceed as planned, reduce volume, reduce intensity, swap sessions or rest. Over time, patterns in AISRi can inform AEI. Repeated poor readiness after certain types of session may indicate that the athlete's durability or recovery capacity is lower than their test results suggest, which in turn may justify revising the profile.

The two layers must also share a confidence logic. A readiness flag based on a single missing night of HRV data should carry less weight than one based on converging signals from sleep, resting heart rate and subjective wellness. Monitoring literature, including consensus work on training load by Bourdon and colleagues, emphasises combining internal and external load measures rather than relying on any single marker.

COACH: Plan the week from capacity; adjust each day from readiness. When readiness is low on a key session, move the session rather than abandoning it. When readiness is high but capacity is modest, enjoy the good day without inflating the workload.

### A simple decision matrix

Combining the two layers in the decision rather than the score leads naturally to a four-way matrix. High capacity with high readiness supports the planned key session, performed as prescribed. High capacity with low readiness calls for a modified or rescheduled session while leaving the long-term profile unchanged. Modest capacity with high readiness supports the planned session at the athlete's own level, perhaps with slightly more confidence to complete it fully. Modest capacity with low readiness suggests easy running, rest or recovery work, and a conversation about sleep, stress and health. The matrix is deliberately simple. Its purpose is to make the coach's reasoning explicit and recordable, so that the closed-loop system described in the next chapter can learn from it.

CHECK: Think of the last time you changed a session because an athlete seemed tired. Were you responding to a change in capacity or in readiness, and did your records reflect that distinction?

## 71. The Closed-Loop Coaching System

A training plan written in January and followed unchanged until the race is an open-loop system. It sends instructions but does not listen to what happens. Most real coaching is already partly closed-loop: coaches watch, adjust and learn. The AKURA model makes that loop explicit and structured, so that every stage produces data that informs the next. This is the foundation for a digital coaching platform that behaves more like a longitudinal research system than a static plan generator.

### The ten steps

The complete AKURA loop has ten stages. Each one has a defined purpose, defined inputs and defined outputs.

1) Athlete intake: collect history, goals, injuries, medical screening, lifestyle, occupation, climate and access to facilities.

2) Baseline performance and physiology: record recent races and standardised field or laboratory tests to establish the starting point.

3) AEI profile: combine baseline data into a capacity profile with a stated confidence tier.

4) Training prescription: set zones, weekly structure and progression based on the profile and the athlete's goals.

5) Workout execution: the athlete completes sessions, with pace, heart rate and other data captured as available.

6) Environmental and recovery capture: record temperature, humidity, altitude, sleep, wellness and other recovery signals.

7) Readiness assessment: AISRi compares today's signals with the athlete's baseline to judge what training can be expressed.

8) Adaptation decision: the coach proceeds, modifies, moves or replaces sessions, recording the reason.

9) Retest: repeat standardised tests or races at planned intervals under comparable conditions.

10) AEI update: revise the profile and its confidence using new data, then return to prescription.

KEY: The loop is only closed if each decision and its reason are recorded. Without that record, the system cannot learn which adjustments worked.

### Two loops running at different speeds

The ten steps contain two nested loops. The fast loop runs daily: workout execution, environmental and recovery capture, readiness assessment and adaptation decision (steps 5 to 8). The slow loop runs across weeks and months: prescription, retest and AEI update (steps 4, 9 and 10). Intake and baseline testing (steps 1 and 2) are repeated less often, typically at the start of a season, after injury or when goals change.

Separating the loops helps avoid two failure modes. The first is over-reacting: changing the entire programme because of a single poor morning reading. The second is under-reacting: ignoring several weeks of consistent warning signs because the plan says to continue. The fast loop handles day-to-day variation; the slow loop handles genuine changes in capacity.

### Recording decisions, not just data

Most training platforms record what the athlete did. Fewer record what the coach intended and why the plan changed. For a closed-loop system to learn, step 8 must be documented: the planned session, the actual session, the readiness signals at the time and the reason for any change. Over months, these records allow the coach, and eventually the research team, to ask whether modifications based on readiness improved outcomes compared with sticking to the plan.

This record also protects the athlete. If a pattern of repeated modifications appears around certain sessions, the coach can identify a mismatch between prescription and capacity before it becomes an injury or a period of prolonged fatigue.

COACH: Add one field to your training log: "planned versus done, and why". Review it monthly. Patterns in that single field often reveal more about an athlete's needs than any new device.

### Keeping the human in the loop

The closed loop is not a case for automation replacing coaches. Algorithms can flag signals, estimate confidence and suggest options, but the adaptation decision involves context that data rarely captures fully: exams, family responsibilities, motivation, a niggle the athlete mentioned in passing. The AKURA model treats software as a structured assistant to the coach and athlete, not as the decision-maker.

### An example cycle

Imagine a club marathoner beginning a 16-week block. Intake records a desk job, a two-year training history and a previous calf strain. Baseline testing includes a 5 km time trial and a submaximal heart rate test on a flat loop. The AEI profile shows good aerobic capacity but limited durability, with moderate confidence. Prescription emphasises gradual long-run progression and strength work. Over the following weeks, daily readiness occasionally flags poor sleep during a busy work period, and two long runs are shortened. At week eight, a retest shows improved submaximal heart rate at the same pace. The AEI update raises durability slightly and confidence to high, and prescription adjusts for the second half of the block. Every step is recorded, so the next cycle begins from evidence rather than memory.

## 72. Garmin, Strava and Real-World Data

A modern endurance system can combine structured workouts with GPS, heart rate, elevation, pace, cadence and training history. Consumer platforms such as Garmin Connect and Strava, along with many coaching platforms that connect to them, make it possible to collect this information automatically from thousands of sessions. For AKURA, integrations are valuable because they reduce manual entry and increase the amount of real-world data available. But integration is not validation. A clean API feed can still contain poor sensor data.

### The promise and limits of automatic data

Automatic data collection solves one of the biggest problems in athlete monitoring: incomplete manual logs. Every run is captured with a timestamp, a route and a set of physiological signals. For an Indian research system hoping to follow many athletes across diverse climates and terrains, this is transformative.

The limits are equally real. Devices differ in sensor quality, sampling rate and processing algorithms. Firmware updates can change how a variable is calculated without notice. Estimated values, such as device-derived VO₂max or recovery time, are produced by proprietary models whose validity varies between individuals and conditions. And every sensor has characteristic failure modes that produce plausible-looking but wrong numbers.

KEY: A data feed that arrives cleanly formatted is not therefore accurate. Data quality rules must sit between the device and the index.

### Data-quality rules

The table below lists common artefacts in wearable running data and how AKURA should handle them. The rules apply regardless of device brand; all manufacturers face similar physical limitations.

|Artefact|What happens|Detection rule|Handling|
|GPS drift|Position errors inflate distance or create pace spikes, especially near tall buildings, tree cover or tracks|Implausible pace or acceleration jumps; track laps not matching 400 m|Smooth or flag segment; prefer footpod or known distance|
|Optical HR cadence lock|Wrist sensor locks onto step rate instead of heartbeat|HR suddenly matches cadence for a sustained period|Exclude segment from HR analysis; recommend chest strap|
|Missing HR|Sensor dropout or strap not worn|Gaps or zero values in HR stream|Store as missing, not zero; lower session confidence|
|Auto-pause artefacts|Device pauses at traffic lights or slow uphill sections|Moving time far below elapsed time; gaps in stream|Use moving and elapsed time appropriately; flag intervals|
|Elevation noise|Barometric or GPS elevation drifts with weather or signal|Implausible total climb for known flat route|Correct with terrain data or flag|
|Treadmill mis-calibration|Wrist-estimated distance differs from belt|Large mismatch with treadmill display|Use manual entry where available; flag pace data|
|Device or firmware change|Calculated metrics shift without training change|Step change in a metric at a known update date|Mark discontinuity; recalculate baseline|

Heart rate data from wrist-based optical sensors deserve particular care during intervals, in cold conditions, with darker skin tones or tattoos and with loosely worn straps. These are known technical limitations of optical sensing, and they are a reason to validate data quality rather than a reason to exclude any athlete group.

### Building a quality layer

A practical quality layer does three things. It checks each incoming session against physical plausibility rules, such as maximum realistic pace and heart rate ranges for that athlete. It assigns a quality flag to each data stream rather than a single pass or fail for the whole session. And it feeds those flags into the confidence layer described in Chapter 67, so that a score built from flagged data is labelled accordingly.

Where possible, the quality layer should also learn the athlete. A heart rate of 205 may be implausible for one athlete and routine for another. Athlete-specific plausibility ranges, built from clean historical data, reduce false alarms without letting genuine errors through.

COACH: Use a chest strap for sessions where heart rate matters, such as threshold tests and intervals. Run key time trials on a measured course or track rather than trusting GPS distance. When a number surprises you, look at the raw graph before changing the plan.

### Estimated metrics and privacy

Many devices report derived values such as estimated VO₂max, training status, recovery time or race predictions. These estimates can be helpful for engagement and trend-watching, but they are not direct measurements. AKURA should store them as device estimates, clearly separated from laboratory or field-test values, and should not use them as primary inputs to AEI unless their agreement with measured values has been checked for the athlete concerned.

Integrations also carry responsibilities. Athletes should know which data are imported, how they are stored, who can see them and how to withdraw consent. Location data in particular can reveal home addresses and routines, so research datasets should remove or coarsen GPS traces where they are not needed for the analysis.

CHECK: Look at the last three sessions of an athlete's data. Can you spot any of the artefacts in the table? How would each have affected a score if left uncorrected?

## 73. The Indian Athlete Profile

Indian endurance athletes are not one physiological population. A distance runner training in the humid heat of Chennai, a trail athlete living at altitude in Ladakh, a masters marathoner in Bengaluru who trains before an office commute, and a young athlete in a sports hostel with limited access to laboratory testing all face different conditions and respond to training in their own ways. Climate, altitude, age, sex, nutrition, occupation, training history and access to facilities vary widely. Any system that treats "the Indian athlete" as a single type risks replacing one set of borrowed assumptions with another.

### Why local longitudinal data matter

Much of the published endurance science, and many of the reference values built into consumer devices, come from populations studied in different climates, with different training cultures and different diets. Some of those findings transfer well; others may not. The honest answer is often that we do not yet know.

The value of an Indian research system is precisely that it can collect local longitudinal data instead of importing assumptions from another population. Repeated measurements of the same athletes across seasons, including pre-monsoon heat, monsoon humidity and cooler winter months, can show how environment and training interact in real Indian conditions. This is information no imported norm can provide.

KEY: Diversity within India is larger than any single average can capture. The goal is to describe variation carefully, not to create a new stereotype.

### Profile variables

An AKURA athlete profile should record the context that shapes physiology and training, so that analyses can account for it rather than assume it away.

|Domain|Variables|Why it matters|
|Demographics|Age, sex, body mass, stature|Reference ranges and training response vary|
|Environment|Home climate, typical training temperature and humidity, altitude of residence|Heat and altitude affect pace, HR and recovery|
|Training history|Years of training, weekly volume, event focus, coaching access|Explains current capacity and adaptation rate|
|Lifestyle and occupation|Work hours, commute, shift work, sleep pattern|Affects recovery and available training time|
|Nutrition|Dietary pattern (e.g. vegetarian), energy availability, iron and vitamin D status where tested|Influences adaptation, health and injury risk|
|Health|Injury history, menstrual health where relevant, illness, medications|Informs safe prescription and risk flags|
|Facilities and data access|Track, trails, gym, laboratory access, device type|Determines which tests are feasible and data quality|

Recording these variables does not mean every athlete must provide all of them. Sensitive items should be optional, collected with informed consent and stored securely. The aim is to allow analyses to adjust for context and to ensure that conclusions are not driven by hidden differences in circumstances.

### Avoiding stereotypes

Research on populations must never slide into assumptions about individuals. Observed differences between groups often reflect environment, opportunity, nutrition and training history far more than fixed biological traits. The AKURA dataset should be analysed with this in mind: variation within any group will usually be larger than differences between groups, and every individual athlete should be assessed on their own data.

This is also why subgroup analysis in validation (Chapter 68) matters. If AEI performs well for well-resourced urban runners but poorly for athletes from rural or less-equipped settings, the index must be improved before it is used to make decisions about those athletes.

COACH: Record where and when your athletes train, not just what they did. A slow tempo run on a hot, humid afternoon may reflect excellent effort. Compare each athlete with their own history in similar conditions before drawing conclusions.

### An evidence base for India, open to the world

AKURA's long-term dataset should become an evidence base for Indian endurance coaching while remaining open to international comparison. Using common definitions, standard protocols and transparent reporting allows Indian findings to be compared with those from other countries, strengthening both. Over time, such a dataset could help Indian coaches move from borrowed averages to locally grounded, individually interpreted evidence.
