PART XIV — RESEARCH PROTOCOLS

PARTINTRO: Ideas become knowledge only when they are tested in a way that another coach could repeat. This part converts the questions raised throughout the book into four practical pilot protocols: a heat-acclimation study suited to Chennai's climate, an altitude study built around Ooty and Kodaikanal, a marathon durability protocol, and a crossover design comparing heat, altitude and control blocks in the same athletes. Each chapter is written as a mini study protocol with aims, hypotheses, participants, design, measurements, outcomes, safety rules, analysis and ethics. None of these designs is final. ## STORY: Three Soldiers and One Second

It is August 2016, and three men in Indian vests are waiting on an Olympic start line in Rio de Janeiro. All three are soldiers of the Indian Army. None of them is famous at home. Ahead of them lie 42.195 kilometres of city streets, and behind them lie three of the most different childhoods Indian distance running could produce: a hill farm in Kerala, the sand country of western Rajasthan, and the mountain roads of Uttarakhand.

The first is Thonakal Gopi. He comes from Sulthan Bathery in Wayanad, a town that sits about 1,000 metres above sea level among forest, spice gardens and paddy. His parents were farmers who grew rice and ginger. He joined the Army through the sports quota and rose to Havildar in the Artillery regiment, and the Army lists him at the Army Sports Institute in Pune. He did not enlist to become an Olympian. Like many Indian distance runners, he found that the Army offered something rare: a steady job that allowed him to run.

The second is Kheta Ram. He is reported to have grown up in Barmer district, in the desert of western Rajasthan, and to have run four kilometres to school. He serves in the Jat Regiment. Think of a Barmer summer afternoon, the horizon shimmering, the sand too hot to stand on barefoot, and then think of a boy running to class across it every morning.

The third is Nitendra Singh Rawat, reported to come from Garur in Bageshwar district, Uttarakhand, where roads are cut into hillsides and every run is either up or down. In January 2016, at the Mumbai Marathon, Rawat ran 2:15:48. That time broke Ram Singh Yadav's Indian course record of 2:16:59 and booked his place in Rio. According to the reporting, all three of the Rio marathoners qualified at that same Mumbai race.

### The same mountain

What makes the three men interesting to a scientist is not only how different they were. It is how much they had in common. They served in the same Army. They trained under the same coach, Surendra Singh Bhandari of the Army Sports Institute (his first name appears as Surinder in some reports). And before Rio they shared the same mountain.

ESPN described Gopi's preparation as a "ten-month training stint at a high-altitude Ooty facility". Ooty sits at 2,240 metres in the Nilgiris, high enough for mist to roll across the tea gardens in the early morning and for a sea-level runner to feel the air go thin on the first hill out of town. Bhandari's altitude block there covered all three marathoners. Rawat later credited it directly.

QUOTE: I was personally confident after the training we had been put through in the national camp at Ooty by our coach (Surinder Singh) Bhandari. — Nitendra Singh Rawat (Sportskeeda/IANS, 2016)

Picture that camp as any coach who has taken a group to the hills will recognise it: cold mornings, steep roads, runners in tracksuits steaming in the mist, long days of eating and sleeping and running again. Three men, one coach, one altitude, one goal.

### The race

Then Rio. Gopi finished 25th in 2:15:25, a personal best. Kheta Ram finished 26th in 2:15:26, also a personal best, one second behind his team-mate. Read that again. After more than two hours of running, after months at altitude, two men from a Kerala hill farm and a Rajasthan desert crossed the line one second apart.

Rawat's day went differently. He finished 84th in 2:22:52. The reporting afterwards explained why: he had run with a hamstring injury. Seven months after setting an Indian course record in Mumbai, he was seven minutes slower on the biggest stage of his life.

To place these times in history: in 2016 the Indian men's marathon record still belonged to Shivnath Singh, who had run 2:12:00 at Jalandhar in 1978. It would stand for about 48 years, until Sawan Barwal ran 2:11:58 in Rotterdam in April 2026. The Rio trio were chasing ghosts from before they were born.

### What happened next

The three careers kept moving. Gopi won the 2017 Asian Marathon Championship in Dongguan in 2:15:48, the first Indian to take that title. In January 2018 he won the Indian elite race at the Mumbai Marathon in 2:16:51. Second, in 2:16:54, was Nitendra Singh Rawat, who had recovered after Rio and trained with the national camp in Bangalore. In 2019 Rawat ran 2:15:52 in Mumbai, four seconds outside his own course record, and in 2022 he won the national marathon title in 2:16:04. Gopi kept going longer still: at the Valencia Marathon in December 2025 he ran 2:12:23, a personal best just 23 seconds outside the old national record, after four more months of training at Ooty.

### The experiment nobody wrote down

Now look at Rio the way a research scientist would. Three trained athletes, all from the same organisation. One coach. One altitude camp of the same length, as far as the reports show. One qualifying race. One Olympic course, on one day, in one set of conditions. That is about as close to a natural experiment as elite sport ever gets. Two athletes responded almost identically. The third was compromised by an injury.

What did the camp actually do to them? Did their haemoglobin mass rise, and by how much? At what altitude did they sleep, and at what altitude did they run their hard sessions? How many kilometres a week did each man run in the final ten weeks? Did heart rate at a fixed pace fall during the camp, and did it fall equally for all three? When did Rawat's hamstring first complain, and what was his training load in the fortnight before? How long after coming down from Ooty did each man race best?

The public record answers none of these questions. The data may exist in a coach's notebook or an Army file. But a search for this book found no peer-reviewed study of Indian athletes' blood responses at Ooty at all. Generations of India's best runners have gone up the ghat road, and almost nothing about what their bodies did up there has been published in a form another coach could read, check and use.

This is not a criticism of the coach or the athletes. They did their jobs superbly: two personal bests at an Olympic Games is a real achievement. It is a description of a system in which experience accumulates in people rather than in records. When a coach retires, the knowledge often retires with him.

### What a protocol would have caught

A simple research protocol, built with an ethics committee and a doctor, would not have changed the race. It would have changed what came after. It would have recorded a baseline before the camp: blood measures, a submaximal run at a fixed pace, a time trial. It would have logged the dose: sleeping altitude, training altitude, days at camp. It would have tracked load, sleep and resting heart rate every day. It would have logged every niggle, including the first one in a hamstring. And it would have fixed the retest dates in advance, so that nobody could pick the best result after the fact.

With three athletes you cannot prove anything about altitude. You can describe how each individual responded, and that description would have been worth a great deal. When Gopi returned to Ooty for four months before Valencia in 2025, a coach could have compared his response nine years later with his response before Rio, instead of relying on memory. Afterwards Gopi said simply: "I think the high altitude at Ooty helped my performance." He may be right. A protocol would tell us how, how much, and whether the same is true for the next soldier who goes up the mountain.

The four chapters that follow are written so that the next camp, the next heat block and the next marathon produce knowledge as well as results. We begin where every Indian runner begins, whether they choose to or not: with the heat.

## 74. Heat Study Protocol

It is 5:10 a.m. on Marina Beach, and the air already feels like a wet towel. A club runner jogs past the fishing boats pulled up on the sand, her shirt dark with sweat before the first kilometre is done. By the time the sun clears the Bay of Bengal, the heart rate on her watch has drifted well above where it sat in December, at exactly the same pace. Her coach shrugs: Chennai makes you tough, everybody knows that. Perhaps it does. But nobody in the group can say by how much, how quickly, or whether the runner who trains at 5 a.m. adapts differently from the one who trains after work in an air-conditioned gym. The research gathered for this book found no peer-reviewed heat-stress study of an Indian marathon at all. The heat in Chennai is free and arrives every morning. The knowledge about what it does to Indian runners does not.

Chennai offers something many high-performance centres pay to recreate: sustained heat and humidity for most of the year. That natural environment is both an opportunity and a risk. If AKURA wants to know whether a structured heat-acclimation block changes an athlete's physiology and performance, it must stop relying on the general impression that "training in Chennai already makes us heat-adapted" and instead run a controlled, documented, repeatable study. This chapter outlines a pilot design. It is deliberately modest: the goal is to generate clean data from a small group, learn what is practical, and prepare for a larger study.

### Aim and hypotheses

The aim is to quantify the physiological and performance response of trained endurance runners to a structured heat-acclimation block, and to describe how much that response varies between individuals. Three working hypotheses guide the design. First, heart rate at a fixed submaximal pace in standardised warm conditions will fall after the block. Second, thermal comfort and perceived exertion at that pace will improve. Third, the size of the response will differ meaningfully between athletes, and part of that difference may be explained by prior heat exposure. The study does not assume that every athlete will improve, and a null or mixed result is an acceptable outcome.

### Participants, inclusion and exclusion

Recruit a small pilot group (for example 12 to 20 runners) with stable training for at least several weeks, no current injury, and a recent race or time-trial result that anchors their performance level. Inclusion: adult athletes, regular training of a defined minimum weekly volume, willingness to complete all visits, and medical clearance. Exclusion: history of exertional heat illness without specialist clearance, known cardiovascular, renal or metabolic conditions, current febrile or gastrointestinal illness, medications that impair thermoregulation, and pregnancy. Record sex, age, training history, home climate and typical training time of day, because a runner who trains at 5 a.m. on the Marina and one who trains in an air-conditioned gym arrive with different heat histories.

### Design and timeline

|Week|Phase|Main activity|
|1|Familiarisation|Consent, medical screening, practise test protocol|
|2|Baseline|Standardised submaximal run and time trial in logged conditions|
|3–4|Heat block|Controlled heat sessions (e.g. 8–10 sessions) with normal training|
|5|Short recovery|Reduced load, no extra heat sessions|
|6|Post-test|Repeat baseline tests under matched conditions|
|8–9|Decay check (optional)|Repeat submaximal test to observe retention|

A control or crossover condition strengthens causal inference. In the simplest version, a matched control group continues normal training without the extra heat sessions. In a stronger version, each athlete completes both a heat block and a control block in randomised order with a washout between them. Heat sessions can be outdoor runs in logged conditions, indoor sessions in a heated room, or post-exercise passive heat exposure, but the method must be fixed in advance and applied consistently.

### Measurements and outcomes

Before every test record temperature, relative humidity and, ideally, wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), along with time of day, wind, surface and footwear. Primary outcomes can include heart rate at a fixed pace, core-temperature response where it can be measured safely and with appropriate equipment, body-mass change as a sweat-loss estimate, thermal comfort, and performance in a standardised time trial. Secondary outcomes can include plasma-volume estimates from haemoglobin and haematocrit, sweat rate, sleep and resting heart rate, and blood markers where appropriate and clinically supervised.

KEY: A heat study is only as good as its environmental log. If baseline and post-tests are run in different heat and humidity, the change in heart rate may simply reflect the weather, not the athlete.

### Safety and stop rules

Each heat session requires a supervisor trained in recognising heat illness, access to shade, cooling methods and fluids, and a clear emergency plan with the nearest hospital identified. Stop a session immediately if the athlete shows confusion, unusual behaviour, loss of coordination, stops sweating or develops hot, dry skin, collapses, vomits, or reports a severe headache or dizziness; begin cooling and seek emergency medical care. Predefine upper limits for environmental conditions and, where core temperature is monitored, a termination threshold set by the medical lead. Athletes who are unwell, underslept or dehydrated on arrival do not complete the session that day.

### Analysis and ethics

Report individual responses alongside group means, with confidence intervals, and compare each change against the test's typical error and a predefined smallest worthwhile change. Adjust for environmental differences where possible and state clearly what could not be controlled. The protocol must receive approval from an institutional ethics committee before recruitment, every participant must give written informed consent after a plain-language explanation of risks, and a qualified physician must provide medical oversight throughout. Participants may withdraw at any time without consequence.

### Practical notes for Chennai

In Chennai the difficulty is often not creating heat but controlling it. Outdoor conditions can change sharply between a 6 a.m. and an 8 a.m. start, and sea breeze, cloud cover and pre-monsoon humidity can make two apparently similar mornings very different. For that reason the baseline and post-tests are best run indoors on a treadmill in a room whose temperature and humidity can be held steady, or outdoors only when WBGT falls within a pre-agreed band. Keep the same treadmill, footwear, time of day and pre-test meal. Ask athletes to avoid hard training for 48 hours before each test and to record their sleep for the preceding two nights. Small details like these decide whether the final data can be trusted.

COACH: Even without a formal study, you can borrow this structure. Test a fixed-pace run in logged conditions, add a planned heat block, then retest in similar conditions. Keep the notes; a season of carefully recorded mini-experiments is worth more than a lifetime of impressions.

CHECK: Can you name the exact conditions of your athlete's last baseline test? If you repeated it today, how would you know whether a change came from adaptation or from the weather?

## 75. Altitude Study Protocol

Morning mist hangs over the Nilgiris, and the road out of Ooty climbs through tea gardens into cold, thin air. Indian distance runners have been coming here for generations. Before the Rio Olympics, Army marathoner T. Gopi spent what ESPN described as a ten-month training stint at a high-altitude Ooty facility. Before the 2025 Valencia Marathon he returned for four months, and afterwards said, "I think the high altitude at Ooty helped my performance." Sawan Barwal's group spent about four months at Ooty and Wellington before he broke the national marathon record in 2026. In February 2024 Tamil Nadu opened a dedicated high-altitude training centre at Breeks Ground. The mountain is busy. Yet a search for peer-reviewed studies of Indian athletes' blood responses at Ooty comes back empty. Thousands of runners have climbed the ghat road, and almost nothing about what their bodies did up there has been written down in a form another coach could check and use.

The Nilgiris and the Palani hills give South Indian athletes access to moderate altitude within a day's travel of Chennai. Ooty sits at about 2,240 metres and Kodaikanal at a little over 2,100 metres, which places both in the range often used for altitude training camps. Many Indian coaches already send athletes there. What is usually missing is measurement: a clear record of dose, a baseline, a controlled training load and a proper retest. This protocol treats an altitude camp as an experiment rather than a pilgrimage.

### Aim and hypotheses

The primary question is the size and timing of each athlete's response, not whether altitude "works". The study should not begin with the assumption that performance must improve. Working hypotheses: haemoglobin mass, where it can be measured with a validated method, will show a small average increase after sufficient exposure; individual responses will vary widely; and changes in blood measures will not map perfectly onto changes in race performance. If haemoglobin mass changes but race performance does not, that is still a scientifically meaningful result.

### Participants, inclusion and exclusion

Recruit trained endurance athletes who can commit to the full camp and follow-up. Inclusion: stable training, medical clearance, and baseline iron status reviewed by a clinician. Exclusion: iron deficiency that has not been treated, anaemia, known cardiopulmonary disease, previous severe altitude illness, sickle-cell trait or disease unless cleared by a specialist, recent illness, and pregnancy. Record prior altitude exposure, home altitude and any iron supplementation already in use.

### Design and timeline

|Week|Phase|Main activity|
|1–2|Baseline (Chennai)|Blood measures, ferritin, performance test, submaximal test|
|3|Pre-camp|Iron review with clinician; set sleeping and training altitude|
|4–6|Camp (Ooty or Kodaikanal)|Controlled training load; daily sleep, SpO₂, RHR, wellness|
|7|Return|Monitor first-week responses at sea level|
|8–9|Post-test|Repeat blood measures and performance tests|
|10–12|Follow-up|Observe timing of best performance and decay|

Define sleeping and training altitude precisely, because "we went to Ooty" can mean very different exposures depending on where the athlete slept and where the sessions took place. Log hours per day at altitude. Control training load so that any change is not simply a result of a heavier or lighter training block. A sea-level control group training in Chennai under a similar programme, where feasible, makes interpretation much stronger.

### Measurements and outcomes

Primary outcome: change in haemoglobin mass by an appropriate specialist method, or, where that is unavailable, carefully standardised haemoglobin concentration and haematocrit interpreted with caution because concentration is affected by plasma volume. Secondary outcomes: time-trial or race performance, heart rate at fixed pace, SpO₂ trend during the camp, sleep quality, resting heart rate and heart-rate variability trend, body mass, ferritin, and athlete-reported wellness. Every blood draw should follow a standard posture, time of day and hydration protocol.

KEY: Haemoglobin concentration and haemoglobin mass are not the same thing. A concentration can rise simply because plasma volume fell. Only a total-mass method tells you whether the athlete actually built more red-cell capacity.

### Safety and stop rules

Altitude exposure should be medically supervised when indicated. Brief all athletes on warning signs of altitude illness: worsening headache, nausea or vomiting, unusual breathlessness at rest, a persistent cough, confusion, loss of coordination or extreme fatigue. Any of these requires the athlete to stop training, be assessed by a clinician, and descend if symptoms are serious or worsening. Reduce training intensity during the first days of the camp. Monitor iron status and sleep, and stop the study for any athlete whose symptoms or blood results raise clinical concern.

### Analysis and ethics

Report individual response trajectories as well as means, with confidence intervals, and compare changes against the measurement's typical error. Pre-register the timing of retests so that the "best" post-camp result is not selected after the fact. The protocol requires institutional ethics committee approval, written informed consent, and a named physician responsible for blood testing, iron decisions and altitude illness. Blood abnormalities must be interpreted by qualified clinicians, not by the coach or the algorithm.

### Practical notes for Ooty and Kodaikanal

Hill-station camps bring their own complications. Road travel from Chennai is long, and the first days are often disturbed by travel fatigue, cold nights and changed food. Terrain in both towns is hilly, so paces are not directly comparable with flat roads in the plains; use heart rate and RPE to guide intensity and record elevation gain for every session. Tourist seasons can affect accommodation, noise and sleep. Plan retesting at sea level in Chennai under the same conditions as baseline, and schedule it in advance rather than choosing the day the athlete "feels good". Finally, remember that nutrition, particularly energy and iron intake, must be adequate at altitude; an athlete who under-eats at camp may show no haematological response for reasons that have nothing to do with altitude itself.

COACH: Before the next camp to Ooty or Kodaikanal, write down five things: sleeping altitude, training altitude, days at camp, planned training load and the exact retest dates. If you cannot write them down, you are not yet running an experiment.

CHECK: What result would convince you that a particular athlete does not benefit much from altitude? Are you prepared to accept that result if the data show it?

## 76. Marathon Durability Protocol

Mumbai, January 2018. After 42.195 kilometres of city roads, T. Gopi wins the Indian elite men's race in 2:16:51. Nitendra Singh Rawat, who had set the Indian course record two years earlier, is second in 2:16:54. After more than two hours of running, the two soldiers are separated by three seconds on the clock. Two years before that, at the Rio Olympics, Gopi and his Army team-mate Kheta Ram had finished 25th and 26th, one second apart. Somewhere in the closing stages of each race, bodies that looked evenly matched at the start began to separate by tiny amounts. Some athletes hold their pace as fatigue piles up; others slowly unravel, and nothing in a fresh laboratory test warned that they would. That quality, the ability to keep running well when already tired, rarely appears on a test report. It decides marathons.

Two athletes can share the same VO₂max, the same threshold pace and the same half-marathon time and still finish a marathon ten minutes apart. The difference often lies in durability: how well physiology and performance hold up as duration and fatigue accumulate. Durability has become an important topic in endurance science because traditional laboratory tests are usually performed on fresh athletes. This chapter proposes a practical protocol to measure how pace, heart rate and perceived effort relationships change with duration in real training.

### Aim and hypotheses

The aim is to develop and test a repeatable method for describing durability in marathon runners. Hypotheses: first, the relationship between pace and heart rate will drift in a measurable way after a certain duration; second, the pattern of drift will differ between athletes; third, that drift will relate to how much an athlete slows in the final part of a marathon. A durability index can be built from the rate of deterioration after a defined duration, but the formula should be validated before being used as a score.

### Participants, inclusion and exclusion

Recruit marathon runners with at least one completed marathon and a planned race within the study window. Inclusion: stable training, capacity to complete long runs of at least 90 to 150 minutes, medical clearance, and a heart-rate device that records reliably. Exclusion: current injury, recent illness, or any condition making prolonged exercise in heat unsafe. In Chennai, schedule long runs early in the morning and log conditions carefully, because heat is the most common confounder of any durability measure in Indian cities.

### Design and timeline

|Week|Phase|Main activity|
|1|Baseline|Fresh submaximal test at fixed pace; time trial|
|2–3|Standard long run 1|Fixed pace, fixed fuelling, full data capture|
|6–7|Standard long run 2|Same route, time and protocol|
|10–11|Standard long run 3|Repeat to measure reliability and trend|
|Race|Marathon|Race data with splits, fuelling and conditions|
|Race + 2|Review|Compare long-run drift with race fade|

Use standardised long runs or controlled race segments. The same route, start time, target pace, footwear and fuelling plan should be used each time. A useful structure is a steady long run with short fixed-pace "probe" segments at the start and near the end, so that heart rate and RPE at identical pace can be compared fresh and fatigued.

### Measurements and outcomes

Record pace, heart rate, RPE at set intervals, fuelling (grams of carbohydrate and timing), fluid intake, body mass before and after, temperature, humidity and WBGT, and a brief post-run note on muscle soreness, gut comfort and cramps. Primary outcome: change in heart rate and RPE at the fixed probe pace between early and late segments. Secondary outcomes: pace decline in the race's final 10 kilometres, cardiac drift, and athlete-reported limiters.

KEY: The most useful result may be identifying whether the athlete loses performance through metabolic, thermal, pacing or musculoskeletal pathways. Each pathway calls for a different training response.

### Interpreting the pathways

A metabolic pattern may show progressive heart-rate and RPE rise with poor fuelling or low glycogen availability. A thermal pattern may show sharper drift on hotter days, larger body-mass loss and improvement when conditions are cooler. A pacing pattern may show a fast early section followed by inevitable decline. A musculoskeletal pattern may show relatively stable heart rate but rising soreness, form breakdown and pace loss. These are working categories, not diagnoses, and many athletes show a mix.

### Safety, analysis and ethics

Stop a long run if heat-illness warning signs appear, if the athlete reports chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness, or if an injury changes running mechanics. Carry fluids and have a support vehicle or check-in points on longer routes. Analyse within-athlete changes against typical error, and check reliability across the three long runs before calculating any index. As with every AKURA protocol, obtain institutional ethics committee approval, written informed consent, and medical oversight.

### Building and validating a durability index

A simple prototype index might express the percentage change in heart rate at the probe pace between the early and late segments, adjusted for temperature. Before such a number appears on any dashboard it must pass three tests. First, reliability: does it give similar values when the same athlete repeats the same long run in similar conditions? Second, sensitivity: does it change when durability is expected to change, for example after a block of specific long-run training? Third, validity: does it relate to what matters, such as the degree of slowing in the final 10 kilometres of a marathon? Only when all three questions have acceptable answers should the index be reported as a score rather than a research variable.

COACH: Add two short fixed-pace segments to your athlete's next long run, one early and one late. Record heart rate and RPE for both. Over a season, that simple habit will teach you more about marathon fade than any single laboratory test.

CHECK: When your athlete slowed in their last marathon, which pathway was most likely responsible? What data would you need to be sure?

## 77. Heat Versus Altitude Crossover

Chennai Central, late evening. A coach stands on the platform with a list of eight runners and a budget that will cover one block of specialised training before the season's target race. Option one: put the squad on the overnight train towards the Nilgiris and spend three weeks at Ooty, 2,240 metres above the sea. Option two: stay at home, where the heat and humidity of a Chennai summer arrive every morning without a ticket. India's map of choices is growing. Hindustan Times reported in 2026 that SAI's campuses at Ooty and Shilaroo are the country's only two high-altitude training centres, with a new centre planned for Shillong and a Khelo India centre approved at Leh. Heat, meanwhile, is available almost everywhere. What nobody can give that coach is a direct comparison: for these particular eight athletes, which block would do more, and for which goal?

Throughout this book we have treated heat and altitude as separate stimuli with some overlapping effects. Both can influence plasma volume, heart-rate responses and perceived effort; altitude may also increase haemoglobin mass over sufficient exposure, while heat acclimation primarily improves thermoregulatory and cardiovascular responses to heat. For an Indian coach who can choose between Chennai's heat and the hills of Ooty or Kodaikanal, the practical question is obvious: which block, for which athlete, for which goal? A crossover study is the most informative way to ask it.

### Aim and hypotheses

The aim is to compare heat, altitude and control blocks in the same athletes, so that each athlete acts as their own comparison. Hypotheses: heat blocks will produce larger changes in thermal response and heart rate in the heat; altitude blocks will produce larger changes in haemoglobin mass; both may produce changes in submaximal heart rate; and individual response patterns will differ. Outcomes should distinguish plasma volume, Hb mass, submaximal heart rate, thermal response, performance and recovery. This allows the research team to ask which adaptation overlaps and which is unique.

### Participants, inclusion and exclusion

Recruit trained athletes able to commit for a long study period, ideally six months or more. Inclusion and exclusion criteria combine those of Chapters 74 and 75: medical clearance, clinician-reviewed iron status, no history of serious heat or altitude illness without specialist clearance, no relevant cardiovascular or respiratory conditions, and no current injury. Because the study is long, plan for drop-outs and seasonal race schedules.

### Design and timeline

|Period|Block|Notes|
|Weeks 1–2|Baseline testing|Blood, heat-response test, performance test|
|Weeks 3–6|Block A (randomised)|Heat, altitude or control|
|Weeks 7–12|Washout|Normal training; retest at end|
|Weeks 13–16|Block B (randomised)|Second condition|
|Weeks 17–22|Washout|Normal training; retest at end|
|Weeks 23–26|Block C (randomised)|Third condition, then final tests|

Randomisation and washout are desirable where practical. Randomising the order protects against season, fitness trend and race calendar being mistaken for block effects. The washout must be long enough for the main adaptations to return towards baseline; haemoglobin mass changes, in particular, persist for weeks. Where a full three-way crossover is impractical, a two-condition version (heat versus control, or altitude versus control) is a sensible first step.

### Measurements and outcomes

Use identical tests at every time point: haemoglobin mass by a specialist method where available, plasma-volume estimates, heart rate at fixed pace in both temperate and warm conditions, core or skin temperature where safely measured, sweat rate, time-trial performance, resting heart rate, heart-rate variability trend and sleep. Log training load throughout so that each block is matched in volume and intensity as closely as possible.

KEY: The value of a crossover is not only in the average result. It is in discovering which athletes respond more to heat, which to altitude, and which to neither.

### Safety and stop rules

The study must be designed around safety and qualified medical oversight, particularly for hypoxic exposure and blood testing. All stop rules from the heat and altitude protocols apply. Any athlete showing heat-illness or altitude-illness warning signs stops immediately and is assessed; any abnormal blood result is referred to a clinician. Limit the total number of blood draws to what is needed and explain each one in the consent form.

### Analysis and ethics

Use mixed-effects models or similar methods appropriate for repeated measures, report individual responses with confidence intervals, and account for order and carry-over effects. Be explicit that a small pilot crossover will generate hypotheses rather than definitive answers. Institutional ethics committee approval, informed consent and a named medical lead are mandatory, and participants should receive their own results in a clear, plain-language summary.

### Why the comparison matters for India

Travel to hill stations costs money, time and time away from work or study, while heat is available at home for much of the year in Chennai and many other Indian cities. If a well-designed crossover showed that, for some athletes and some goals, a controlled heat block produced useful changes at a fraction of the cost, that would matter greatly for Indian endurance development. Equally, if altitude produced unique benefits that heat could not replicate for particular athletes, that would justify investment in camps. The study is not designed to declare a winner. It is designed to help coaches match the right stimulus to the right athlete at the right time, with evidence rather than habit.

COACH: If you cannot run a crossover, you can still think like one. Record each athlete's response to every heat block and every camp over the years. Across a career, those records form a personal crossover study.

CHECK: For your squad, do you currently know which athletes respond best to heat and which to altitude? If not, what would be the first block you could measure properly?

## STORY: The Girl Who Ran With the Recruits

On the hill roads of Pauri Garhwal, the mornings belong to young men. You see them in any Uttarakhand hill district: groups of teenagers in cheap trainers running up switchbacks above terraced fields, training for the physical tests that stand between them and a job in the Army. They run hard, because the test is hard and the job matters.

In Maroda, a village at about 1,400 metres, one of a farmer's four children watched them and made a decision. Her name was Ankita Dhyani, and she told ESPN what happened next: "I decided to run with them ... They never slowed down for me."

Nobody slowed down, so she sped up. The route from that hill road to an Olympic Games ran through a sports hostel in Rudraprayag in 2017, then to Bhopal. In late 2018 she joined the Elite Distance Running Programme there, coached by the Dutch former marathoner Hugo van den Broek, whose group travelled to Iten in Kenya once a year. From 2019 she was at the National Centre of Excellence in Bhopal under coach Toppo, who pointed out to ESPN that the hills had already done some of the work: "because she was running in Uttarakhand, she had already been doing a lot of high-altitude training."

The results followed, not in a straight line but in a series of steps. She ran the 1500 m and 5000 m at the 2021 World U20 Championships in Nairobi. She ran the heats of both the 5000 m and the steeplechase at the Paris Olympics in 2024. In Jerusalem in August 2025 she set a national record of 6:13.92 for the 2000 m steeplechase. A month later, at the World Championships in Tokyo, she ran 10:03.22 in her heat. Then, on 27 September 2026 in Nagoya, she ran 9:19.24 for fourth place at the Asian Games, a personal best, in the same race in which Parul Chaudhary took bronze with a national record of 9:08.67.

### Reta, Chamba

Further west along the Himalaya, in Chamba district of Himachal Pradesh, another girl was running barefoot. Seema was born in Reta village in 2001, the youngest of six children. Her father died when she was twelve. She grew up herding cattle and running barefoot.

A SAI hostel gave her structure. She won junior national gold at Ranchi in 2015. Nine years later, at the Asian Cross Country Championships in Hong Kong in 2024, she won gold in 37:20, with Sanjivani Jadhav given the same time for silver. Then came a run of marks that show an athlete climbing steadily: 1:11:23 to win the Indian elite race at the Delhi Half Marathon in October 2025, 52 seconds outside L. Suriya's 2017 national record of 1:10:31; a national record of 1:26:04 for 25 km in Kolkata that December; 32:02.43 for 10,000 m in March 2026.

In Nagoya, on 24 September 2026, she won 10,000 m bronze at the Asian Games. She became only the third Indian woman to win an Asian Games medal in the event, and the first in 16 years. Afterwards she described it plainly.

### Amroha to Los Angeles

The third story begins in Amroha, Uttar Pradesh, where K. M. Deeksha was born in 1999. She competes for Madhya Pradesh and, as of 2024, had trained for about five years under coach S. K. Prasad at the Madhya Pradesh Athletics Academy in Bhopal.

On 11 May 2024, at the Sound Running Track Fest in Los Angeles, she finished third in the 1500 m in 4:04.78. It was a national record. To understand what that meant, follow the record back. Sunita Rani ran 4:06.03 at the 2002 Asian Games in Busan, and that mark stood until Harmilan Bains ran 4:05.39 in Warangal in 2021. Deeksha's run took the record under 4:05 for the first time. Nineteen years for the first improvement; three years for the second. The line of Indian middle-distance running had started to move.

### The others on the road

They are not alone. Parul Chaudhary, who started with a barefoot school race in a Meerut village, now holds national records at 3000 m, 5000 m and the steeplechase. After her double bronze in Nagoya she said: "It feels really good. Last time in Hangzhou, I also took two medals, but this time the colour has changed..." Priti Lamba, steeplechase bronze medallist at the Hangzhou Asian Games, once broke her ankle in two places at a water jump at the Patiala national camp, and still runs with a rod in it. Harmilan Bains had knee surgery in 2022 and missed both the Commonwealth Games and the World Championships.

Each of these careers is also a trail of data: race times, injuries, camps, coaches, flights, hostels. Most of it lives in scattered places, and some of it is already lost.

### What implementation means for them

Look at Ankita Dhyani's path again, this time as a data problem. A hill village. A hostel in Rudraprayag. An elite programme in Bhopal with an annual camp in Kenya. A national centre. Junior championships in Nairobi, an Olympics in Paris, a Grand Slam meet in Jerusalem, World Championships in Tokyo, Asian Games in Nagoya. At every transition a new coach, doctor or physio met an athlete whose history arrived, at best, in someone's memory and a phone gallery.

An athlete card of the kind described in Chapter 78 is a simple idea: one record that travels with the athlete, with her consent, from hostel to academy to national camp. It keeps raw measurements separate from derived scores, marks what is missing as missing, and dates everything. When a new coach meets her, the card tells them what altitude blocks she has done, how she responded, and which injuries have come and gone.

A dashboard of the kind described in Chapter 79 asks a sharper question. Between Tokyo in September 2025 (10:03.22) and Nagoya a year later (9:19.24), something changed by 44 seconds. The public record does not say what. It might have been conditions, health, training, travel or tactics; nobody outside her team can know, and nobody should guess. A good dashboard would not guess either. It would show readiness, environment, load and recent interventions side by side, so that the people responsible for her could see the pattern, and learn from it.

And safety, the subject of Chapter 81, is not abstract for this generation. A 2025 study of 104 female athletes in Tamil Nadu, from volleyball and ball badminton, found that about 53.8% were anaemic, against a background in which a large survey found anaemia in 44% of Indian adolescent girls and 41% of adult women. Those were not runners, and the numbers cannot be transferred directly. But any system that collects blood values from young Indian women athletes must put a clinician, not a coach and not an algorithm, in charge of interpreting them. In April 2026 the Athletics Integrity Unit also moved India into its highest-risk category, which means more out-of-competition testing, including blood tests, for distance runners. The athlete's body is becoming a data record whether she likes it or not. The question is whether that record is kept securely, read honestly and used for her benefit.

The girl who ran with the recruits did not need a dashboard to get started. She needed hills, stubbornness and people who did not slow down. What she and the runners who follow her deserve now is a system that remembers what worked, notices early when something is wrong, and knows when to stop and call a doctor. The next four chapters describe how to build one.

## 78. The AEI Athlete Card

Try to build an athlete card for Shivnath Singh. The headline number is easy: 2:12:00, run at Jalandhar in 1978, a national marathon record that stood for about 48 years until Sawan Barwal broke it in Rotterdam in April 2026. Then the fields start to go blank. His coach: unverified. His training base: unverified. He finished 11th in the marathon at the 1976 Montreal Olympics, but even that result comes down to us with two different times, depending on which source you read. He is reported to have run barefoot throughout his career. Of weekly mileage, heart rates, blood values or the weather on the day of the record, nothing survives in the public record. One of the greatest performances in the history of Indian running is, as data, almost empty. The athlete card exists so that the next record-breaker leaves behind more than a single number.

Every athlete in the AKURA system should have one standardised card. It is the single place a coach, scientist or clinician can go to see who the athlete is, what they have done, how they have been tested, and how confident we are in each piece of information. Without a standard card, data scatters across watches, spreadsheets, message threads and memory. With one, the athlete becomes a coherent research record that grows over a career.

### What the card contains

The card should contain demographics relevant to sport, training history, recent race results, physiological tests, environmental exposure, injury and recovery flags, equipment and data confidence. The table below shows a proposed structure. It is a starting point and will change as the system learns what coaches actually use.

|Section|Fields|
|Identity|Athlete ID, age band, sex, event focus, home city, coach|
|Training history|Years training, typical weekly volume, recent block summary|
|Performance anchor|Recent 5K/10K/HM/marathon results with date and conditions|
|Physiological tests|Threshold marker, VO₂-related measure, economy, Hb/Hb mass|
|Cardiovascular|Resting HR, HR at fixed pace, HRV trend|
|Environment|Heat exposure log, altitude exposure log, home climate|
|Durability|Standard long-run drift, marathon fade|
|Recovery flags|Sleep trend, soreness, illness, injury notes|
|Equipment|Watch, HR strap, footwear, device settings|
|Interventions|Heat blocks, camps, nutrition changes, with dates|
|Data confidence|Protocol version, missing data, quality flags|
|Clinical notes|Clinician-entered only; restricted access|

### Raw data first, scores second

The card should separate raw data from derived scores. Coaches must be able to inspect the raw measurement behind every conclusion. If the card shows an AEI Oxygen domain value, one tap should reveal the actual haemoglobin result, the date, the laboratory, the method and the protocol notes. If a durability rating appears, the coach should see the long runs from which it was calculated. This is not bureaucracy; it is the foundation of trust.

KEY: A score without traceability is difficult to trust. Every number on the AEI card must lead back to a measurement, a date and a method.

### Confidence and privacy

Each field should carry a confidence marker: high when the data are recent, standardised and from a reliable device or laboratory; lower when data are old, self-reported or collected under unknown conditions. Missing data should be shown as missing, not quietly filled in. Because the card holds sensitive health information, access should be role-based. Clinical notes and blood results should be visible only to those who need them, athletes should be able to see their own card, and consent for research use should be recorded explicitly. Indian data-protection requirements should be followed, and athletes should know how their data are stored and who can see them.

### Keeping the card alive

A card that is filled in once and forgotten is useless. Updating should be part of routine: race results added within a few days, test results on the day, environmental logs with every heat block or camp, and a quarterly review in which coach and athlete read the card together. Over time the card becomes the athlete's physiological biography, one that can show, for example, how a runner from Madurai responded to three successive Kodaikanal camps, or how heat tolerance changed across four Chennai summers.

### Using the card in practice

Imagine a coach preparing a runner for a winter marathon. Before planning, the coach opens the card and sees the most recent half-marathon anchor with its date and conditions, the heart rate at fixed pace from the last three tests, a haemoglobin result from before the previous Kodaikanal camp, the heat-session log from the summer, and a note that the athlete had a calf strain in August. Each item shows its confidence. In a few minutes the coach can see what is known, what is uncertain and what needs testing before the key block begins. That is the purpose of the card: not to decide for the coach, but to make sure the coach decides with the full picture.

A card also makes handovers safer. When an athlete moves from a school coach to a university programme, or from a club in Coimbatore to a training group in Chennai, the new coach inherits a documented history instead of starting from guesswork. With the athlete's consent, the card can travel with them.

COACH: Start with a paper or spreadsheet version if needed. Fill in only the sections you can support with real data, mark everything else as missing, and review it with the athlete at the end of each training block.

CHECK: If a colleague asked you tomorrow for the raw measurement behind your athlete's most important training decision, could you find it in less than a minute?

## 79. The AEI Dashboard

It is 5:40 a.m. in a training hostel, and the coach's phone has already buzzed thirty times. Screenshots of watch data. A voice note about a sore calf. A photo of a thermometer on a verandah. Somebody's sleep score, somebody's resting heart rate, somebody's GPS track that cuts straight through a building. Each message is a fragment of truth, and together they are almost useless, because nobody can read thirty fragments before the 6 a.m. session begins. The modern Indian coach is not short of data. Watches are cheap, apps are free, and a squad of fifteen runners can generate thousands of numbers in a single week. What the coach is short of is attention. The real skill is no longer collecting information. It is knowing, in the few minutes before the athletes arrive at the track, which three facts matter today.

A card holds everything; a dashboard shows what matters now. Coaches work under time pressure, often with many athletes and limited staff. A dashboard that tries to show every metric at once becomes noise. The AEI dashboard is designed around restraint: a small number of panels that together answer the coach's most urgent questions, with the raw card always one click away.

### Three questions

The dashboard should answer three questions quickly. What can the athlete do? How ready are they today? What changed recently? Everything else is secondary. The first question is answered by the performance anchor and fitness trend. The second is answered by readiness indicators such as sleep, resting heart rate, heart-rate variability trend and subjective wellness, which in the wider AKURA ecosystem feed the AISRi daily readiness layer. The third is answered by recent interventions, environmental exposure and training-load changes.

### Proposed panels

A useful dashboard should show current performance anchor, fitness trend, readiness trend, key physiological domains, environmental exposure, training load, durability, confidence and recent interventions.

|Panel|What it shows|Question answered|
|Performance anchor|Most recent standardised race or time trial|What can they do?|
|Fitness trend|HR at fixed pace, threshold trend over weeks|What can they do?|
|Readiness trend|Sleep, RHR, HRV trend, wellness (AISRi)|How ready today?|
|Physiological domains|AEI domain values with confidence|What can they do?|
|Environment|Recent heat and altitude exposure, WBGT|What changed?|
|Training load|Acute and chronic load, recent changes|What changed?|
|Durability|Long-run drift trend|What can they do?|
|Confidence|Data quality and missing-data flags|How sure are we?|
|Interventions|Heat blocks, camps, nutrition, equipment|What changed?|

### Design principles

Show trends rather than single values, because a single low heart-rate-variability reading means much less than a two-week decline. Display confidence alongside every value, using clear visual cues for low-quality or missing data. Separate fitness from readiness visually, so that a tired athlete is not mistaken for an unfit one. Keep colour meanings consistent and avoid alarming reds for normal day-to-day variation. Allow the coach to annotate the dashboard directly, and make sure those annotations are saved.

KEY: A dashboard is not a verdict. It is a prompt for a conversation between coach and athlete, grounded in evidence.

### Avoiding dashboard traps

Dashboards can create false precision. A number displayed to one decimal place feels more certain than it is. They can also encourage over-monitoring, where athletes become anxious about daily fluctuations. The AEI dashboard should round sensibly, show ranges where appropriate, and remind users that daily readiness signals are context-dependent. Athletes should see a simplified version focused on their own progress and practical guidance, not a wall of physiological variables.

### An example morning

At 6 a.m. a coach in Chennai opens the dashboard for a squad of fifteen runners. Most readiness panels show normal variation. One athlete's resting heart rate has been elevated for three days, sleep has fallen, and the wellness note mentions a sore throat. Another athlete's fitness trend shows heart rate at fixed pace improving steadily since a heat block ended two weeks earlier. A third has a low-confidence flag because their chest strap was not worn. Within a minute the coach knows whom to call, whose session to modify, and whose data to ignore until it is fixed. That is what a good dashboard does: it directs attention, and then it gets out of the way.

### Building gradually

A dashboard should grow with the data. A club with only race results and watch heart rate can begin with the performance anchor, fitness trend and training-load panels. Readiness and environment panels can be added once check-ins and environmental logs become routine, and physiological domains once standardised testing is in place. Adding panels before the data exist only creates empty boxes and false impressions of completeness.

Finally, the dashboard must be honest about what it does not know. A blank panel labelled "no recent data" is more useful than a confident-looking estimate built on stale measurements. Coaches quickly learn to distrust tools that look precise but prove wrong, and that distrust spreads to the whole system.

COACH: Look at your current monitoring tools and ask whether they answer the three questions within ten seconds. If they do not, remove panels until they do.

CHECK: Which single panel on your current dashboard do you look at most, and which have you never used to make a decision?

## 80. The Coach's Interpretation Layer

Look at the results of the Rio 2016 Olympic marathon and you will find Nitendra Singh Rawat in 84th place, in 2:22:52. An algorithm reading that line might conclude that he was unfit, badly paced or poorly prepared. Seven months earlier he had run 2:15:48 in Mumbai, an Indian course record that qualified him for Rio. The result sheet cannot tell you what the reporting did: that he ran in Rio with a hamstring injury. After recovering and training with the national camp in Bangalore, he ran 2:16:54 in Mumbai in 2018, and in 2019 he ran 2:15:52 there, four seconds outside his own course record. The Rio result is accurate. It is simply incomplete. Every coaching system faces this problem every day: numbers arrive without their stories, and a model that sees only numbers will sometimes be confidently wrong.

No algorithm, however carefully validated, sees the whole athlete. It sees what it has been given. A runner's heart rate may be high because of fitness loss, but also because of a late shift at work, a family crisis, a fever starting, a new medication, a hot bus journey, or a watch strap worn loosely. The coach's interpretation layer exists so that this context is never lost and never overridden silently by a number.

### Context the algorithm cannot see

No algorithm should hide context. Travel, family stress, work shifts, illness, medication, menstrual-cycle factors where relevant, equipment changes and unusual weather can alter performance. In the Indian setting, add long commutes, exam seasons for student athletes, festival periods with changed sleep and diet, monsoon disruptions and power cuts that affect sleep in hot weather. Each of these can shift the physiological signals on which AEI and AISRi depend.

### Annotation and override

The coach should be able to annotate events and override a recommendation with a reason. If the system suggests a hard session but the coach knows the athlete travelled overnight by train from Coimbatore, the coach should be able to change the plan and record why. The override is not a failure of the system. It is information. Those overrides become research data themselves, because they record exactly where the model and the expert disagreed.

KEY: An override with a reason is one of the most valuable data points a coaching system can collect. It captures human knowledge the model does not yet have.

### Learning from the coach

Over time, the system can learn which signals the coach repeatedly uses correctly. If a coach consistently reduces load when a particular athlete's sleep drops below a personal threshold, and those decisions are followed by better sessions, that pattern can inform future recommendations. Equally, if some overrides are followed by poor outcomes, the system can highlight this gently. The aim is mutual learning: the algorithm becomes more context-aware, and the coach becomes more aware of their own patterns and biases.

### Guarding against bias

Human interpretation brings its own risks. Coaches can hold assumptions about athletes based on background, gender, region or appearance. The interpretation layer should encourage evidence-based notes, not labels. Write "athlete reported poor sleep for three nights due to exams", not "this athlete is always lazy before races". Keep annotations factual, respectful and useful, remembering that athletes may be able to read them.

### Annotations in practice

A good annotation is short, specific and timed. It states what happened, when, and what was changed. For example: "Returned from wedding travel, two nights of four hours' sleep; replaced intervals with 40 minutes easy." Or: "Hot humid morning, WBGT high; moved tempo to treadmill in the evening." Standard tags such as travel, illness, work, exams, heat, equipment and menstrual-cycle note (entered only with the athlete's consent) make these notes searchable later. When the research team later analyses why a fitness trend dipped or a test result looked unusual, these annotations often provide the explanation that no sensor could.

### Athlete voice

The interpretation layer is not only for the coach. Athletes should be able to add their own notes about how sessions felt, what else was happening in their lives, and what they think explains a good or poor result. Athletes often notice patterns before the data show them. Including their voice improves the data, builds trust and helps athletes develop the self-awareness that is the long-term goal of the whole AKURA approach.

### When the coach and system disagree

Disagreement should be expected and welcomed. When the system and the coach point in different directions, the useful response is neither to obey the algorithm blindly nor to ignore it, but to ask why they differ. Perhaps the model lacks context; perhaps the coach is reacting to a single vivid memory. Recording the disagreement, the decision and the outcome turns each case into evidence. Over hundreds of such cases, patterns emerge that neither the coach nor the model could have seen alone, and that shared record becomes one of the most distinctive assets of the AKURA system.

COACH: Build the habit of a one-line note whenever you change a planned session: what you changed and why. After three months, read the notes back. You will learn a great deal about your own coaching.

CHECK: When did you last override a plan based on context the data could not see? Did you record it, and what happened afterwards?

## 81. Safety Rules

Rio de Janeiro, August 2016. O. P. Jaisha, who a year earlier had set the Indian women's marathon record of 2:34:43 in Beijing, collapses after crossing the finish line of the Olympic marathon. Afterwards she says that Indian officials gave her no refreshments along the course. Scroll quoted her: "All the [other] countries had their stalls at every two kilometres, but our country's stall was empty." The Athletics Federation of India disputed her account, and her team-mate Kavita Raut said she had had enough water. Years later, what happened that morning is still argued over. That is exactly the point. When a safety plan exists only in people's heads, nobody can check afterwards what was planned, what was provided and where the chain broke. A written plan, briefed in advance and reviewed without blame afterwards, protects athletes and officials alike.

Every measurement system eventually reaches a point where the right answer is not a number but a person. AKURA's safety rules define that point clearly. They apply to every protocol in this book, every dashboard, and every automated recommendation. They are not optional extras; they are the conditions under which the rest of the system is allowed to operate. Nothing in this book replaces the judgement of a qualified doctor.

### The core rules

1) No readiness score should override symptoms requiring medical attention. If an athlete reports chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, palpitations or signs of illness, the session stops and the athlete is assessed, whatever the dashboard says.

2) Heat training should be stopped when heat-illness warning signs appear. Every heat session needs supervision, cooling, fluids and an emergency plan.

3) Altitude exposure should be medically supervised when indicated, especially for athletes with medical conditions, low iron status or previous altitude illness.

4) Blood abnormalities should be interpreted by qualified clinicians. Coaches and algorithms may flag a result but must not diagnose or prescribe treatment.

5) No supplement, iron or medication decision should be made by the AEI system. These belong to clinicians.

6) Athletes must be able to stop any test or session at any time without penalty.

7) Any result the system cannot confidently interpret must be flagged for human review.

### Heat-illness warning signs

Stop exercise immediately, begin cooling and seek emergency medical care if an athlete shows any of the following:

- Confusion, disorientation, irritability or unusual behaviour
- Stopping sweating, or hot, dry skin
- Collapse or loss of consciousness
- Vomiting or persistent nausea
- Severe headache, dizziness or loss of coordination

Exertional heatstroke is a medical emergency. Rapid cooling and emergency care save lives. In Chennai and other hot Indian cities, every training group should know where the nearest emergency department is and how to reach it quickly.

### Altitude warning signs

Stop training and arrange clinical assessment if an athlete at Ooty, Kodaikanal or higher altitude shows:

- Worsening headache that does not settle with rest
- Nausea, vomiting or loss of appetite with fatigue
- Unusual breathlessness at rest or a persistent cough
- Confusion, unsteadiness or extreme drowsiness
- Chest tightness or coughing up frothy or blood-stained sputum

Serious or worsening symptoms require descent and urgent medical care. Moderate altitudes carry lower risk than high mountains, but individual susceptibility varies and warning signs should never be ignored.

KEY: A research system must know when not to automate. The safest algorithm is the one that can say: "This result needs human review."

### Defer to clinicians

This book discusses physiology to improve coaching decisions, not to diagnose disease. Where symptoms, blood results, heart rhythm irregularities, disordered eating, repeated injuries or menstrual disturbances appear, the coach's role is to refer to an appropriately qualified clinician. The AEI system should make referral easy and never make it feel like failure.

### Building a safety culture

Rules on paper are not enough. Safety depends on a culture in which athletes feel able to report symptoms without being seen as weak, and in which coaches reward honest reporting. In many Indian sporting environments, athletes may hide illness or discomfort to avoid losing selection or disappointing a coach. The AKURA system should make it normal, even expected, to say "I don't feel right today". Assistant coaches and senior athletes should be trained in first response, including how to cool an athlete rapidly and how to call for emergency help. After any incident or near miss, the team should review what happened and what can be improved, without blame.

### Data safety and privacy

Safety also includes the athlete's information. Blood results, menstrual-cycle notes, injury histories and mental-health comments are sensitive. They should be stored securely, shared only with those who need them, and never used against an athlete in selection or contract discussions without clear consent. Athletes should be able to see, correct and, where appropriate, withdraw their data.

COACH: Print the heat and altitude warning signs and keep them in your kit bag, at the track and on the team group. Brief every new athlete and every assistant coach before their first hot session or first camp.

CHECK: If an athlete collapsed during a hot session tomorrow, does everyone in your group know exactly what to do in the first five minutes?

PART XVI — PHILOSOPHY

PARTINTRO: The final part steps back from protocols and dashboards to ask what the whole enterprise is for. Endurance communities everywhere carry myths, and many contain a real observation wrapped in an inaccurate explanation. AKURA's task is not to mock those beliefs but to test them, keeping what the evidence supports and letting go of what it does not. These closing chapters set out the attitude behind the method: humility before data, respect for the athlete, and a cycle of training, measuring, understanding and adapting that continues across a career. They end with the real goal of AEI, which is not a famous number but athletes who understand themselves better.

## STORY: The Barefoot Record

Montreal, July 1976. The Olympic 10,000 metres heats are under way, and among the vests and spikes of the world's best distance runners is a pair of bare feet. They belong to Hari Chand, a 23-year-old policeman from Hoshiarpur district in Punjab. He runs the whole race barefoot. He finishes eighth in his heat and does not reach the final.

On the scoreboard, it looks like an ordinary early exit. In the Indian record books, it becomes something else. The time Hari Chand ran that day was a national record, and it stood for 32 years. Sources disagree on the exact figure: Wikipedia and other accounts give 28:48.72, while Olympics.com prints 29:48.72, almost certainly a typing error, since a slower time could hardly have survived as the record for three decades. Even the most famous run of his life comes down to us with a question mark attached.

### A policeman who ran

Hari Chand was born on 1 April 1953. The Times of India gives his village as Ghorewaha in Hoshiarpur district. He served with the Central Reserve Police Force, where Olympics.com describes him as an inspector, and he is reported to have retired as a Commandant. He ran the 5000 m and the 10,000 m, and later the marathon.

Imagine the Punjab of his youth as any traveller would see it: flat, green wheat country stretching to the Shivalik foothills, winter fog lying on the fields, summers that bake the canal roads. That is a picture of the place, not of the man. What we know about Hari Chand himself comes almost entirely from results.

And the results are remarkable. At the 1975 Asian Championships in Seoul, he won gold in the 10,000 m and bronze in the 5000 m, and received the Arjuna Award for them. The runner-up in that 10,000 m, according to Wikipedia, was Shivnath Singh, the Army runner who would soon set a marathon record of his own. After Montreal came 1978, the year Indian distance running seemed to catch fire. In May, Shivnath Singh ran 2:12:00 for the marathon at Jalandhar. At the Asian Games in Bangkok that year, Hari Chand won both the 5000 m and the 10,000 m. Double gold.

He went to the Moscow Olympics in 1980 and went out in the heats of the 10,000 m; Wikipedia records that he also ran the marathon there, finishing 22nd. He missed the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi because of a viral fever. Then the record books go quiet.

He died on 13 June 2022, aged 69. The Times of India's obituary headline said that he had died unsung.

### What the record books do not say

Try to reconstruct Hari Chand's training, and the pages turn blank almost immediately. Who coached him? Unverified. Where did he train? Unverified. How many kilometres did he run a week, on what surfaces, in what heat, with what food? Nothing survives in the public record. His coach and training base are among the most basic facts a sport could keep, and for one of its greatest runners, India did not keep them.

The same is true of Shivnath Singh, reported to have run barefoot throughout his career. His 2:12:00 from 1978 was the longest-standing record in Indian athletics. It lasted about 48 years, until Sawan Barwal ran 2:11:58 in Rotterdam in April 2026 and then 2:11:37 for Asian Games silver in September. Shivnath Singh's coach and training base are also unverified. Even his 11th-place time at the Montreal Olympic marathon appears in two different versions.

### The long view

Line up the Indian distance records and a strange pattern appears. The 10,000 m record set by Hari Chand in 1976 stood until Surendra Singh ran 28:02.89 in Vigo, Spain, in 2008. Gopal Saini's steeplechase record from 1981 stood for 37 years, until Avinash Sable began breaking it in 2018. Bahadur Prasad's 5000 m record from 1992 stood about 30 years. Shivnath Singh's marathon stood 48.

Then, in a rush, they fell. Gulveer Singh took the 10,000 m record to 27:41.81 in 2024 and then to 27:00.22 in California in 2025. Sable broke the steeplechase record ten times. The marathon record went twice in one year.

Why the long silence, and why the sudden rush? The honest answer is that nobody can say for certain. What the evidence does show is a pattern: almost every record since 2022 was set at an overseas meet with fast pacing, in an era of foreign coaching, altitude camps abroad and racing against the world's best. That is an observation. It is not yet an explanation, and it certainly does not prove that the athletes of the 1970s were less talented. They ran what they ran with what they had.

### How myths grow in empty space

When measurements are missing, stories rush in to fill the space. Two of the great Indian distance records of the 1970s belong to men reported to have run barefoot. It is very tempting to build a theory on that. Perhaps bare feet made them tough, and modern shoes have made runners soft. Or perhaps the opposite: imagine what they could have done in proper shoes. Both stories are satisfying. Both are repeated. Neither can be tested, because we do not know how these men trained, how their bodies responded, or what the conditions were on the days they ran.

Every endurance culture produces myths like this. They usually start with a real observation, such as a barefoot runner who was genuinely fast or a hill village that genuinely produced champions, and then attach an explanation that nobody has checked. Over time the explanation hardens into common sense. Coaches repeat it to athletes. Athletes repeat it to their own students. Fifty years later it is simply what everybody knows.

Sawan Barwal, whose Asian Games silver in 2026 was India's first men's marathon medal at the Games since 1982, described his racing to the Tribune with an answer with no myth in it at all: "There was only one thing in my mind: I have to go till the finishing line and how I can maintain the pace."

Consider the contrast with today. A club runner in Chennai with a cheap sports watch now collects more data in a single Sunday long run than survives about Hari Chand's entire career: pace for every kilometre, heart rate every second, temperature, elevation, cadence. The problem has flipped. The 1970s had champions and almost no data. The 2020s have oceans of data and, for most runners, very little understanding of what it means. Neither situation, on its own, turns observation into knowledge. That takes a method.

### Legacy and patience

Hari Chand's legacy is not only two gold medals in Bangkok and a record that outlived most of the careers that chased it. It is also a question he leaves behind: how many great Indian athletes have been reduced, in memory, to a single time and a single story, because nobody wrote down the rest?

The answer to that question is not to mock the old stories. It is to take their kernel of truth seriously enough to test it, and to make sure that the next Hari Chand leaves behind not only a record but a record of how it was made. Patience is part of this. Records that stood for 32 and 48 years remind us that progress in endurance running is measured in decades, not seasons. A research system has to think on the same scale.

That is where this final part of the book begins: with the move from myth to measurement.

## 82. From Myth to Measurement

"Kavita Raut, Monica Atre… Sanjivani. I am telling you Nashik is the Kenya of India ... Remember, Kavita had to walk so many kilometres to reach school or fetch water." The speaker is Vijender Singh, the coach who has worked in Nashik since 1991, talking to the Indian Express. It is a wonderful line, and it contains a real observation: his group, drawn from Nashik and its surrounding villages, has produced a Commonwealth Games medallist, a World Championships marathoner and Asian medallists. It also contains the seed of a myth. Is the secret the long walks to school? The terrain? The coach himself? Or simply which children stayed in the sport long enough to be noticed? The same shape of explanation has been applied to Kenya, to Ethiopia and to the hills of Uttarakhand. A coach who loves his athletes is entitled to his metaphor. A scientist has to ask what would need to be measured to know whether it is true.

Every endurance community has myths. In India we hear that runners from hill regions are naturally stronger, that Chennai's heat alone makes athletes tough, that a camp in Ooty automatically adds a minute to a marathon, that a certain diet or a certain shoe is the secret. Internationally, the myths are often about East African runners, about genes, about altitude, about poverty and hunger. Some contain a useful observation wrapped in an inaccurate explanation. This book has tried to treat every such belief with curiosity rather than contempt.

### The useful kernel

A myth usually survives because it contains something true. Athletes who live and train at moderate altitude do experience real physiological challenges. Athletes who grow up running long distances as children may accumulate substantial aerobic training. Hot climates do produce heat adaptation. The problem arises when a partial truth becomes a complete explanation, or when a population-level observation is turned into a statement about every individual. That is where stereotypes form, and stereotypes are both scientifically weak and unfair to athletes.

### Separating observation from conclusion

The job of AKURA is not to ridicule the myth. It is to separate the useful observation from the unsupported conclusion. That requires a simple discipline: state the belief as a testable hypothesis, identify what would be measured, predict what result would support or contradict it, and then collect the data. "Altitude makes runners faster" becomes "a three-week camp at a defined sleeping altitude will increase haemoglobin mass and time-trial performance in this group, with a measurable individual response". The second statement can be wrong, and that is exactly why it is useful.

MYTH: Athletes from a particular region or ethnic group are naturally built for endurance, so training matters less. || Elite endurance performance is multifactorial. Training history, environment, economy, motivation, opportunity, coaching and support systems all matter, and individual variation within any group is large. No population label predicts an individual athlete's potential.

### Belief and data

Belief is not the enemy of science. Coaches need conviction to commit to a plan, and athletes need belief to endure hard training. Belief is often where a good question starts. But belief cannot be the end point. The athlete deserves to know what is actually happening in their own body, and that can only come from careful measurement interpreted honestly.

QUOTE: நம்பிக்கையால் hypothesis உருவாகலாம். ஆனால் athlete-ன் உண்மையை data தான் சொல்ல வேண்டும். — Kura

In English, this means: "Belief can create a hypothesis. But only data can tell the athlete's truth." The line is written in the mixed Tamil and English that many Chennai coaches and athletes actually speak, and it captures the spirit of this book. Faith, tradition and intuition are welcome at the beginning of the research process. They generate the questions. The answer, however, must come from what the athlete's body shows when it is measured properly.

### Humility in both directions

Measurement also has limits. Devices have error, tests have poor reliability when not standardised, and statistics can mislead when sample sizes are small. Moving from myth to measurement does not mean replacing one certainty with another. It means holding every conclusion with an appropriate level of confidence, updating it as new evidence arrives, and being honest when the answer is "we don't know yet".

KEY: A myth becomes useful the moment it is turned into a hypothesis that could be proved wrong.

### Myths in the Indian context

Indian endurance culture has its own inherited beliefs: that drinking water during runs makes athletes weak, that pain must always be pushed through, that a particular village or state produces "natural" runners, that women should train less, or that recovery is laziness. Some of these beliefs once contained a grain of practical sense in a particular setting; others were never supported at all. Treating them as hypotheses allows coaches to keep the respect for hard work and tradition while discarding practices that harm athletes. That process should be gentle and respectful, because the people who hold these beliefs often care deeply about their athletes.

Ultimately, the move from myth to measurement is a move from arguing about who is right to asking what is true for this athlete. That shift lowers the temperature of debates between coaches, and it keeps the conversation centred on the person who matters most.

COACH: Write down three beliefs you hold about training that you have never tested. For each, write what you would measure and what result would change your mind. Choose one to test this season.

CHECK: Which training belief in your group is most widely accepted and least often questioned? What would it take to test it fairly?

## 83. Train. Measure. Understand. Adapt.

In July 2025, at the Monaco Diamond League, Avinash Sable injured the ACL and meniscus of his right knee. He had surgery. He missed the World Championships in Tokyo that year and the Glasgow Commonwealth Games in 2026. Speaking about his injuries, he offered one of the most honest sentences an elite athlete can say in public: "I think I might have rushed my return a bit, which caused the injury to recur." In September 2026 he ran his first steeplechase back, 8:32.39, at the Indian Athletics Series final. Weeks later in Nagoya he ran 8:21.67, his best of the season, to finish fourth at the Asian Games. Train, test, learn, adjust, train again. The road back was a loop rather than a straight line, and one of its most valuable lessons came from a step that went wrong.

Training without measurement can still work. Coaches have produced great athletes with a stopwatch, a notebook and years of experience. But measurement makes the learning process clearer. It shortens the time needed to discover what works for an individual, and it protects athletes from repeating mistakes that nobody noticed. Measurement without interpretation is just data. Interpretation without humility becomes dogma. AKURA's approach can be summarised in four words.

### The four words

1) **Train.** Do the work, consistently and intelligently. Training is the stimulus that drives adaptation. No amount of measurement replaces months and years of well-structured running, strength work and recovery. The training plan should be clear enough that it can be described, recorded and repeated.

2) **Measure.** Capture what happened and how the body responded, using standardised protocols. That includes performance, heart rate, perceived effort, environment, sleep and, where appropriate, laboratory tests. Measure carefully, log context, and note the confidence of each piece of data.

3) **Understand.** Interpret the measurements in the context of the athlete's life, history and goals. Ask what changed, why it might have changed, and whether the change is larger than measurement error. Separate fitness from readiness, adaptation from noise, and correlation from cause.

4) **Adapt.** Change the plan based on what was learned. Adaptation applies to the athlete's body and to the coach's thinking. If the data disagree with the hypothesis, revise the hypothesis. Then return to training, and the cycle begins again.

### A cycle, not a line

The cycle repeats for the athlete's entire career. Each loop adds to the athlete's record and sharpens the coach's understanding. Early loops may be crude, with few measurements and much uncertainty. Later loops can be more precise, because the athlete's baseline, typical responses and personal limiters are known. This is the practical meaning of "the athlete is the laboratory": every block is an experiment, and the results accumulate.

KEY: Train, measure, understand and adapt is a loop. Skipping any step breaks the learning: training without measuring leaves you guessing, measuring without understanding leaves you with noise, and understanding without adapting changes nothing.

### Speed of the loop

Different questions run on different cycle lengths. Daily readiness decisions, the domain of AISRi, run on a loop of hours. Training-block decisions run over weeks. Questions about heat or altitude responses run over months. Questions about long-term development run over years. A good system keeps all of these loops turning without confusing them, so that a single bad night's sleep is not mistaken for a failed training philosophy.

### Where things go wrong

The loop fails in predictable ways. Some coaches train hard but measure nothing, so they cannot learn from the season. Some collect vast amounts of wearable data but never interpret it. Some interpret confidently but never change their minds. And some adapt constantly, changing plans every week in response to noise. The discipline is to move through all four steps with patience, allowing enough time for training to take effect before judging it.

### An example loop

Consider a club runner in Chennai preparing for a half marathon. The coach plans a four-week block of steady runs and one weekly threshold session (Train). Each week they record heart rate at a fixed easy pace on the same route before 6.30 a.m., along with WBGT, sleep and RPE (Measure). After four weeks, heart rate at the fixed pace has fallen by more than the typical day-to-day variation, while threshold sessions feel easier; the coach notes that two of the hottest mornings produced outlying values and excludes neither but annotates both (Understand). The next block increases threshold volume slightly and adds a durability probe to the long run (Adapt). Nothing about this is complicated, but it turns a season of running into a season of learning.

### The loop for the coach

The same four words apply to coaches themselves. A coach trains through practice, measures through records and reflection, understands through study, mentoring and conversation with scientists and clinicians, and adapts by changing methods when evidence demands it. A coach who stops learning eventually coaches the same year over and over again. The AKURA cycle is meant to keep both athlete and coach growing, season after season, so that experience becomes expertise rather than habit.

COACH: At the end of each training block, hold a fifteen-minute review with the athlete using the four words as headings: what did we train, what did we measure, what do we understand now, and what will we change?

CHECK: Which of the four steps is weakest in your current coaching practice? What one habit would strengthen it?

## 84. The Real Goal of AEI

In 2011, a schoolgirl from Iklauta village near Meerut lined up for an 800 m race without ever having trained. "Papa asked me if I wanted to run and I agreed. I had never practised before that school race," Parul Chaudhary told the Indian Express years later. She ran barefoot that day, and afterwards trained on the paths between sugarcane fields. Fifteen years later, on 27 September 2026 in Nagoya, she ran the steeplechase in 9:08.67 for Asian Games bronze and a national record, and followed it with bronze in the 5000 m. Between those two moments lie several coaches, a railway job, a police job, a training base in Colorado Springs and six national records in five seasons. No single number from that journey explains it. What explains it is a long accumulation of learning about one particular body, by the athlete and by the people around her.

It is tempting for any new index to chase attention: a single number that can be compared, ranked and shared. That is not the purpose of the AKURA Endurance Index. The real goal is not to produce a famous number. The goal is to help athletes become more understandable to themselves and their coaches. An athlete who knows why a race went well or badly, which environments suit them, how they respond to heat or altitude, and what their early warning signs of overload look like is better equipped for a long career than one who simply knows a score.

### What success would look like

If AEI eventually becomes a validated index, its success should be measured by whether it improves training decisions, reduces avoidable overload, identifies useful interventions and helps athletes perform more consistently. These are practical outcomes that can themselves be studied. Do coaches using AEI make decisions they later judge to be better? Do athletes experience fewer periods of unplanned fatigue or injury? Do interventions such as heat blocks or altitude camps become better targeted? Do race performances become more predictable? If the answer to these questions is no, the index has not yet earned its place, however elegant its mathematics.

### The number and the system

The number is the interface. The learning system is the product. A score is only a convenient way to summarise a large body of information. The real value lies underneath: the athlete card with traceable raw data, the standardised protocols, the environmental logs, the coach's annotations and overrides, and the accumulating record of how each athlete responds. If the score were removed tomorrow, a well-built AKURA system should still help coaches coach better.

KEY: AEI succeeds not when its number becomes famous but when athletes and coaches understand the body better and make better decisions because of it.

### A national opportunity

India has vast numbers of endurance athletes training across extraordinary diversity of climate, altitude, culture and circumstance: coastal heat in Chennai and Mumbai, hill stations in the Nilgiris and the Himalaya, dry heat in the north-west, humid monsoon seasons almost everywhere. Very little of this natural variation has been studied systematically. A carefully built, ethically run, openly reported research system could turn this diversity into knowledge that benefits Indian athletes first and contributes to global endurance science as well.

### Staying honest

The final responsibility of AEI is honesty. It must report uncertainty, admit when it is wrong, allow the data to disagree with its designers, and keep the athlete's health and dignity above any performance goal. It should be open to independent validation and willing to be revised or even replaced if better methods emerge. That humility is not weakness; it is what makes a research system trustworthy.

### What AEI must never become

It is equally important to state what AEI should not be. It should not become a selection tool that excludes young athletes on the basis of a single test. It should not be used to label athletes by region, background or appearance. It should not replace a coach's relationship with an athlete, or a clinician's judgement about health. And it should not be used to pressure athletes into training through illness or injury because "the numbers look fine". Any use of the index that reduces an athlete's dignity, safety or opportunity is a misuse, regardless of the data behind it.

### An invitation

This edition is a prototype, and prototypes are meant to be tested, broken and rebuilt. Coaches who try the protocols, athletes who keep careful logs, scientists who challenge the assumptions and clinicians who point out safety gaps all become part of the research. The long-term value of AEI will depend less on the ideas written here than on the quality of the data and the honesty of the people who collect and interpret it. If that community forms, the index will improve with every season, and Indian endurance athletes will have a research system built around their own bodies, climates and ambitions.

COACH: When you share any AEI or readiness number with an athlete, always pair it with an explanation of what it means for their training this week. A number without a conversation teaches nothing.

CHECK: If the AEI score disappeared tomorrow, what would your athletes still know about themselves because of the work you have done together?
