## 54. From AEI to Training

The purpose of an athlete index is not to create a pretty dashboard. A number that looks impressive on a screen but never alters a session, a pace target or a recovery day is decoration, not science. The AKURA Endurance Index (AEI) earns its place only if it improves decisions: which session to run, how hard to run it, when to hold back and when to push. This chapter describes how the different layers of AEI connect to the training plan, and why the coach remains the final point of responsibility.

### Five layers, five kinds of decision

Each layer of AEI answers a different practical question. A performance score describes current capacity and can guide intensity: it tells us what paces or power outputs are realistic for threshold, long-run or interval work. A readiness layer describes today's ability to express that capacity and can modify the session: shorten it, lower the target, or swap it for an easier day. Environmental data—temperature, humidity, altitude, air quality—can alter pace expectations, so that a threshold run in Chennai humidity is judged against the conditions rather than against a cool-morning benchmark. Recovery data such as sleep, resting heart rate and subjective fatigue can reduce unnecessary intensity before it becomes accumulated fatigue. Finally, actual workout execution—what was really run, at what heart rate and with what perceived effort—feeds back into the model and updates the next decision.

|AEI layer|Question it answers|Typical training decision|
|Performance|What can the athlete do?|Set pace or HR targets for each domain|
|Readiness|Can they express it today?|Keep, modify or replace the session|
|Environment|How hard are the conditions?|Adjust pace expectations, hydration, timing|
|Recovery|Is fatigue accumulating?|Reduce intensity or add a reset day|
|Execution|What actually happened?|Update the model and next week's plan|

KEY: AEI is a decision-support loop, not a scoreboard. Performance sets the targets, readiness and environment adjust them, recovery protects them, and execution data closes the loop.

### The feedback loop

A training plan is a hypothesis: "If this athlete does this work, they will adapt in this way." Each session is a small experiment, and execution data is its result. When an athlete completes a planned threshold session at the expected pace with a stable heart rate and moderate perceived effort, the model gains confidence in its estimate of threshold. When the same session produces an unusually high heart rate, a sharp rise in perceived exertion or an early stop, something in the model—fitness, readiness, environment or execution—needs to be re-examined. Over weeks, these small signals accumulate into a clearer picture than any single test can offer.

This loop also protects against a common error: treating the plan as more important than the athlete. A plan written three weeks ago did not know about last night's poor sleep, a sudden rise in humidity or an exam week. The loop allows the plan to bend without breaking.

### Judgement remains with the coach

The coach remains responsible for context. An algorithm can notice that resting heart rate is elevated; it cannot know that the athlete travelled overnight by train, is fasting for a festival, is caring for a sick family member or has just received good news that will lift motivation. Algorithms work with the variables they are given. Coaches work with the whole person.

For this reason, AEI outputs should be framed as recommendations with confidence levels, not commands. A sensible default is that the system proposes and the coach disposes: the coach accepts, modifies or overrides the suggestion, and the override itself is recorded so that the model can learn which contexts it handles poorly. Over time, a well-designed system should make the coach more consistent and better informed—not more passive.

COACH: Before each training block, write down which AEI outputs will influence which decisions—for example, "readiness below the athlete's usual range on a key day means we shorten intervals, not cancel them." Agreeing the rules in advance reduces emotional decisions on the day and makes your overrides easier to review later.

### Avoiding dashboard drift

Many monitoring systems begin with good intentions and slowly become cluttered with metrics that nobody acts upon. A useful discipline is to ask of every metric: "What decision would change if this number moved?" If the answer is "none", the metric may still have research value, but it should not compete for attention on the daily training screen. In Indian club and academy settings, where coaches may manage large groups with limited staff, fewer and clearer signals are usually more valuable than a comprehensive but unread report.

CHECK: For each metric you currently collect, can you name the specific training decision it influences? Which metric would you remove if you could only keep five?

## 55. Training Intensity Domains

Coaches and athletes need a shared language for effort. "Go easy" means different things to a teenager chasing a club record and a veteran marathoner returning from injury. AKURA uses broad intensity domains—Recovery, Aerobic, Endurance, Threshold, Power and Speed—that can be mapped to pace, heart rate, rating of perceived exertion (RPE) or workout structure depending on the athlete, the equipment available and the purpose of the session.

### The six AKURA domains

The domains are deliberately broad. They are categories of training purpose, not precise physiological boundaries, and the transitions between them are gradual rather than sharp. The table below gives illustrative descriptions; each athlete's anchors must be established from their own testing and training history.

|Domain|Main purpose|Feel (RPE 1–10)|Typical session examples|
|Recovery|Promote circulation and restoration|1–2, very easy, full conversation|20–40 min jog or brisk walk; easy cycling|
|Aerobic|Build aerobic base and capillarity|3–4, easy, conversational|45–75 min steady easy run|
|Endurance|Durability and fuel use over time|4–5, comfortable but sustained|Long run 90–150 min; marathon-pace segments|
|Threshold|Raise sustainable pace near lactate turn-point|6–7, comfortably hard|3 × 10 min or 20–30 min tempo|
|Power|Develop VO₂max and high aerobic power|8–9, hard, few words|5 × 3 min or 6 × 1 km with recoveries|
|Speed|Neuromuscular speed and running mechanics|Fast but relaxed; not maximal strain|8 × 100 m strides; 6 × 200 m with full recovery|

KEY: Intensity domains describe the purpose of a session, not only its pace. The same pace can belong to different domains on a cool morning and a humid afternoon.

### Anchoring domains to the individual

Established systems already show how current fitness can define training categories. Jack Daniels' VDOT approach, for example, derives Easy, Marathon, Threshold, Interval and Repetition paces from a recent race performance, and it remains a practical and widely used reference. Heart-rate zones based on laboratory lactate or ventilatory thresholds offer another route. RPE, meanwhile, is available to every athlete at no cost and captures internal strain that pace alone may miss.

AKURA does not need to replace these tools. The six domains can sit on top of any of them: a well-resourced athlete may have laboratory-derived heart-rate anchors, while a school athlete in a district programme may use recent race times and RPE. What matters is consistency—using the same anchoring method over time so that changes can be interpreted.

Research on intensity distribution adds useful perspective. Seiler and Kjerland's 2006 analysis of elite junior cross-country skiers found that much of the training volume was performed at low intensity, with a smaller proportion at high intensity. Such findings do not prescribe an exact ratio for every runner, but they caution against the common habit of doing most runs at a "moderately hard" pace that is too fast for recovery and too slow for high-quality stimulus.

### Adding environmental and readiness context

AEI should add environmental and readiness context rather than simply creating another pace table. A threshold pace established in December in Bengaluru may be unrealistic in May in Chennai. If the athlete insists on hitting the same pace in higher heat stress, the session silently moves from Threshold into Power, with a very different cost. AKURA's approach is to hold the domain constant and let pace flex: the session's purpose is protected, and heart rate and RPE help confirm the athlete is in the intended domain.

Readiness works the same way. On a low-readiness day, the athlete may still train in the Aerobic domain but at a slower pace than usual, or a planned Threshold session may be shortened. The domain language helps the coach explain the change: "Today's purpose is still threshold—we are just protecting it."

COACH: When conditions are hot or humid, prescribe threshold and endurance sessions by heart rate and RPE with a pace range as a guide, not a target. Tell athletes in advance that slower pace in heat is expected and does not mean lost fitness.

### Common misuses

Three misuses appear repeatedly. First, the grey zone: easy days drift into moderate effort, leaving the athlete too tired for quality days. Second, the chase: an athlete forces a pace from a watch rather than respecting the session's purpose. Third, the false precision: treating a zone boundary as a hard line, so that a heart rate two beats above the limit is judged a failure. Domains are tools for communication and planning, not tests of obedience.

CHECK: Over the last two weeks, what proportion of your athlete's runs were genuinely in the Recovery or Aerobic domains? Were any "easy" days actually moderate?

## 56. The 3–3–1 Concept

Weekly structure gives training its rhythm. Athletes like to know what Tuesday and Saturday will bring, and coaches need a framework that balances stress and recovery. AKURA uses a 3–3–1 concept as one possible weekly architecture. It is a starting template for thinking, not a rule to be applied identically to every athlete.

### What 3–3–1 means

A 3–3–1 weekly rhythm can be interpreted as three days emphasising aerobic development, three days supporting complementary qualities or recovery, and one key recovery or reset day. The three aerobic-development days usually carry the week's main endurance stimulus: a long run, a threshold session and a steady aerobic run, for example. The three complementary days might include strength work, speed or strides, easy running, mobility or cross-training. The single reset day allows physical and psychological recovery—complete rest for some athletes, a short easy jog or walk for others.

The exact structure must be individualised. A school athlete balancing examinations, a working professional training before office hours and a full-time academy runner will not share the same week. Some athletes need two reset days; some advanced athletes may combine elements on the same day.

|Day|Illustrative emphasis|AKURA domain|Example content|
|Mon|Reset|Recovery|Rest or 20–30 min very easy jog; mobility|
|Tue|Aerobic development|Threshold|Warm-up; 3 × 10 min threshold, 2 min jog; cool-down|
|Wed|Complementary|Aerobic + strength|45 min easy; runner strength session (Chapter 58)|
|Thu|Aerobic development|Aerobic|60–70 min steady aerobic run|
|Fri|Complementary|Recovery + speed|30–40 min easy; 6–8 × 100 m relaxed strides|
|Sat|Complementary|Aerobic + strength|40 min easy; short strength maintenance|
|Sun|Aerobic development|Endurance|Long run 90–120 min, early morning in heat season|

This example week is illustrative only. It must be individualised to the athlete's training age, injury history, race calendar, climate and life commitments before use.

KEY: The value of 3–3–1 lies in how it distributes stress and recovery, not in the numbers themselves. A week is well designed when the athlete can absorb it and arrive at key sessions ready to perform.

### Load distribution over labels

Two athletes with the same weekly kilometres can experience very different stress depending on intensity, hills, heat and strength work. Sixty kilometres of flat, easy running on cool mornings is not the same as sixty kilometres containing two threshold sessions, a hilly long run and afternoon runs in 34°C humidity. Kilometre totals are easy to count and easy to compare on social media, but they hide much of what the body actually experiences.

For this reason, AEI should track internal load as well as external kilometres. A practical and validated approach is session RPE: the athlete rates the whole session on a 0–10 scale about 30 minutes after finishing, and this rating is multiplied by the duration in minutes to produce a session load in arbitrary units. Session RPE, described by Foster and colleagues, requires no equipment and captures the combined effect of pace, terrain, heat and fatigue. Heart-rate-based measures can complement it where reliable data are available.

### Spacing the key days

The order of days matters. Hard sessions placed back to back leave little time for recovery unless the athlete is highly trained and the second session is deliberately modest. In the illustrative week, the threshold day and long run are separated by lower-stress days, and the reset day follows the long run. Strength work sits on days when the legs can tolerate it without compromising the next key running session.

Environmental timing also belongs in the design. In much of India, the long run is best placed early on a weekend morning, before heat and humidity rise and before traffic makes roads unsafe. During monsoon months, flexible scheduling helps—moving a threshold session by a day to avoid waterlogged roads is sensible planning, not indiscipline.

COACH: Record session RPE for every session for four weeks, including strength and cross-training. Compare weekly load with weekly kilometres. You will often find that the "hardest" week by kilometres was not the hardest week for the athlete.

### When 3–3–1 does not fit

Rigid adherence to any template can cause problems. An athlete returning from injury may need a 2–4–1 week with fewer aerobic-development days. An athlete in a taper will reduce volume while preserving some intensity. Travel weeks, exam weeks and festival periods may call for a simpler structure. The concept is useful precisely because it can flex: it reminds the coach to balance development, support and recovery, whatever the exact numbers.

CHECK: If you compared two of your athletes with similar weekly kilometres, would their internal load be similar? What in their weeks—heat, hills, intensity, strength—might explain the difference?

## 57. Progressive Overload Without Blind Progression

Adaptation requires progressive challenge. If the training stimulus never changes, the body has little reason to change either. But progression that ignores the athlete's response is not a strategy; it is a hope. This chapter argues for progression that is planned, monitored and reversible.

### The problem with fixed percentages

A fixed percentage increase every week—such as the familiar "add 10% per week"—is easy to remember but not always appropriate. For a beginner running 15 km per week, 10% is a tiny change; for an athlete already at 120 km, it may be a large one. Percentages also ignore what the kilometres contain: adding 10% of easy running is very different from adding 10% of threshold work or hill repetitions.

The athlete's current load, recovery, injury history, race calendar and environmental stress all matter. An athlete who has recently come through a stress injury needs more conservative progression than a healthy athlete with years of consistent training. An athlete entering the hottest months may need to hold volume while the body adapts to thermal strain, even if the calendar says it is a "build" week.

MYTH: Increasing weekly mileage by exactly 10% is the safe way to progress. || There is no universal safe percentage. Safe progression depends on current load, training history, session content, recovery and environment, and should be adjusted to the athlete's observed response.

### Load, capacity and the training–injury paradox

Gabbett's 2016 review on the training–injury prevention paradox argued that well-developed physical qualities, built through appropriately high training loads, can be protective, while rapid spikes in load relative to what the athlete is prepared for are associated with higher injury risk. The practical message is not "train less" or "train more", but "build load steadily and avoid sudden jumps". Methods used to express this relationship, such as acute:chronic workload ratios, have since been debated statistically, so they are best treated as prompts for attention rather than precise risk predictions.

KEY: The key question is not "Did mileage increase?" but "Did the athlete absorb the load and produce a better response?" Progression is earned by adaptation, not scheduled by the calendar alone.

### A decision matrix for progression

Progression should be planned, monitored and reversible. At the end of each week or block, the coach can review a small set of signals: whether key sessions were completed as intended, how heart rate and RPE compared with previous weeks at similar paces, trends in sleep and resting heart rate, soreness or pain, and the athlete's own sense of how the week went. The matrix below offers an illustrative way to turn those signals into a decision.

|Absorbed-load signals|Interpretation|Decision|
|Key sessions hit; HR/RPE stable or lower; sleep and mood normal|Load absorbed, positive response|Progress: modest increase in volume or one quality variable|
|Sessions completed but RPE higher than usual; mild fatigue|Load absorbed at a cost|Hold: repeat similar week before progressing|
|Mixed: one key session missed or cut; readiness below usual for 2–3 days|Load at the edge of tolerance|Hold or small reduction; review sleep, heat, life stress|
|Multiple sessions compromised; persistent fatigue; rising resting HR|Load not absorbed|Reduce: lower volume and intensity for several days|
|New or worsening localised pain; limping; altered gait|Possible injury warning|Reduce and seek assessment from a qualified professional|

These signals and thresholds are illustrative and should be calibrated to each athlete's own baseline.

### Reduction is part of adaptation

A reduction week is not failure; it is part of adaptation. Many athletes—and some coaches—feel that a lighter week wastes fitness. In reality, recovery is when much of the adaptation to the preceding training is consolidated. Planned reduction weeks, for example every third or fourth week, allow fatigue to dissipate and provide a cleaner window in which to test or observe the athlete's response. Unplanned reductions, triggered by the decision matrix, are equally legitimate: they are the plan responding intelligently to the athlete.

Reversibility also means communicating early. Athletes who know that the plan will adapt to their response are more likely to report fatigue honestly, rather than hiding it to protect their "streak".

COACH: At the end of each week, ask three questions: Were the key sessions completed as intended? Did the effort feel appropriate for the pace? Is anything hurting that did not hurt last week? Let the answers—not the calendar alone—decide whether next week progresses, holds or reduces.

CHECK: Look back at your athlete's last build phase. Was each increase in load preceded by clear evidence that the previous load had been absorbed? Where would a hold week have helped?

## 58. Strength and Running Economy

For many years, distance runners were told that strength training would make them heavy and slow. The evidence now points in a different direction. Strength training can support force production, neuromuscular coordination and resilience. It should be integrated with running rather than treated as a separate sport—and it should be judged by what it does for running, not by how much weight the athlete can lift.

### What the evidence suggests

Several reviews have examined strength training in endurance athletes. Beattie and colleagues' 2014 review in Sports Medicine concluded that strength training, including heavy and explosive methods, can improve performance-related outcomes in endurance athletes, with running economy among the most frequently reported benefits. Balsalobre-Fernández and colleagues' 2016 meta-analysis of controlled trials in highly trained runners found that strength training programmes of several weeks were associated with improvements in running economy. Earlier, Paavolainen and colleagues showed that replacing part of endurance training with explosive-strength work improved 5-km time in well-trained runners, linked to improved economy and muscle power.

These findings are encouraging but should be read carefully. Study samples are often small, programmes vary, and effects on actual race performance are less consistently measured than effects on economy. The likely mechanisms—improved neuromuscular recruitment, tendon and muscle stiffness characteristics, and better force application—are plausible but not fully settled.

KEY: For distance runners, the objective is not bodybuilding volume. It is sufficient strength, control, stiffness management and movement quality without compromising key running sessions.

### Designing strength for runners

Effective strength work for runners tends to share a few features: relatively low volume, emphasis on quality of movement, progressive loading of major lower-limb muscles, some plyometric or elastic work once basic strength is established, and attention to the calf–Achilles complex and hip stability that running places under repeated demand. Sessions are usually short—30 to 45 minutes—and performed two times per week in general preparation, reducing to one maintenance session during competition phases.

|Exercise|Illustrative prescription|Purpose|Coaching note|
|Goblet or back squat|3 × 5–6, controlled|Hip and knee extensor strength|Stop 2–3 reps short of failure|
|Romanian deadlift|3 × 6|Posterior chain, hamstrings|Neutral spine; hinge at hips|
|Rear-foot elevated split squat|3 × 6 each leg|Single-leg strength and control|Knee tracks over mid-foot|
|Single-leg calf raise (straight and bent knee)|3 × 10–12 each|Calf–Achilles capacity|Slow lowering; full range|
|Pogo jumps or low hurdle hops|3 × 10 contacts|Elastic stiffness, ground contact|Quiet, quick contacts; add later|
|Side plank with hip abduction|2 × 20–30 s each side|Lateral hip and trunk control|Stable pelvis|

This session is illustrative and should be adapted to the athlete's training age, technique, injury history and available equipment. Beginners should start with bodyweight or light loads and learn movement patterns before adding plyometrics; athletes with current pain should be assessed by a qualified professional first.

### Integration with the running week

Strength should be placed so that it supports, rather than competes with, key running sessions. A common approach is to schedule strength on the same day as an easy run or after a quality session, leaving the following day easy, so that the hard days stay hard and the easy days stay easy. Heavy lower-body work immediately before a threshold session or long run is usually unwise. During the hottest months, strength sessions performed indoors can also provide useful training stimulus when outdoor running volume is limited by heat.

Rønnestad and Mujika's 2014 review on optimising strength training for running and cycling highlighted the importance of maintaining some strength work during the competition season, since gains can fade when strength training stops entirely.

COACH: Start runners with two short sessions per week built around four or five movements. Progress load only when technique is stable and the running sessions that follow are unaffected. If key runs are suffering, reduce strength volume before reducing running quality.

### What AEI should—and should not—track

AEI can track strength adherence and selected performance markers—for example, sessions completed per week, a single-leg calf raise count, a countermovement jump height or a simple loaded squat estimate—alongside changes in running economy or submaximal heart rate. These records help the coach see whether strength work is being done consistently and whether it coincides with improvements in running.

However, AEI should not assume that more strength always means faster running. Beyond a sufficient level, additional strength may offer diminishing returns for a distance runner, and extra training time spent in the gym is time not spent recovering or running. The relationship is likely non-linear and individual. The useful question is not "How strong is the athlete?" but "Is the athlete strong enough to run well, stay healthy and express their aerobic fitness efficiently?"

MYTH: Strength training will make distance runners bulky and slow. || Low-volume, well-programmed strength and plyometric work is associated with improved running economy in several controlled studies, with little change in body mass when combined with endurance training.

## 59. Case Study A: High VO₂max, Weak Marathon

This is a hypothetical case study. Athlete A (Chennai, 28, club marathoner) has excellent 5K performance and a high VO₂max from a laboratory test, but shows a large fall in pace over the final 10–12 km of the marathon. On paper, the athlete looks capable of a much faster marathon than they have run. The coach's first instinct—and the athlete's—is often to chase even more speed. AEI suggests a different line of enquiry.

|Metric|Illustrative value|Interpretation|
|VO₂max (lab treadmill)|66 ml/kg/min|High aerobic ceiling for a club athlete|
|5K personal best|16:20|Strong short-distance performance|
|Marathon best|2:58, second half 8 min slower|Large positive split; poor durability|
|Threshold as % of VO₂max|~76%|Relatively low fractional utilisation|
|Longest regular long run|24 km, done at mixed paces|Limited race-specific durability|
|Race-day carbohydrate intake|Irregular, gels only after 25 km|Possible fuelling shortfall|
|Race conditions|Start 5:30 am, 27°C, high humidity|Meaningful thermal strain|

All values are illustrative and invented for teaching purposes.

### What AEI shows

AEI would not reduce Athlete A to "needs more VO₂max." Their ceiling is already high; the gap lies in how much of that ceiling can be sustained, for how long, in race conditions. The profile directs attention toward durability, pacing, substrate management and race-specific conditioning. In AEI terms, the performance layer shows high capacity, but indicators of fractional utilisation and late-race resilience lag behind—a mismatch rather than a deficiency. This reflects a point made by Joyner and Coyle in their 2008 review: endurance performance depends on VO₂max, the fraction of it that can be sustained, and running economy acting together.

KEY: A high VO₂max describes the size of the engine, not how long it can run near full power. Marathon performance depends on sustaining a large fraction of capacity for hours, often in heat.

### Likely contributors

Possible contributors include insufficient long-run durability, fuelling errors, excessive starting intensity, low threshold relative to maximal capacity and environmental strain. These are hypotheses, not a diagnosis, and more than one may be operating.

- Durability: long runs have been relatively short and inconsistent, so late-race fatigue resistance may be under-developed. Maunder and colleagues have argued that "durability"—how physiological markers drift with prolonged exercise—deserves a place in athlete profiling.
- Fuelling: taking carbohydrate only after 25 km may leave the athlete short of fuel at the point where glycogen becomes limiting.
- Pacing: a fast early pace, encouraged by a strong 5K background, may produce an unsustainable first half.
- Threshold: threshold at a modest percentage of VO₂max suggests room to raise sustainable pace.
- Heat and humidity: Chennai race mornings impose thermal strain that increases cardiovascular drift late in the race.

### Intervention plan (8–12 weeks)

- Weeks 1–4: extend the long run progressively from 24 km towards 30 km in the Endurance domain, early morning, with steady pacing; introduce one threshold session per week (for example 3 × 12 min).
- Weeks 1–12: practise race-day fuelling in every long run, starting carbohydrate early and aiming for a consistent intake the athlete tolerates, developed with a qualified sports nutritionist where possible.
- Weeks 5–8: add marathon-pace segments late in long runs (for example the final 8–10 km) to train pace control under fatigue.
- Weeks 5–10: progress threshold volume; reduce high-intensity Power sessions to one short session per week to preserve speed without excessive fatigue.
- Throughout: two short strength sessions per week; heat-aware pacing, with heart-rate and RPE ceilings on hot days.
- Weeks 11–12: taper; rehearse race-morning routine, including pacing plan with an even or slightly negative split target.

### What to retest

- Threshold pace and heart rate (field test or laboratory) at weeks 0, 6 and 12.
- Pace–heart-rate decoupling in the last third of long runs of similar length and conditions.
- Gut tolerance and energy in the final third of long runs (athlete log).
- Pacing profile and second-half slowdown in a tune-up half marathon and the target marathon.

COACH: Keep a "late-race" column in the athlete's log: pace, heart rate and RPE for the final 30 minutes of every long run. Improvement in this column is often more meaningful for a marathoner than a faster 5K.

### What would change our mind

If the athlete completes longer, well-fuelled, evenly paced long runs with little decoupling but still slows sharply in the marathon, the explanation may lie elsewhere—perhaps race-day heat management, a gastrointestinal problem or a musculoskeletal issue emerging late in the race. If threshold does not improve despite consistent work, the coach may need to reconsider training distribution or recovery. And if the athlete runs an evenly paced marathon only in cool conditions, heat may be a larger contributor than assumed.

CHECK: For an athlete like Athlete A, which single change—long-run length, fuelling, pacing or threshold work—would you prioritise first, and what evidence would tell you it worked?

## 60. Case Study B: Moderate VO₂max, Excellent Economy

This is a hypothetical case study. Athlete B (Pune, 31, amateur half-marathoner and marathoner) has only a moderate VO₂max, yet runs longer races better than several training partners with far higher laboratory values. The athlete shows a very good oxygen cost at submaximal running speeds and strong threshold performance. This case demonstrates why a single physiological metric cannot define the whole athlete.

|Metric|Illustrative value|Interpretation|
|VO₂max (lab treadmill)|55 ml/kg/min|Moderate for a competitive amateur|
|Running economy at 14 km/h|~185 ml O₂/kg/km|Low oxygen cost; efficient|
|Threshold as % of VO₂max|~87%|High fractional utilisation|
|Half-marathon best|1:21|Strong relative to VO₂max|
|1500 m / 5K times|Modest; limited finishing speed|Low speed reserve|
|Training history|8 years consistent, mostly aerobic|Deep aerobic base|

All values are illustrative and invented for teaching purposes.

### What AEI shows

AEI shows a profile in which economy and fractional utilisation compensate for a moderate ceiling. This athlete may outperform a higher-VO₂max runner at longer distances, because racing speed at the marathon depends on the oxygen that can be sustained and how economically it is used—not just on the maximum. Jones's analysis of the physiology of the women's marathon world-record holder illustrated how improvements in running economy and threshold, rather than VO₂max alone, can accompany performance progression.

KEY: A single physiological metric cannot define the whole athlete. Moderate VO₂max combined with excellent economy and a high threshold can produce outstanding endurance performance.

### Likely contributors

- Long training history building aerobic and musculoskeletal adaptations.
- Favourable running mechanics and possibly anthropometric factors that reduce oxygen cost.
- Consistent threshold-focused training that raises sustainable pace.
- Limited exposure to Speed and Power domains, restricting finishing kick and speed reserve.

These contributors are plausible, but economy is influenced by many factors—footwear, fatigue, measurement conditions and body mass—so a single laboratory value should be interpreted with caution.

### Intervention plan (8–12 weeks)

The training focus might be to preserve economy while improving speed reserve, rather than endlessly chasing maximal oxygen uptake.

- Maintain the established aerobic volume and long run; avoid dramatic changes that could disrupt what already works.
- Add regular Speed-domain work: strides after easy runs two times per week and short relaxed repetitions (for example 8 × 200 m) with full recovery.
- Include one Power session every 7–10 days (for example 5 × 3 min) to nudge aerobic power without large volume.
- Maintain two strength sessions per week, adding progressive plyometrics once basic strength is stable.
- Monitor economy-related markers so that added intensity does not degrade efficiency.

### What to retest

- Running economy at the same standard speeds and conditions, ideally the same laboratory and footwear.
- VO₂max and threshold, noting whether threshold remains a high percentage of maximum.
- Short-distance performance (1500 m or 5K time trial) as a marker of speed reserve.
- Half-marathon race performance and pacing profile.

COACH: When an athlete's strength lies in economy, protect it. Introduce speed in small, relaxed doses and check that easy-run heart rate at familiar paces does not drift upwards as intensity is added.

### What would change our mind

If added speed work increases fatigue and worsens economy or submaximal heart rate, the intervention may be too aggressive, or the athlete may benefit more from continued aerobic development. If the "excellent economy" does not reproduce on repeat testing, the initial result may reflect measurement error or favourable test conditions rather than a stable characteristic. And if longer-race results stall while speed improves, the balance may need to shift back toward threshold and endurance.

CHECK: Among your athletes, who performs better than their VO₂max would predict? What might their economy and threshold profiles reveal?

## 61. Case Study C: Strong Heat Response

This is a hypothetical case study. Athlete C (Kochi, 24, 10K and half-marathon runner) completes a controlled heat-acclimation block before a humid coastal race season. Afterwards, the athlete shows a lower heart rate at a standard submaximal workload, improved thermal comfort and a measurable increase in plasma-volume-related markers. The case is useful not because the response is dramatic, but because it shows how to interpret a response carefully.

|Metric|Before (illustrative)|After (illustrative)|Interpretation|
|HR at 10 km/h, 32°C, 70% RH, 30 min|164 bpm|151 bpm|Lower cardiovascular strain|
|RPE at same workload|7|5|Session feels easier|
|Thermal comfort (1–7 scale)|6 (hot)|4|Improved perceived tolerance|
|Estimated plasma volume change|—|about +6%|Consistent with acclimation|
|Sweat rate (L/h)|1.1|1.3|Possible earlier, greater sweating|
|Body-mass loss in session|1.6%|1.8%|Hydration needs rising|

### What AEI shows

AEI's environmental layer records a clear, measurable response to a specific intervention. Heart rate and RPE fall at an identical workload in identical conditions, and markers linked to plasma-volume expansion move in the expected direction. These changes match the adaptations commonly described in heat-acclimation research, summarised by Périard, Racinais and Sawka in 2015: lower heart rate, earlier and greater sweating, plasma-volume expansion and improved thermal comfort.

The correct conclusion is not "heat made the athlete elite." It is that the athlete demonstrated a measurable response to a specific intervention. Whether that response improves race performance in the heat is a separate question requiring a separate measurement.

KEY: A good heat response is evidence of adaptation to heat, not proof of general performance improvement. Record the intervention, the test conditions and the response, then test again.

### Likely contributors

- The heat-acclimation protocol itself: repeated sessions producing sustained elevation of body temperature over roughly 10–14 days.
- Standardised testing conditions that make the before–after comparison meaningful.
- Possible contributions from improved hydration habits and familiarity with the test (a learning effect).
- Seasonal change: if outdoor temperatures rose during the block, natural acclimatisation may have contributed.

### Intervention plan (8–12 weeks)

- Weeks 1–2: structured acclimation block—for example 60–90 minutes of mostly Aerobic-domain running on most days in hot, humid conditions, with heart-rate and RPE ceilings and a safety plan.
- Weeks 3–8: maintenance exposures two to three times per week to help retain adaptations, since they decay without continued exposure.
- Throughout: individual hydration plan based on measured sweat rate and body-mass change; avoid large body-mass losses and also avoid over-drinking.
- Weeks 6–10: race-specific sessions in heat with heat-adjusted pacing; practise cooling strategies such as pre-cooling and ice towels.
- Weeks 11–12: taper, maintain light heat exposure, rehearse race-day routine.

COACH: Run heat sessions in a buddy system, with water, shade and a clear stop rule for symptoms such as dizziness, confusion, nausea or stopping sweating. Heat acclimation is a training stimulus with real risk; any athlete showing warning signs should stop and receive appropriate care immediately.

### What to retest

- The same standard heat test (speed, duration, temperature, humidity, time of day, hydration state) at 2 weeks and 6–8 weeks.
- Plasma-volume-related markers under consistent conditions.
- Sweat rate and body-mass change to update the hydration plan.
- A heat-affected performance marker, such as a time trial in controlled warm conditions.

Repeat testing is required before treating the response as stable.

### What would change our mind

If the heart-rate reduction disappears on a second test with stricter standardisation, the first result may have reflected day-to-day variability, hydration differences or cooler conditions. If laboratory-style markers improve but performance in hot races does not, the adaptation may be real but insufficient to change the race outcome—or other limiters such as pacing or fuelling may dominate. And if the athlete's response is much smaller in a second block, individual variability, not a stable trait, may be the better explanation.

## 62. Case Study D: Altitude Response

This is a hypothetical case study. Athlete D (Bengaluru, 26, 5000 m and 10,000 m track athlete) completes a documented altitude camp. After returning, haemoglobin mass (Hb mass) has increased and a standardised submaximal test improves after sufficient recovery. The case illustrates how measurement strengthens a causal story that is often told with no data at all.

|Metric|Before (illustrative)|After (illustrative)|Interpretation|
|Altitude exposure|—|~24 days at ~2,100–2,200 m, ~20 h/day|Documented dose|
|Hb mass (CO rebreathing)|820 g|855 g (+4%)|Plausible haematological response|
|Serum ferritin (pre-camp screen)|Within adequate range|Monitored|Iron status supports adaptation|
|HR at standard submax speed (sea level)|158 bpm|152 bpm|Lower cardiovascular strain|
|Blood lactate at same speed|2.8 mmol/L|2.3 mmol/L|Improved submaximal response|
|10,000 m time (2–3 weeks post-camp)|31:05|30:40|Performance improvement|

All values are illustrative and invented for teaching purposes. Retests assume similar conditions, time of day and recovery status.

### What AEI shows

This is a stronger causal story than simply saying "the athlete trained in the hills and ran faster." The measurement separates environmental exposure from the performance outcome. We have a documented dose (time and altitude), a physiological mediator (Hb mass), a standardised submaximal response and a performance result. Each link can be examined on its own. The observed Hb-mass increase is in line with what Gore and colleagues' 2013 meta-analysis reported for similar exposures using the optimised carbon-monoxide rebreathing method, though individual responses vary widely.

KEY: Measuring the exposure, the physiological response and the performance separately turns a story into evidence. Even then, the result belongs to that athlete and that protocol.

### Likely contributors

- Hypoxic stimulus sufficient in duration and altitude to increase red-cell production.
- Adequate iron availability, energy intake and recovery supporting that response.
- Training performed during the camp, which may itself have improved fitness regardless of altitude.
- Focused camp environment: fewer distractions, more sleep, structured routine.
- Race selection and conditions after the camp.

The last three contributors matter: without a comparison condition, some of the improvement may have occurred with equivalent training at home.

### Intervention plan (8–12 weeks)

- Weeks 1–2 (pre-camp): baseline Hb mass, submaximal test and iron screen, with any supplementation decided by a qualified medical professional.
- Weeks 3–6 (camp): reduce intensity for the first days at altitude, rebuild volume gradually, monitor resting heart rate, sleep and oxygen saturation trends, and keep high-intensity work controlled.
- Weeks 7–8 (return): allow a period of readjustment before key tests or races; avoid judging the camp on the first few days back.
- Weeks 8–12: repeat submaximal test and Hb mass; race or time-trial in suitable conditions.

COACH: Log the altitude "dose" as carefully as you log training: altitude of sleeping and training locations, hours per day and number of days. Without the dose, you cannot compare one camp with another.

### What to retest

- Hb mass using the same method and laboratory, allowing for its typical measurement error.
- The standardised submaximal test at sea level, controlling time of day, temperature and preceding training.
- Performance in a comparable race or time trial.
- Whether gains persist 4–6 weeks after return.

### What would change our mind

If Hb mass rises but submaximal and race performance do not improve, the haematological adaptation may not have been the limiting factor. If performance improves without any change in Hb mass, other mechanisms—training quality, rest or motivation—may explain the result. If a second camp produces no Hb-mass change, the athlete's response may be less reliable than the first result suggested. Even with a clear response, this result should not be generalised to everyone: other athletes, altitudes and protocols may produce different outcomes.

## 63. Case Study E: Strong Fitness, Poor Readiness

This is a hypothetical case study. Athlete E (Hyderabad, 35, masters marathoner working in the IT sector) has an AEI-Fitness score that is high by their own standards: recent threshold tests and long runs show the best form in years. Yet over the past week sleep has been poor, resting heart rate is elevated relative to baseline, and subjective fatigue is high. A key threshold session is scheduled for tomorrow. The case shows why fitness and readiness must be kept separate.

|Metric|Athlete baseline (illustrative)|Current week (illustrative)|Interpretation|
|AEI-Fitness|Personal high|Unchanged|Capacity is strong|
|Sleep duration (7-day mean)|7.2 h|5.4 h|Substantial sleep deficit|
|Morning resting HR|48 bpm|55 bpm|Elevated relative to baseline|
|Subjective fatigue (1–10)|3|7|High perceived fatigue|
|Muscle soreness (1–10)|2|4|Moderately raised|
|Context|—|Project deadline; late calls|Life stress increasing|

All values are illustrative and invented for teaching purposes. Thresholds for concern should be set from each athlete's own baseline and variability.

### What AEI shows

A readiness layer can prevent the coach from confusing capacity with today's ability to express capacity. The performance layer says the athlete can run a strong threshold session; the readiness layer says the athlete may not be able to run it well, or absorb it well, today. Several independent signals—sleep, resting heart rate and subjective fatigue—point in the same direction, which strengthens the case for caution. Reviews of athlete monitoring, including Saw, Main and Gastin's 2016 systematic review, have found that subjective self-reported measures often respond to training load and well-being changes at least as sensitively as common objective markers.

KEY: Fitness is what the athlete has built; readiness is what the athlete can express today. Treating them as the same number leads to hard sessions on the wrong days.

### Likely contributors

- Sleep restriction linked to work deadlines and late calls.
- Accumulated training load from a strong build phase.
- Psychological stress, which adds to physical load.
- Possible early illness—which the coach should not diagnose, but should consider when advising the athlete to seek medical advice if symptoms appear or persist.
- Environmental strain if recent training has been in afternoon heat.

### Intervention plan (8–12 weeks)

The correct intervention may be a modified workout rather than a complete programme change. The next 24–72 hours of data become part of the decision.

- Tomorrow: replace 3 × 10 min threshold with 40–45 min Aerobic-domain running plus 6 relaxed strides, or shorten the threshold work to 2 × 8 min if the athlete feels better after the warm-up.
- Next 24–72 hours: monitor resting HR, sleep and fatigue; reschedule the threshold session once two or more signals return towards baseline.
- Weeks 1–4: agree a simple readiness routine—morning resting HR, sleep hours and a one-line well-being score—and practical sleep strategies around work demands.
- Weeks 1–12: plan key sessions around predictable work peaks; place reduction weeks where deadlines cluster.
- Throughout: if symptoms such as fever, chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness appear, stop training and consult a doctor.

COACH: Offer the athlete a choice within a range—"today we run easy, or a shortened threshold if the warm-up feels good"—rather than a flat cancellation. Athletes who feel trusted to adjust report fatigue more honestly, and the data become more reliable.

### What to retest

- Resting HR, sleep and subjective fatigue daily for the next week, compared with the athlete's own baseline range.
- Performance and effort in the rescheduled threshold session: pace, heart rate and RPE compared with recent equivalents.
- Weekly readiness trends over the 8–12 weeks to see whether work stress and training peaks are better aligned.

### What would change our mind

If readiness signals return to baseline within 24–48 hours and the rescheduled session goes well, the episode was likely short-term and the programme can continue unchanged. If signals remain depressed for more than a week despite reduced load, the coach should review total training load, life stress and recovery more broadly, and encourage the athlete to seek medical assessment to rule out health issues. Sustained underperformance with persistent fatigue deserves careful attention; the consensus statement on overtraining syndrome by Meeusen and colleagues emphasises that such states require exclusion of other causes by qualified professionals. Conversely, if the athlete repeatedly performs well despite poor readiness scores, the readiness model may need recalibrating for this individual.
