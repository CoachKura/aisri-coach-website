## 44. The AKURA Athlete Baseline

A serious athlete research programme starts with a baseline. Without a clear picture of where an athlete begins, every later number floats free of context: a threshold pace that looks impressive may simply reflect a cool morning, and a heart rate that looks alarming may be that athlete's normal. The AKURA baseline is not a single test but a structured snapshot of the athlete's physiology, training history and daily life, recorded in a way that can be repeated months or years later. It has two layers: a minimum field baseline that any coach in India can collect with a watch, a scale and a notebook, and a laboratory baseline added when it is clinically and logistically appropriate.

### The minimum field baseline

The minimum field baseline can include body mass, a recent race result, resting heart rate, the heart-rate variability (HRV) trend, sleep, training history, the easy pace-HR relationship, a threshold session and long-run durability. None of these requires a laboratory, yet together they describe much of what the AEI needs. The race result anchors performance; resting HR and HRV describe the autonomic background; sleep and training history explain what the athlete has been able to absorb; the easy pace-HR relationship describes aerobic efficiency; the threshold session estimates sustainable intensity; and long-run durability shows how well that efficiency holds as fatigue builds.

The laboratory baseline may include blood testing, body composition, ECG, echocardiography, VO₂ testing and lactate testing. These add depth and precision, but they are not a prerequisite for a meaningful AEI. A well-run field battery, repeated consistently, often tells the coach more than a single expensive laboratory visit that is never repeated.

|Domain|Field measure|Laboratory measure|
|Performance|Recent race or time trial|Incremental test to exhaustion|
|Aerobic efficiency|Easy pace-HR relationship|Submax VO₂ and running economy|
|Threshold|Threshold session pace and HR|Lactate step test|
|Durability|Long-run pace-HR decoupling|Prolonged steady-state testing|
|Health background|Resting HR, HRV, sleep, body mass|Blood markers, ECG, echocardiography|
|Body composition|Body mass trend|DXA or other validated method|

FIELD: AKURA field baseline battery (spread over 7–10 days) ; Days 1–7: record waking resting HR, HRV (same device, same posture), sleep duration and morning body mass after toilet, before food ; Day 2: standardised submax run – 10 min easy warm-up, then 20 min at a fixed easy pace on a flat, known route, recording mean HR for minutes 10–20 ; Day 4: threshold session – for example 3 × 10 min at estimated threshold effort with 2 min jog, recording pace, HR and RPE for each rep ; Day 7: long run of the athlete's usual duration at easy effort on a flat route, recording pace and HR for pace-HR decoupling ; Log temperature, humidity, time of day, surface and shoes for every session

### Metadata: the invisible half of every test

Every test needs a protocol, date, device, operator, environmental conditions and units. Without those fields, longitudinal comparison becomes fragile. A resting heart rate taken lying down on waking is not comparable with one taken seated after tea; a lactate value from a fingertip is not directly comparable with one from an earlobe; a submax run at 6 a.m. in Pune in January is not the same test as the same run at 4 p.m. in Chennai in May. Metadata is what allows AEI to compare like with like, and to flag comparisons that should not be made.

|Metadata field|Example entry|Why it matters|
|Protocol ID|SUBMAX-20-v1|Ensures the same test is repeated|
|Date and time|2026-03-14, 06:10|Circadian and seasonal effects|
|Device and firmware|Chest strap, watch model|Device changes alter values|
|Operator|Coach or lab technician name|Operator technique varies|
|Environment|Temp, humidity, WBGT, wind|Heat alters HR and lactate|
|Units|mmol/L, ml/kg/min, min/km|Prevents conversion errors|
|Athlete state|Sleep, illness, last meal|Explains outliers|

KEY: A baseline is not a single number but a reproducible snapshot. The test and its metadata together form the measurement; a value without its protocol, device, conditions and units cannot safely be compared over time.

### Typical error and the smallest worthwhile change

Every measurement contains noise. Hopkins's influential 2000 review in Sports Medicine described how the typical error of a measurement, the within-athlete variation when a test is repeated under stable conditions, determines how confidently we can detect real change. Alongside typical error sits the smallest worthwhile change: the smallest difference that would matter in practice, often estimated from the variation between athletes or from what would change a race result. A change is interesting only when it is clearly larger than the noise and at least as large as the smallest worthwhile change. The practical way to estimate typical error for an individual athlete is simple: repeat the same test two or three times, close together, under controlled conditions, and see how much the results vary when nothing real has changed.

COACH: In the first month, run the standardised submax test twice, a week apart, before any major training change. The difference between those two runs gives you a rough sense of that athlete's day-to-day noise. Later, treat only changes clearly beyond that range as signals worth acting on.

## 45. Blood Testing

Blood tests are powerful because they reach beneath performance into physiology: oxygen-carrying capacity, iron stores, vitamin status, thyroid and kidney function. They are also easy to misuse. A panel ordered without a question produces numbers without meaning, and a coach who interprets them without clinical training can cause real harm, either by missing something serious or by creating anxiety and inappropriate supplementation. In the AKURA system, blood testing should be clinically supervised and interpreted by qualified professionals. The coach's role is to recognise when a question exists, to help the athlete arrive at the test in a standardised state, and to record the clinician's interpretation alongside the training data.

### Markers and why they are measured

Depending on the athlete and research question, relevant markers can include haemoglobin, haematocrit, ferritin, iron indices, B12, folate, vitamin D, thyroid markers, renal function and electrolytes. Each has a purpose, and each has confounders that a clinician must weigh.

|Marker|Why it may be measured|Note|
|Haemoglobin|Oxygen-carrying capacity; anaemia screening|Interpret with clinician|
|Haematocrit|Proportion of red cells; plasma-volume context|Interpret with clinician|
|Ferritin|Iron stores; fatigue or altitude planning|Rises with inflammation; clinician|
|Iron indices (serum iron, transferrin saturation)|Iron availability for red-cell production|Interpret with clinician|
|Vitamin B12 and folate|Red-cell formation; diet-related deficiency|Interpret with clinician|
|Vitamin D|Bone health and general status|Interpret with clinician|
|Thyroid markers|Unexplained fatigue or weight change|Interpret with clinician|
|Renal function and electrolytes|Kidney health; hydration and heat context|Interpret with clinician|

### Question first, test second

The list should not become a routine shopping list for every runner. Tests should be linked to a clinical or research question. "Why is this athlete's easy-run heart rate rising while pace falls?" is a question. "Let us check everything" is not. Question-led testing reduces cost, avoids false alarms from the many markers that will fall slightly outside a laboratory's reference range by chance, and makes interpretation sharper because the clinician knows what is being asked.

Standardisation also matters. Many markers vary with hydration, time of day, recent hard exercise, illness and menstrual cycle. Plasma volume can shift after heavy training or heat exposure, altering concentrations without any change in total red-cell mass. A clinician can only interpret a result well when the sampling conditions are known and consistent.

FIELD: Preparing an athlete for a clinically ordered blood test ; Confirm the clinical or research question with the supervising clinician ; Schedule a morning draw after an easy day or rest day, not within 24 hours of a hard or very long session ; Follow the clinician's instructions on fasting and supplements ; Record sleep, recent illness, menstrual-cycle day where relevant, and recent heat or altitude exposure ; Use the same laboratory for repeat tests where possible ; Store the report and clinician interpretation in separate AEI fields from the coach's notes

### Iron: the endurance athlete's recurring question

In endurance athletes, iron status is particularly relevant when investigating erythropoietic response, fatigue or unexplained performance change. Iron is needed to build new red cells, so an athlete heading to altitude with depleted stores may not gain the expected haematological benefit. Endurance training itself can increase iron losses and alter absorption, and dietary patterns common in India, including predominantly vegetarian diets, mean iron intake and bioavailability deserve attention. Female athletes face additional menstrual losses. Yet iron supplementation is not harmless and should never be started on guesswork; the decision belongs with a qualified clinician after appropriate testing.

Longitudinal records add a further layer of value. A single ferritin value tells a clinician relatively little; a series of values taken under similar conditions, alongside training load, diet notes and menstrual history, can reveal a slow decline long before it becomes a clinical problem. This is where AKURA's data discipline helps the medical team. The coach does not interpret the numbers, but by keeping clean, dated, well-annotated records, the coach gives the clinician the context needed to interpret them well. In Indian programmes where athletes may use different laboratories in different cities, recording the laboratory name, the method where reported and the reference range printed on each report is especially important, because values and ranges are not always directly comparable between laboratories.

Privacy also matters. Blood results are sensitive health information. They should be stored with the athlete's informed consent, shared only with those who need them, and never posted in group chats or displayed on team noticeboards. For minors, parental or guardian consent and the involvement of a paediatric clinician are essential.

MYTH: Every tired runner needs an iron tablet. || Fatigue has many causes, including under-recovery, poor sleep, low energy availability and illness. Iron should be assessed with appropriate tests and supplementation decided by a qualified clinician, not assumed by a coach.

KEY: A blood test is only as useful as the question behind it. Markers must be interpreted by a qualified clinician who knows the sampling conditions and the athlete's history.

COACH: When an athlete reports persistent fatigue or an unexplained drop in performance, first check training load, sleep and illness records, then refer to a doctor with a written summary. Do not suggest a diagnosis or a supplement; describe what you have observed and let the clinician decide what to test.

## 46. Measuring Haemoglobin Mass

Altitude training is often justified by its effect on red cells, so it is natural to reach for a blood test to see whether it "worked". The difficulty is that the most common blood value, haemoglobin concentration, answers a different question from the one being asked. This chapter explains why, and why the AKURA research rule is simple: use the measurement that actually answers the question.

### Concentration is not mass

Haemoglobin concentration from a standard blood sample is not identical to total haemoglobin mass. Concentration is the amount of haemoglobin per unit volume of blood; total haemoglobin mass is the absolute amount in the whole circulation. Plasma-volume changes can alter concentration without proportional changes in total Hb mass. When an athlete arrives at altitude, plasma volume often falls in the first days, so concentration rises even though no new red cells have been made. Conversely, heat acclimation and heavy endurance training typically expand plasma volume, diluting haemoglobin so that concentration may fall even while total mass is stable or increasing. An endurance athlete can look mildly "anaemic" on concentration while carrying a large, healthy total haemoglobin mass.

|Measure|What it describes|Main limitation|
|Haemoglobin concentration|Hb per unit blood volume|Changes with plasma volume|
|Haematocrit|Red-cell share of blood volume|Changes with plasma volume|
|Total haemoglobin mass|Absolute Hb in circulation|Needs specialist method|
|Ferritin|Iron stores for red-cell production|Inflammation raises it|

### Direct measurement: optimised CO-rebreathing

When the research objective is altitude response, direct Hb-mass measurement methods such as optimised carbon-monoxide rebreathing can provide a more specific measure. The method most widely used in sport was described by Schmidt and Prommer in 2005. In simple terms, the athlete rebreathes a small, precisely controlled dose of carbon monoxide for a short period; because carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin, the change in blood carboxyhaemoglobin after a known dose allows total haemoglobin mass to be calculated.

The method requires specialist equipment and safety procedures. Carbon monoxide is toxic, so dosing must be calculated carefully, exhaled and ambient levels monitored, and the procedure run by trained staff under qualified medical supervision. It is not a field test, and it must never be improvised. Even in expert hands it has a measurement error that must be known, which is why duplicate measurements or a well-documented laboratory typical error are important before claiming an altitude effect.

KEY: Haemoglobin concentration can change simply because plasma volume changes. If the question is whether red-cell mass increased, measure total haemoglobin mass with a validated method under medical supervision.

### Designing an altitude Hb-mass study

A useful altitude study measures total haemoglobin mass before the camp, towards the end of it, and after return, with iron status checked beforehand so that low stores do not limit the response. Timing, hydration and the laboratory's own typical error must be documented. The AEI record should store the raw values, the method, the laboratory, and whether the observed change exceeds measurement error, rather than only "Hb up" or "Hb down".

It also helps to understand why the distinction matters for performance. Total haemoglobin mass is closely related to the body's capacity to transport oxygen, and it tends to be higher in endurance-trained athletes. A training or altitude intervention that genuinely increases total mass may support a higher VO₂max, whereas a rise in concentration caused only by plasma-volume loss does not add oxygen-carrying capacity to the circulation as a whole. Confusing the two can lead to wrong conclusions: a coach may believe a camp was successful when it simply dehydrated the athlete, or may abandon a useful camp because concentration fell after plasma volume expanded on return.

For most Indian programmes, direct Hb-mass measurement will be available only through research collaborations or specialist centres. That is acceptable. The AKURA approach is not to demand every measure for every athlete, but to be honest about what each available measure can and cannot show. When total mass cannot be measured, the record should say so, and conclusions about red-cell adaptation should be framed cautiously.

FIELD: Outline for an altitude Hb-mass research block (clinically supervised) ; 3–4 weeks before the camp: clinician reviews iron status and advises on any action ; Baseline Hb-mass by optimised CO-rebreathing in an accredited or experienced laboratory, ideally duplicate ; Log altitude, days at altitude, training load, illness and sleep during the camp ; Repeat Hb-mass near the end of the camp using the same laboratory and protocol ; Repeat after return to assess retention ; Compare changes against the laboratory's typical error before drawing conclusions

COACH: If an athlete shows you a post-camp blood report with a higher haemoglobin concentration, do not celebrate or worry yet. Ask whether plasma volume, hydration or timing could explain it, and whether a total Hb-mass measure is available. Frame the result as a question for the medical team, not a verdict.

## 47. VO₂ Testing

VO₂max is the most famous number in endurance physiology, and for that reason it is also one of the most over-interpreted. A VO₂ test can provide maximal or submaximal oxygen-uptake information, but the value depends heavily on protocol, equipment calibration, athlete effort and whether the athlete reaches a valid maximal criterion. In AKURA, the VO₂ test is treated as a source of raw physiological data, of which the headline VO₂max is just one output.

### What makes a maximal value valid

A maximal test typically uses an incremental protocol on a treadmill until volitional exhaustion. The number reported depends on the gas analyser's calibration, the sampling and averaging interval, the treadmill grade and speed increments, the athlete's motivation and familiarity, and the environment. Traditional criteria for confirming a true maximum include a plateau in oxygen uptake despite increasing workload, a high respiratory exchange ratio, near-maximal heart rate and high post-test blood lactate. Midgley and colleagues reviewed these criteria in 2007 and showed that none is perfect, which is why many laboratories add a verification bout at a higher workload. A value obtained without clear criteria should be labelled "peak" rather than "max".

|Factor|Effect on the result|What to record|
|Analyser calibration|Systematic error in all values|Calibration time and gases|
|Averaging interval|Short intervals give higher peaks|Interval used (e.g. 30 s)|
|Protocol|Stage length and grade alter peak|Protocol ID and increments|
|Athlete effort|Early stop underestimates max|Criteria met, RPE, reason for stopping|
|Environment|Heat raises HR and lowers peak|Temperature and humidity|

### Submaximal testing: seeing adaptation beneath the ceiling

Submaximal testing can be especially useful for economy and fixed-workload comparisons. If the athlete uses less oxygen at the same speed after training, that can reveal an adaptation even when VO₂max is unchanged. Many well-trained athletes improve for years with little change in VO₂max; the improvement lies in running economy, in the fraction of VO₂max that can be sustained, and in durability. A submaximal test is also less demanding, so it can be repeated more often and with less disruption to training.

FIELD: Combined submax and max treadmill test outline (laboratory, qualified staff) ; Confirm medical clearance and analyser calibration ; 10 min warm-up at easy pace, 1% treadmill grade ; Submax phase: 3–4 stages of 4 min at fixed speeds below threshold, recording steady-state VO₂, HR and RPE in the last minute of each stage ; Short rest ; Max phase: incremental stages (e.g. grade or speed increase each minute) to volitional exhaustion ; Optional verification bout after recovery at a workload above the final stage ; Record criteria met, averaging interval, environment and the athlete's recent training and sleep

KEY: VO₂max is one output of a VO₂ test, not the whole test. Submaximal oxygen cost at fixed speeds often reveals adaptations that VO₂max hides.

### Field estimates and their limits

Many watches now report an estimated VO₂max from pace and heart-rate data. These estimates can be useful for tracking broad trends in a single athlete using the same device, but they are not measurements of oxygen uptake. They depend on the device's algorithm, the accuracy of heart-rate recording, terrain, heat and the athlete's recorded maximum heart rate. A hot, humid week in Chennai can lower a watch estimate without any change in true aerobic capacity, and a firmware update can shift every value overnight. AKURA therefore stores watch estimates in a separate field from laboratory measurements, labels them clearly as estimates, and never mixes the two in a single trend line. Where a laboratory test and a watch estimate disagree, the laboratory value, collected under a documented protocol, takes priority, while the watch trend can still be used to monitor week-to-week direction.

Safety is part of protocol too. A maximal test is a demanding effort, and athletes should be medically cleared, free from illness and supervised by staff trained in emergency procedures.

### Storing raw data

AEI should store raw workload and oxygen data, not only the headline number. The breath-by-breath or averaged export, the speed and grade at each stage, heart rate, respiratory exchange ratio and lactate samples allow later reanalysis, comparison across devices and detection of errors. A single headline VO₂max in a spreadsheet cannot be corrected, reprocessed or compared fairly with a test done on a different system; the raw data can.

Finally, remember that VO₂max is only one of several determinants of endurance performance. The fraction of VO₂max an athlete can sustain, their running economy and their durability over long distances often explain more of the difference between athletes of similar aerobic capacity. AEI treats VO₂ data as one input among these, not as a ranking of talent.

COACH: When an athlete brings a VO₂max number from a gym or clinic, ask for the protocol, the averaging method and the raw export. Use the submaximal stages to set a baseline for future comparisons, and be cautious about comparing numbers from different laboratories or watches.

## 48. Lactate Testing

Blood lactate has a reputation it does not deserve. For decades it was blamed for fatigue and soreness; in reality it is a metabolic intermediate that is produced and cleared continuously, and a useful window into how the body is coping with a given workload. Lactate testing can map the relationship between workload and blood lactate. Used well, it turns lactate from a mysterious number into a performance relationship: a curve showing how much work the athlete can do before the balance between production and clearance tips.

### Why protocol consistency is everything

Protocol consistency is essential: stage duration, sample timing, sampling site, analyser, warm-up and nutrition can all influence results. Short stages may not allow lactate to reach a steady state, shifting the curve to the right. A sample taken 30 seconds late after a stage may read differently from one taken immediately. Fingertip and earlobe samples can differ, portable analysers differ from one another, and sweat contamination on the skin can raise a reading. An athlete who has not eaten carbohydrate, or who trained hard the day before, may produce a lower lactate curve that looks like improved fitness but actually reflects depleted glycogen. Faude, Kindermann and Meyer reviewed the many lactate threshold concepts in 2009 and highlighted how different definitions and protocols produce different "thresholds" from the same data, which is exactly why the method must be fixed and documented.

|Variable|How it can distort results|Standardise by|
|Stage duration|Too short: no steady state|Fixed length, e.g. 4–5 min|
|Sampling site|Finger vs earlobe differ|Same site every test|
|Analyser|Devices read differently|Same analyser, check calibration|
|Pre-test nutrition|Low glycogen lowers lactate|Same meal timing and content|
|Prior training|Fatigue shifts the curve|Easy day before testing|
|Environment|Heat raises HR and lactate|Record and match conditions|

### An incremental step test outline

FIELD: Incremental lactate step test (track or treadmill, trained operator, hygienic sampling) ; Standardise the previous 24 hours: easy training, usual carbohydrate intake, same meal 2–3 h before ; 10–15 min easy warm-up, then take a resting or post-warm-up baseline sample ; Run 5–7 stages of 4–5 min each, starting well below marathon pace and increasing by a fixed step (e.g. 0.5–1.0 km/h or 10–15 s/km) ; At the end of each stage stop briefly (30 s or less), clean and dry the site, discard the first drop, sample, and record HR and RPE ; Stop when lactate rises steeply or the athlete reaches a pre-agreed limit ; Record temperature, humidity, surface, shoes, analyser and site ; Plot lactate and HR against speed

Sampling blood, even from a fingertip, must follow hygiene and sharps-disposal procedures, and should be done by a trained person. On a track, holding a steady pace is harder than on a treadmill, so use pacing lights, a pacer or clear lap splits.

### Asking the right question

A useful research design repeats the same test after an intervention. The question is not whether lactate is "high" or "low", but whether the athlete can sustain a higher workload at a comparable physiological response. If, after an eight-week block, the curve shifts to the right, so that the speed at a given lactate concentration or at a fixed rise above baseline is faster, the athlete has improved in a way that matters for racing. If only the peak lactate changed, that may reflect motivation, glycogen or the analyser more than fitness.

Interpretation also depends on context outside the curve. The same athlete may show a different curve in the hot season than in the cool season, because heat increases cardiovascular strain and can raise lactate at a given speed. Carbohydrate availability, recent racing, travel and illness all matter. That is why every lactate test in the AKURA system is stored with its full metadata, and why test-retest comparisons should ideally be made at a similar time of year, time of day and phase of training. When conditions cannot be matched, the difference should be noted and the conclusion softened.

It is also worth resisting the temptation to prescribe training from a single lactate number. Thresholds derived from different methods, such as a fixed concentration, a rise above baseline or a curve-fitting approach, can give quite different speeds from the same data. The value of the test lies less in a precise threshold and more in repeated, comparable curves that show whether the athlete's relationship between workload and lactate is moving in the right direction. For many coaches, the speed and heart rate at a fixed lactate level, tracked over months, are more useful than any single named threshold.

KEY: Lactate is not good or bad. The useful information lies in the relationship between workload and lactate, compared under identical conditions before and after training.

COACH: Choose one threshold definition and one protocol for your group and never change it mid-season. When comparing tests, look first at the speed and HR at a fixed lactate level, then check whether the change is larger than your test's typical error before adjusting training zones.

CHECK: If your athlete's lactate curve moved to the right, what other explanations besides improved fitness would you rule out first?

## 49. Running Economy Testing

Two runners with identical VO₂max can race very differently, and running economy is often the reason. Economy describes how much oxygen, or energy, an athlete needs to run at a given speed. Saunders and colleagues' 2004 review in Sports Medicine described how training, physiology, biomechanics, footwear and environment all influence it, and why it is one of the strongest distinguishing features among well-trained distance runners with similar aerobic capacity. For AKURA, economy is a key efficiency component of the AEI, but only if it is measured well.

### Standardising the test

Running economy testing requires a standardised speed, grade, footwear condition and steady-state collection period. Economy can be expressed as oxygen cost per unit distance at a given speed, for example millilitres of oxygen per kilogram per kilometre, which makes values at different speeds easier to compare. Some laboratories also express it as energy cost, which accounts for changes in fuel use via the respiratory exchange ratio.

The test must be submaximal: speeds should be comfortably below the lactate threshold so that oxygen uptake reaches a true steady state and anaerobic contribution is small. Shoes matter; a change from a worn trainer to a new carbon-plated racer can alter economy, so footwear must be recorded or held constant. Treadmill familiarisation matters too, because unfamiliar athletes often run less economically on their first visits.

FIELD: Running economy steady-state protocol (laboratory, calibrated gas analysis) ; Standardise the previous 24 hours, meal timing and caffeine ; Record body mass in running kit and shoe model and condition ; Familiarise the athlete with the treadmill if new ; 10 min warm-up at easy pace ; Run 3 stages of 5–6 min at fixed submaximal speeds below threshold, with treadmill grade set at 1% ; Average VO₂ over the final 2 min of each stage, confirming a steady state and RER below 1.0 ; Express economy as ml O₂ per kg per km at each speed ; Record HR, RPE, room temperature and humidity ; Repeat the identical protocol for follow-up tests

### Change versus noise

Small changes matter, but measurement error must be understood. If the observed change is smaller than the test's typical error, it should not be treated as a real adaptation. Running economy has a typical error of a few per cent in well-controlled laboratories, which is similar in size to the improvements that training or footwear might produce. That makes the logic of Hopkins's work essential: estimate the typical error from repeated tests, identify the smallest worthwhile change, and interpret an observed change in relation to both.

|Observed change vs noise|Interpretation|AEI confidence|
|Smaller than typical error|Cannot distinguish from noise|Low|
|Similar to typical error|Possible change, needs repeat|Moderate|
|Clearly larger than typical error and above smallest worthwhile change|Likely real and meaningful|High|

KEY: A 2% improvement in running economy means nothing if the test's typical error is 3%. Every economy result needs a confidence label based on known measurement error.

### Beyond the laboratory

Laboratory economy testing is not available to most Indian athletes. Field proxies, such as the heart rate at a fixed easy pace on a standard flat route, can offer a rough window into efficiency, but they mix economy with cardiovascular and thermal factors. A lower heart rate at the same pace may reflect better economy, a larger stroke volume, cooler weather or simply better sleep. AKURA therefore labels such measures as efficiency indicators rather than economy measurements, and interprets them cautiously and only when conditions are matched.

Body mass, footwear and surface also deserve attention in field comparisons. Because economy is often expressed relative to body mass, a change in weight can alter the number without any change in running mechanics. Shoes with different foam or plates, and surfaces from synthetic tracks to uneven roads, can all change the oxygen cost of a given speed. Recording these details is what allows a real improvement to be separated from a change in equipment or ground.

### Building measurement confidence into AEI

AEI should therefore include measurement confidence rather than pretending every change is meaningful. Each economy result can carry a confidence flag derived from the laboratory's typical error, the number of repeat trials and whether conditions were matched. A trend supported by several tests carries more weight than a single striking result.

Economy also changes with fatigue. An athlete may be economical in the first minutes of a fresh laboratory test yet lose efficiency late in a marathon. Where resources allow, repeating economy stages after a prolonged run can show how well economy is preserved, linking this chapter to the durability domain of the AEI.

COACH: If a new shoe or strength block seems to improve economy, ask for a repeat test before changing race plans. Report the result to the athlete as "probably improved", "unclear" or "no change", not as a precise percentage that suggests false certainty.

CHECK: Do you know the typical error of the economy or submax test you use? If not, how could you estimate it with your athletes over the next month?

## 50. ECG and Echocardiography

The endurance athlete's heart adapts to training, and those adaptations can look unusual to anyone unfamiliar with them. A slow resting heart rate, certain ECG patterns and a larger heart can all be normal consequences of training. Rarely, similar-looking findings signal disease that can be dangerous. This chapter sets out the AKURA position clearly: cardiac screening and diagnostic tests belong under qualified medical supervision, and the coach's job is to observe, record and refer.

### What the tests show

ECG can identify rhythm and electrical patterns; echocardiography can evaluate structure and function. A resting 12-lead ECG records the heart's electrical activity and can reveal rhythm disturbances and patterns associated with some inherited conditions. Echocardiography uses ultrasound to image the chambers, walls, valves and pumping function. Other investigations, such as exercise testing, ambulatory monitoring or cardiac MRI, are decided by cardiologists when needed.

|Test|What it evaluates|Who performs and interprets|
|12-lead resting ECG|Rhythm and electrical patterns|Trained staff; physician or cardiologist interprets|
|Echocardiography|Heart structure and function|Sonographer or cardiologist; cardiologist interprets|
|Exercise ECG|Response to exertion|Under medical supervision|
|Ambulatory monitoring|Rhythm over 24 h or longer|Ordered and read by physician|

### Athlete's heart or pathology

Endurance athletes may develop physiological cardiac adaptations, but interpretation must distinguish normal training-related findings from pathology. The international recommendations for electrocardiographic interpretation in athletes, published by Sharma and colleagues in 2017, grouped ECG findings into those considered normal in athletes, borderline findings and abnormal findings that warrant further evaluation. These criteria are tools for physicians trained to use them, and they must be applied with the athlete's age, sex, ethnicity, symptoms and family history in view.

A coach should not diagnose from a watch, ECG screenshot or isolated heart-rate value. Consumer devices can mis-detect rhythm, and a low resting heart rate on its own is common in trained runners. What a coach must never ignore are warning symptoms: chest pain or pressure, fainting or near-fainting especially during or just after exercise, unexplained breathlessness out of proportion to effort, palpitations or a racing irregular heartbeat, and a family history of sudden cardiac death at a young age. These require the athlete to stop training and be assessed by a doctor promptly.

Context is essential in the Indian setting. Many athletes come from regions where formal pre-participation screening is uncommon, and some may never have had an ECG before joining a structured programme. Family history is often under-reported because relatives' deaths may not have been investigated. A careful, respectful conversation at registration, supported by a standard questionnaire, is therefore an important first step. Where screening is offered, it should be organised through qualified clinicians with access to cardiology referral, and the athlete should understand what the tests can and cannot rule out.

It is also important to avoid the opposite error. Over-reading normal athletic adaptations can lead to unnecessary anxiety, repeated tests and lost training. The purpose of expert interpretation is to reassure where reassurance is appropriate and to investigate where it is not. Neither job belongs to a coach, but a coach who understands the difference will refer calmly and support the athlete through the process.

MYTH: A coach can clear an athlete by looking at their smartwatch ECG. || Consumer ECG features are limited and are not a cardiac screening. Only a qualified clinician can interpret cardiac tests and decide on fitness to train or compete.

KEY: Coaches observe and refer; clinicians interpret and clear. Any exertional chest pain, fainting or unexplained palpitations means stop and see a doctor.

### Recording clearance in the AKURA system

The research system should record medical clearance and clinician interpretation as separate fields. The clearance field states whether a qualified clinician has cleared the athlete for training or competition, when and with what restrictions; the interpretation field holds the clinician's written summary. Keeping these apart from coach observations prevents a coach's note such as "HR felt high today" from being mistaken for a medical finding, and makes it clear who is responsible for each judgement. Access to these records should be restricted and handled with the athlete's consent.

FIELD: AKURA cardiac referral pathway for coaches ; Ask about symptoms and family history at registration using a standard questionnaire ; If any warning symptom occurs, stop the session and do not resume training that day ; Refer the athlete to a doctor with a written, factual description of what happened ; Store the clinician's clearance and interpretation in separate, restricted fields ; Resume training only after documented clearance, following any restrictions

COACH: Keep a simple emergency plan for every training venue, including the location of the nearest automated external defibrillator and how to call for help. Encourage pre-participation screening organised by qualified clinicians, and never let an athlete talk their way out of a medical review after a fainting episode.

CHECK: Does your athlete record keep medical clearance separate from coaching notes? Could every coach in your group name the cardiac warning symptoms and the next step?

PART IX — ENVIRONMENT

PARTINTRO: India offers almost every endurance environment on one subcontinent: humid coastal cities, dry northern plains in summer, cool hill stations and high Himalayan terrain. The same session can therefore produce very different physiological strain depending on where and when it is run. This Part explains why temperature alone is not enough, how wet-bulb globe temperature captures the combined thermal load, how pace-HR decoupling can signal cardiac drift and heat strain, and why humidity deserves to be treated as a separate variable. The aim is simple: every major AEI session should carry its environmental context, so that fitness is never confused with weather.

## 51. WBGT and Heat Load

Temperature alone does not describe heat stress. A thermometer reading tells us how warm the air is, but the body gains and loses heat through several routes at once: through the air, through solar and ground radiation, through airflow over the skin and, most importantly for runners, through the evaporation of sweat. Humidity influences evaporative cooling, while radiant heat and wind can alter the total thermal environment. Wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is one commonly used environmental index that tries to capture this combined load in a single number.

### What WBGT combines

WBGT was developed for military training in the 1950s and is now widely used in occupational health and sport. Outdoors, it combines three readings: a natural wet-bulb temperature, which reflects the cooling power of evaporation and so responds strongly to humidity and airflow; a black-globe temperature, which reflects radiant heat from sun and surfaces; and ordinary dry-bulb air temperature. The wet-bulb component carries the greatest weight, which is why a humid day can have a high WBGT even when the air temperature does not look extreme.

|Component|What it reflects|Why it matters for runners|
|Natural wet-bulb temperature|Evaporative cooling potential|Humidity limits sweat evaporation|
|Black-globe temperature|Radiant heat from sun and ground|Direct sun adds heat load|
|Dry-bulb air temperature|Air temperature|Convective heat exchange|

Proper WBGT measurement uses a dedicated instrument placed in the conditions the athletes experience, not a phone app's city estimate. Values can differ markedly between a shaded park at dawn and an exposed road at mid-morning.

### Example categories and guidance

Many race-medicine and sports-medicine bodies use flag or category systems based on WBGT. The table below shows example categories in the style of the widely used American College of Sports Medicine flag system for endurance events. They are illustrative only: thresholds differ between organisations, sports, and levels of acclimatisation, and local sports-medicine and federation guidelines always apply.

|Example WBGT category|Illustrative risk|General guidance|
|Below 18°C|Low|Normal training; still hydrate and monitor|
|18–23°C|Moderate|Monitor closely; adjust pace and hydration|
|23–28°C|High|Reduce intensity and duration; extra cooling; watch vulnerable athletes|
|Above 28°C|Very high|Consider postponing or moving hard sessions; follow medical guidance|

Individual factors shift risk within any category. Athletes who are not yet heat-acclimatised, who are unwell, sleep-deprived or dehydrated, who are returning from injury, or who are running at high intensity for long durations can be at greater risk even in a moderate category. Young athletes and masters athletes may respond differently, and body size, clothing and fitness also matter. The category is a starting point for decisions, not a permission slip.

In practice, WBGT guidance should shape the whole session plan: when to start, how long to run, how hard to go, where to place fluid and shade, and who will watch for early signs of heat illness such as confusion, unsteady running, collapse or unusual behaviour. Exertional heat stroke is a medical emergency, and every hot-weather programme should have a clear plan for rapid recognition, cooling and medical help, developed with qualified sports-medicine professionals.

KEY: WBGT combines humidity, radiant heat and air temperature into one heat-load index. Category thresholds are guides that must be adapted to local medical and federation rules and to each athlete's acclimatisation.

### Logging environment in Indian training

For Indian endurance training, the environment should be logged whenever possible. A 60-minute run at the same pace can create very different physiological stress at 20°C and at 34°C with high humidity. Heart rate may be many beats higher, sweat loss much greater and perceived effort far harder, even though the watch shows the same pace. If AEI ignored this, it would read a hot-season plateau as lost fitness and a cool-season improvement as a breakthrough.

This is why AEI should carry an environmental context tag for major sessions and races: at minimum air temperature, relative humidity and time of day; ideally WBGT, sun or shade, and wind. With that context, the system can compare sessions run in similar conditions and adjust expectations for sessions that were not.

FIELD: Environmental logging for key sessions ; Measure at the training site within 15 minutes of the start, at athlete height, in the same sun or shade exposure as the route ; Record air temperature, relative humidity, WBGT if a meter is available, wind estimate, cloud cover and time ; Re-measure at the end of sessions longer than 60 minutes ; Tag the session with its environmental category ; Compare only sessions within similar categories when judging fitness trends

COACH: Schedule quality sessions in the coolest part of the day during hot months, and treat pace targets as flexible when WBGT is high: run by effort and heart rate instead. Make heat-illness recognition and a cooling plan part of every hot-season training camp.

CHECK: For your last five key sessions, could you state the temperature, humidity and time? How many of your "bad" sessions coincided with high heat load?

## 52. Pace-HR Decoupling

On a steady run, pace and heart rate usually travel together. As the run goes on, that partnership can loosen. If heart rate rises while pace remains constant, or pace falls while heart rate remains similar, the relationship may indicate cardiac drift, heat strain, dehydration, fatigue or pacing changes. Pace-HR decoupling measures this loosening, and it is one of the most useful field signals available to AKURA because it needs nothing more than a heart-rate strap and a GPS watch.

### Why the relationship drifts

During prolonged exercise, especially in the heat, heart rate tends to rise gradually even at a constant workload. Coyle and González-Alonso described this cardiovascular drift and linked it to rising body temperature, fluid loss and the increasing demand for skin blood flow, which together reduce stroke volume so that heart rate must climb to maintain output. Fatigue of the running muscles, glycogen depletion and subtle changes in running mechanics can add to the effect. A well-conditioned, well-hydrated athlete in cool weather typically shows little drift; the same athlete in humid heat may show a lot.

### Calculating decoupling

The most practical method compares the first half and the second half of a steady run. For each half, calculate an efficiency ratio: average speed divided by average heart rate. Decoupling is the percentage change in this ratio from the first half to the second half. A falling ratio means the athlete is getting less speed for each heartbeat as the run progresses.

FIELD: Pace-HR decoupling calculation ; Choose a steady run of at least 45–60 min on flat or consistent terrain, after a 10–15 min warm-up that is excluded from analysis ; Split the analysed portion into two equal halves by time ; For each half, compute average speed (e.g. metres per minute) and average HR ; Efficiency ratio = average speed ÷ average HR for each half ; Decoupling (%) = (first-half ratio − second-half ratio) ÷ first-half ratio × 100 ; Record temperature, humidity, fluid intake, terrain and any pace changes ; Store the result against the athlete's own history, not a universal cut-off

For example, suppose the first half averages 200 m/min at 140 bpm, giving a ratio of about 1.43, and the second half averages 200 m/min at 150 bpm, giving about 1.33. The decoupling is roughly 7%. Whether that is large depends on the conditions and on what is normal for this athlete.

|Half of run|Average speed|Average HR|Efficiency ratio|
|First half|200 m/min|140 bpm|1.43|
|Second half|200 m/min|150 bpm|1.33|
|Decoupling|—|—|About 7%|

### A signal, not a diagnosis

Decoupling is not a diagnosis. It is a signal. The athlete's hydration, temperature, terrain and effort must be considered. A hilly second half, a headwind, a stop at a traffic signal, a poorly fitting strap or a sunrise that turned a cool start into a hot finish can all create apparent decoupling without any change in fitness. Conversely, a runner who slowed down deliberately may show little decoupling despite significant strain.

Repeated standardised runs can make this signal more useful. Over time, AEI can learn an athlete's normal response instead of using a universal threshold. If an athlete usually shows 2–4% decoupling on a standard 60-minute run in the cool season, a value of 10% under similar conditions deserves attention, while the same 10% on a humid May morning may simply be expected.

A few technical points improve the quality of the signal. Chest straps usually give more reliable heart-rate data than wrist sensors during running, particularly in sweat and heat. Very short runs produce noisy ratios, so the analysed period should be long enough to reveal drift. Runs that include intervals, big hills or long stops are unsuitable. On undulating routes, some coaches use grade-adjusted pace, but even then the most reliable comparisons come from the same route under similar conditions. Finally, decoupling on a treadmill and outdoors are not directly comparable, because airflow, surface and cooling differ.

Decoupling can also help with race preparation. A marathon runner who shows modest decoupling on a long run at goal pace in conditions similar to race day has useful evidence of durability, while one who shows large decoupling may need to reconsider pace targets, fuelling or hydration.

KEY: Decoupling compares speed per heartbeat in the first and second halves of a steady run. Read it against the athlete's own history and the day's conditions, never as a stand-alone verdict.

COACH: Use one standard route and duration for decoupling checks every few weeks. Falling decoupling on matched days suggests improving aerobic durability; a sudden rise should prompt questions about sleep, illness, hydration and heat before any change to training.

CHECK: What decoupling does your athlete usually show on a standard steady run in cool conditions? How would you tell whether a higher value reflected fatigue or simply the weather?

## 53. Humidity Is Not Just “More Heat”

It is tempting to think of humidity as simply making a hot day feel hotter. Physiologically, it does something more specific. Humidity changes how effectively sweat evaporates. Two environments with the same air temperature can create very different thermal strain, because the runner's main cooling mechanism works well in one and poorly in the other.

### Evaporation: the runner's radiator

A running body produces a great deal of heat. In warm conditions, most of that heat must be removed by evaporating sweat from the skin. Evaporation depends on the difference between the water vapour pressure at the skin and in the surrounding air. In dry air, sweat evaporates readily and cools effectively. In humid air, the gradient is small, sweat drips rather than evaporates, and much of the fluid lost provides little cooling. The athlete sweats heavily, dehydrates faster and still heats up. Heart rate climbs as blood is diverted to the skin, and perceived effort rises.

|Condition|Sweat evaporation|Typical consequence|
|Hot and dry|Efficient|Cooling works; fluid loss high|
|Hot and humid|Limited|Core temperature rises faster; HR drift larger|
|Warm and very humid|Limited|Strain can exceed a hotter dry day|
|Cool and humid|Adequate at low heat production|Usually manageable|

### The coastal Indian runner

This matters particularly for coastal India. An athlete who can run comfortably at 32°C in dry conditions may struggle at a lower temperature when humidity is high. Runners in Chennai, Mumbai, Kochi, Visakhapatnam or Kolkata face long stretches of the year when the air is warm and saturated, and early mornings may be the most humid part of the day even though they are the coolest. An athlete who moves from a dry inland city to the coast, or travels to a humid race, can find familiar paces unexpectedly hard.

Heat-index or apparent-temperature numbers reported by weather apps attempt to combine temperature and humidity into a single feels-like value. They can be helpful as a quick warning, but they were not designed for athletes producing large amounts of heat, and they do not include radiant load or wind at the training site. For AKURA, the raw temperature and humidity values, plus WBGT where available, are more useful than a single feels-like figure, because they allow the analysis to separate the contributions of each variable.

Clothing and practical choices also interact with humidity. Light, breathable kit, shaded routes, earlier start times and regular cooling with water or ice can reduce strain, but none of them fully overcomes the physics of limited evaporation. Humid conditions therefore call for honest adjustment of expectations rather than simply trying harder.

MYTH: If the temperature is lower, the run will be easier. || A warm, very humid day can impose more thermal strain than a hotter, drier one, because humidity limits sweat evaporation, the body's main cooling route during running.

### Designing heat-acclimation research

Heat-acclimation research should therefore distinguish temperature, humidity and total heat stress rather than treating them as one variable. An acclimation block in dry heat may not fully prepare an athlete for humid heat, and vice versa. Consensus recommendations on training and competing in the heat, such as those led by Racinais and colleagues in 2015, emphasise matching acclimatisation to the expected competition environment where possible. For AKURA, that means recording air temperature, relative humidity and, where available, WBGT separately, so that the analysis can ask whether an athlete's response is driven by heat, by humidity, or by their combination.

FIELD: Humid-heat readiness check ; Choose a standard 45–60 min easy run performed in both drier and more humid conditions over several weeks ; Record air temperature, relative humidity, WBGT if available and time of day ; Weigh the athlete before and after (minimal clothing, towel-dried) to estimate sweat loss, adjusting for fluid taken ; Calculate pace-HR decoupling for each run ; Compare decoupling, sweat loss and RPE between drier and more humid days at similar temperatures ; Use the results to individualise pacing and hydration plans for humid races

Acclimatisation itself is specific and reversible. Adaptations such as an expanded plasma volume, earlier and greater sweating, and a lower heart rate at a given workload develop over roughly one to two weeks of repeated heat exposure and fade if exposure stops. Recording the type of heat an athlete has trained in, and for how long, allows AEI to judge how prepared they are for a particular race environment.

KEY: Temperature, humidity and total heat stress are different variables. Record them separately so AEI can tell which one is limiting the athlete.

COACH: Before a humid race, rehearse race-pace segments in similar humidity and adjust targets by effort and heart rate. Plan fluid and cooling strategies with a qualified professional where possible, and remind athletes that heavy sweating in humidity is not a sign of efficient cooling.

CHECK: Does your training log record humidity separately from temperature? Which of your athletes struggle most on humid days, and how would you test whether humidity rather than temperature is the main cause?
