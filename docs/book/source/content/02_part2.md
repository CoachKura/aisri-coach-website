PART II — THE HUMAN ENDURANCE ENGINE

PARTINTRO: Before we can measure an endurance athlete, we have to understand what we are measuring. Part II opens the engine. It follows oxygen from the air of a Chennai beach road or an Ooty hillside into the lungs, across into blood, around the body on haemoglobin, through the heart and into the working muscle, where mitochondria turn fuel into movement. Each chapter takes one link of that chain, explains its physiology in plain language, separates evidence from myth, and shows how it becomes a variable inside the AKURA Endurance Index. The lesson throughout is the same: no single number is the athlete.

## STORY: The Boy from Mandwa

Mandwa is a village near Ashti in Beed district, in the Marathwada region of Maharashtra, a part of the Deccan where the land is dry for much of the year and the monsoon is watched anxiously. Avinash Sable was born there on 13 September 1994, the son of farmers. The detail that every profile of him repeats is the road to school. It was 6 kilometres away, and he ran.

It is tempting to stop the story there, at the image of a boy on a dusty road, and declare that the road explains everything. It does not. The boy from Mandwa did not become a runner on that road. He became a soldier first.

### The soldier

After Class 12, Sable joined the Army and was posted to 5 Mahar Regiment. His postings took him to the Siachen region, to Rajasthan and to Sikkim. In one career he met some of the harshest environments on the subcontinent: the cold of Siachen and the desert heat of Rajasthan, where temperatures can reach 50 °C. None of it was a training plan. It was service.

Running entered his life through Army cross country in 2015. Two years later, an Army coach named Amrish Kumar, himself a former South Asian Games steeplechase champion, spotted him and moved him into the 3000 m steeplechase, the event that asks a distance runner to clear 28 barriers and 7 water jumps while racing at close to full aerobic capacity.

### The record that fell ten times

The Indian steeplechase record then belonged to Gopal Saini: 8:30.88, set at the Asian Championships in Tokyo in 1981. It had stood for 37 years. In September 2018, at the Open National Championships in Bhubaneswar, Sable broke it with 8:29.80.

What followed was one of the most sustained assaults on a national record in Indian athletics. In March 2019 he lowered it to 8:28.94 at the Federation Cup in Patiala. At the 2019 World Championships in Doha he ran 8:25.23 in the heats and then, after an appeal by the Athletics Federation of India got him into the final, 8:21.37 there, finishing 13th. At the Tokyo Olympics in 2021 he ran 8:18.12 in his heat, a national record that still was not enough for a place in the final. In 2022 came 8:16.21 at the Indian Grand Prix, 8:12.48 at the Rabat Diamond League and then, at the Commonwealth Games in Birmingham, 8:11.20 and a silver medal. He finished just 0.05 seconds behind Kenya's Abraham Kibiwot. It was India's first Commonwealth medal in the event and the first by a non-Kenyan since 1994.

The tenth record came on 7 July 2024 at the Paris Diamond League: 8:09.91.

Along the way he did things off the barriers too. In November 2020 he ran 1:00:30 at the Delhi Half Marathon, the first Indian under 61 minutes, and that remains the official national record. In 2022 he ran 13:25.65 for 5000 m, breaking a record Bahadur Prasad had set in Birmingham in 1992, and in 2023 he improved it to 13:19.30. At the 2023 Asian Games in Hangzhou he won steeplechase gold in a Games record of 8:19.50 and added silver in the 5000 m.

### Coaches, and a change of air

The coaching behind those numbers was not a straight line. At the national camp, Sable worked with the Belarusian coach Nikolai Snesarev, who had guided a generation of Indian distance runners. In 2018 Sable left him and returned to Amrish Kumar, and it was under Amrish that he ran 8:28.94 in 2019. Snesarev left the Athletics Federation of India in February 2019 in what were reported as acrimonious circumstances, with Sable's return to his Army coach reported as one factor. Snesarev was later reappointed and died in March 2021.

From 2022, Sable trained with the American coach Scott Simmons in Colorado Springs, at around 1,800 metres, as part of a camp that the federation and the government's sports schemes funded. Before the 2023 World Championships in Budapest he trained at altitude in Colorado Springs and in St Moritz in Switzerland, alongside Paul Chelimo. In July 2024, before the Paris Olympics, he was in St Moritz again.

QUOTE: His adaptation upon his return to altitude this year was almost instant. — Scott Simmons, to Hindustan Times

That sentence, casual as it sounds, is a physiological claim. What exactly adapted? His breathing? His blood volume? His red-cell mass? How was "instant" measured, and against what baseline? Hold on to those questions. They are the questions of this whole Part.

In Paris in August 2024 Sable became the first Indian to reach the Olympic steeplechase final, finishing 11th in 8:14.18. In 2025 at Gumi, South Korea, he won the Asian Championships title in 8:20.92, India's first men's steeplechase gold at the championships in 36 years.

### The knee

Then the engine met its limit, not in the heart or lungs but in a joint. At the Monaco Diamond League in July 2025, Sable injured the anterior cruciate ligament and meniscus of his right knee and needed surgery. He missed the World Championships in Tokyo that September, and in 2026 he missed the Commonwealth Games in Glasgow. He did much of his rehabilitation and rebuilding at the SAI Southern Centre in Bengaluru.

He has been candid about the comeback. "I think I might have rushed my return a bit, which caused the injury to recur," he told Olympics.com. His first steeplechase back, at the Indian Athletics Series final in September 2026, was 8:32.39, slower than the record he had first broken eight years earlier. Weeks later, at the Asian Games in Aichi-Nagoya, he ran 8:21.67, his best of the season, and finished fourth. He was entered in the 5000 m too, but did not start.

### A window into the engine

Why begin a Part about physiology with one man's career? Because a career like this is an experiment run in public, even if most of the measurements were never published.

Look at what the record shows. An athlete who discovered serious running only in his twenties, after joining the Army, and who then lowered a national record by nearly twenty-one seconds in six years. An athlete whose results moved when his coaching, his training base and his exposure to altitude changed, and whose coach believed his body responded to that altitude almost immediately. An athlete who ran fast from 5000 m to the half marathon, which says something about both his aerobic capacity and how economically he could run. And an athlete whose progress was stopped not by oxygen but by tissue, a reminder that the endurance engine has a chassis as well as a fuel line.

Each of those observations points to a link in a chain. The heart that pumps the blood. The blood that carries oxygen, and the plasma it floats in. The red cells that altitude may or may not multiply. The ceiling of oxygen uptake, and the economy that decides how much of it each stride costs. The mitochondria that burn the fuel, the lactate that moves between fibres, the threshold that marks how hard a runner can go for how long, and the glycogen that decides how long the fast running can last.

Nobody can yet explain Avinash Sable's career with a single number, and nobody should try. But every coach can learn to ask better questions of the next boy who runs 6 kilometres to school, or the next soldier who turns up at a cross-country race. To ask those questions well, we need to open the engine and look at it one link at a time, starting with the chain itself.

## 7. The Endurance System as a Chain

In the last week of September 2026, at the Asian Games in Aichi-Nagoya, Gulveer Singh did something that looks, on paper, like the work of three different athletes. He won silver in the 10,000 m in 28:29.38. He won silver in the 1500 m in 3:36.72. And on 29 September he took bronze in the 5000 m. Three medals, from a race that lasts well under four minutes to one that lasts nearly half an hour, won by a soldier from a farming family in Aligarh district who has said he "truly learned about competitive running only after joining the army." A 1500 m leans heavily on speed and anaerobic power. A 10,000 m depends on the steady, relentless delivery and use of oxygen. Yet it was one heart, one bloodstream and one set of muscles doing all of it. To understand how, we have to stop thinking of fitness as a single quality and start seeing it as a chain.

Distance running is, at its core, an oxygen-and-energy delivery problem expressed through movement. A marathon runner leaving the Marina at dawn is solving that problem thousands of times a minute. Air must reach the lungs; oxygen must cross into the blood; haemoglobin must carry it; the heart must circulate that blood; the working muscle must receive the oxygen; mitochondria must use it, together with carbohydrate and fat, to generate ATP; and finally the athlete must convert that chemical energy into forward motion with as little waste as possible. Every one of those steps is a link. Performance is what happens when all the links work together under the stress of pace, distance, heat and fatigue.

### Following the oxygen

It helps to picture the chain in order, because each link has its own physiology, its own measurement and its own way of failing.

|Link|What it does|Typical measure|Common confounder|
|Ventilation and lungs|Moves air and exchanges gas|Ventilation, SpO₂, spirometry|Altitude, airway conditions|
|Blood|Carries oxygen on haemoglobin|Hb mass, [Hb], ferritin|Plasma volume, hydration|
|Heart|Pumps blood to tissues|HR, stroke volume, cardiac output|Heat, dehydration, fatigue|
|Muscle and mitochondria|Extracts and uses oxygen|Lactate, muscle oxygenation, biopsy|Glycogen, training state|
|Mechanics|Turns energy into speed|Running economy, cadence, GCT|Footwear, surface, fatigue|

The table is a simplification. In the living athlete these links interact continuously: a change in plasma volume alters the heart's filling, which alters stroke volume, which alters oxygen delivery, which alters how hard the muscle must work at a given pace. That interdependence is exactly why the chain metaphor is useful. It reminds us that the body does not have a single "fitness" dial.

### Weak links and false promises

A limitation at any link can influence performance. An athlete with low iron stores may struggle to build haemoglobin; an athlete with poor mechanics may waste oxygen on vertical bounce; an athlete who has not practised race fuelling may run out of carbohydrate at 32 km regardless of how strong the heart is. In each case the weak link sets the ceiling on the day.

The opposite is also true, and it is where many coaching myths begin. Improving one link does not guarantee an improvement in race time. Plasma expansion after a block of heat training may increase stroke volume, yet the athlete still needs economical mechanics and sustainable metabolic control to turn that extra blood flow into faster kilometres. A higher VO₂max measured in a laboratory is encouraging, but if the athlete's economy has worsened because of a new shoe or an injury compensation, the race result may not move. The chain is only as useful as its weakest working link at the moment of competition.

KEY: Endurance performance is a chain of linked systems, from air to ATP to forward motion. Strengthening one link helps only if it is, or becomes, the link that limits the athlete.

MYTH: One magic variable, such as VO₂max or haemoglobin, explains why some runners are faster. || Performance emerges from several interacting systems; similar values in one link can coexist with very different race results because other links differ.

### Why AEI is a profile, not a single dial

This is the reason the AKURA Endurance Index avoids a single-variable model. AEI is designed as a multidimensional profile with a performance anchor. The profile records the state of several links, such as cardiovascular response, blood markers where available, economy, threshold and fuelling behaviour. The anchor is real performance: a time trial, a race or a standardised field test. The profile helps explain the anchor; the anchor keeps the profile honest. If the physiology improves but performance does not, AEI should flag the mismatch rather than hide it.

For Indian coaches this matters practically. Many squads will have access to heart-rate monitors and GPS watches but not to carbon-monoxide rebreathing or laboratory gas analysis. A chain model lets the coach be explicit about which links are measured, which are estimated and which are unknown, instead of pretending that a watch's single "fitness score" captures the whole athlete.

The chain also has a time dimension. Some links adapt within days, such as plasma volume at the start of a training block or after the first week of heat exposure. Others, such as mitochondrial content and capillary density, build over weeks and months. Red-cell mass responds over weeks and depends on iron and the stimulus. Mechanics and economy may take years of consistent running and strength work. A coach who expects every link to respond on the same schedule will misread the data. AEI therefore records not only what was measured, but when, so that fast and slow adaptations can be separated and a short-term change is not mistaken for a long-term one.

COACH: When an athlete stalls, walk the chain before changing the plan. Ask which link is most likely limiting now: sleep and recovery, iron and blood, heat load, mechanics, threshold or fuelling. Change one major variable at a time so you can see what actually moved.

## 8. The Heart: Pump, Not Just Heart Rate

Two friends leave the Besant Nagar beach road together at six on a May morning, matched stride for stride at 5:30 per kilometre. The sea breeze has not arrived yet. The air is thick, the kind of Chennai morning when a cotton vest is wet before the first kilometre is done. Forty minutes later they compare watches. One shows an average of 148 beats per minute. The other shows 163. Same pace, same road, same sun, and a fifteen-beat difference. The second runner is worried: is his heart weaker? The first is quietly pleased: is he fitter? Neither conclusion follows. The number on the wrist counts how often the heart beats. On its own, it says nothing about how much blood leaves the heart with each beat, how much of that blood is being sent to the skin to shed heat, or how much oxygen the leg muscles are pulling out of it. For that, we need to see the heart as what it really is: a pump.

Heart rate is the most familiar number in endurance sport. It sits on every wrist, it is cheap to record and it responds quickly to effort, heat and stress. It is a genuinely useful field signal. But it is not the heart's entire story. The heart is a pump, and what matters for oxygen delivery is how much blood it moves each minute, not simply how often it beats.

### Cardiac output and the Fick principle

Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pumps per minute. It equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume, the volume ejected with each beat. The oxygen the body consumes is then described by the Fick principle: oxygen uptake equals cardiac output multiplied by the difference in oxygen content between arterial and mixed venous blood. In words, the body's oxygen use depends on how much blood is delivered and how much oxygen the tissues take out of each litre.

|Quantity|Formula or meaning|Illustrative range (varies widely)|
|Cardiac output (CO)|HR × stroke volume|About 5 L/min at rest; can exceed 30 L/min in elite endurance athletes at maximum|
|Stroke volume (SV)|Blood ejected per beat|Often 60–100 mL at rest; larger in trained hearts|
|a–vO₂ difference|Arterial minus venous O₂ content|Rises from about 50 mL/L at rest to well over 100 mL/L in hard exercise|
|VO₂ (Fick)|CO × a–vO₂ difference|Whole-body oxygen uptake|

These values are illustrative only; they differ with body size, sex, training status, posture and measurement method. The principle, however, is robust. Two athletes can share the same heart rate at the same pace while delivering very different amounts of oxygen, because one has a larger stroke volume or extracts more oxygen at the muscle.

KEY: Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, and oxygen uptake = cardiac output × oxygen extraction. Heart rate is only one of the terms.

### Why the same pace feels different

This explains a common coaching observation. During endurance training an athlete may improve performance while running at a similar heart rate, because more blood is pumped per beat or because the muscles extract oxygen more effectively. Conversely, the same pace can feel very different on a cool Bengaluru morning and a humid Chennai evening.

Heat can reduce effective stroke volume in two ways. First, blood is shifted towards the skin to release heat, so a larger share of cardiac output is diverted away from working muscle and less blood returns to fill the heart. Second, sweating reduces plasma volume, which again lowers filling. To maintain cardiac output with a smaller stroke volume, heart rate rises. Physiologists describe the gradual upward drift of heart rate during prolonged exercise as cardiovascular drift, and heat and dehydration make it larger. Endurance training and heat acclimation can improve plasma volume and cardiac function, helping to maintain circulation under the same conditions.

MYTH: A lower heart rate at the same pace always means the athlete is fitter. || It often does, but it can also reflect cooler weather, better hydration, a different course or even a sensor error. Context is required before interpreting the change.

### Recording heart rate properly in AEI

Because heart rate responds to so many inputs, AEI should store HR together with pace, temperature, humidity when available, body mass change and perceived effort, rather than treating HR as a standalone score. A 150 bpm run at 5:00 per km in 24 °C is not the same physiological event as a 150 bpm run at the same pace in 32 °C with high humidity. Stored with context, the two sessions become comparable; stored alone, they mislead.

Wrist-based optical sensors are convenient but can be inaccurate during intervals, in heavy sweat or with loose straps. Chest straps are usually more reliable for training analysis. Where exact stroke volume or cardiac output measurement is needed, that is laboratory or clinical work and should be done by qualified staff. Any unusual symptom such as chest pain, fainting or palpitations during exercise is a medical matter for a doctor, not a coaching variable.

FIELD: Standardised submaximal HR check ; choose a flat, familiar 2–3 km loop ; warm up 10 minutes easy ; run 3 × 1 km at a fixed pace (for example marathon pace) with 1 minute standing rest ; record average HR of the last 30 seconds of each rep, air temperature, humidity, time of day and RPE ; weigh the athlete before and after in minimal clothing ; repeat every 2–4 weeks under similar conditions.

COACH: Do not change training zones on the basis of a single hot-day heart-rate reading. Look for trends in HR at fixed pace across several comparable sessions, and always note the weather and the athlete's sleep and hydration beside the number.

## 9. The Lungs and the Oxygen Myth

In Maroda, a village in Pauri Garhwal about 1,400 metres up in the Uttarakhand hills, a farmer's daughter used to run the hill roads with young men training for the Army's physical tests. "I decided to run with them ... They never slowed down for me," Ankita Dhyani later told ESPN. She went on to the national centre in Bhopal, to the Paris Olympics and, in August 2025, to a national record in the 2000 m steeplechase. Her coach put part of her strength down to geography: "because she was running in Uttarakhand, she had already been doing a lot of high-altitude training." It is an interesting sentence, mostly for what people tend to hear in it. Mountain villages, the popular story goes, have thinner air, and people who train in thin air grow bigger lungs. Almost every part of that belief needs checking. The air in Maroda, in Ooty and on Marina Beach contains the same fraction of oxygen. What differs is something else entirely.

Few ideas in endurance sport are repeated as confidently, or as inaccurately, as "there is less oxygen at altitude" and "hill running builds bigger lungs". Both contain a grain of truth wrapped in a misunderstanding. Getting the physics and physiology right matters, because a coach who misunderstands the stimulus will misjudge the dose.

### Percentage versus pressure

The percentage of oxygen in normal atmospheric air is approximately 20.9% at sea level and remains approximately the same at altitude. The air in Ooty or Kodaikanal contains the same proportion of oxygen as the air in Chennai. What changes substantially with altitude is barometric pressure. Because the total pressure is lower, the partial pressure of oxygen falls, and it is partial pressure that drives oxygen from the lungs into the blood. A lower driving pressure reduces how readily oxygen loads onto haemoglobin, particularly during hard exercise when blood moves quickly through the lung capillaries.

At moderate altitudes of around 2,000–2,500 m, inspired oxygen pressure is roughly a quarter lower than at sea level. The body responds within minutes by breathing more, and over days to weeks with further adaptations. Performance in longer events is usually reduced at altitude, particularly on arrival.

MYTH: There is less oxygen in the air at altitude. || The fraction of oxygen stays about 20.9%. The barometric pressure falls, so the partial pressure of oxygen, and therefore the pressure pushing oxygen into the blood, is lower.

KEY: Altitude is a pressure stimulus, not a percentage stimulus. The same 20.9% oxygen at lower pressure means a lower partial pressure of oxygen and less driving force for gas exchange.

### What hill running really trains

Hill running can make breathing harder because ventilation rises with workload, and it rises further at altitude. Repeated training can strengthen the respiratory muscles and improve tolerance to high ventilation. Hills also load the calves, quadriceps and hip extensors, and they can be a valuable tool for strength and economy. But ordinary hill running should not be described as automatically increasing lung size. In healthy adults, lung dimensions are largely set by body size and development, and training does not reliably enlarge them. What training changes more readily is how the athlete uses the lungs: breathing pattern, respiratory muscle endurance and the sensation of breathlessness at a given work rate.

In healthy, well-trained sea-level runners the lungs are often not the primary limiter of oxygen transport, although some highly trained athletes do show a fall in arterial oxygen saturation during very intense exercise. That phenomenon is individual and should be measured, not assumed.

### Four things that are often confused

The useful distinction is between lung structure, ventilation, gas exchange and systemic oxygen transport. These are related, but they are not the same measurement.

|Concept|Meaning|How it may be assessed|
|Lung structure|Size and mechanics of the lungs|Spirometry, lung volumes (clinical)|
|Ventilation|Volume of air moved per minute|Gas analysis during exercise|
|Gas exchange|Oxygen moving from alveoli into blood|Arterial saturation (SpO₂ as a rough field proxy)|
|Systemic O₂ transport|Oxygen delivered to tissues|Hb mass, cardiac output, VO₂|

A pulse oximeter reading at rest in Kodaikanal tells you something about gas exchange under those conditions. It does not tell you lung size, and it does not tell you how much oxygen is being delivered to the calf muscle at race pace. Keeping these categories separate prevents a common error: using one measurement to make claims about another.

For Indian athletes, air quality in some cities during certain seasons is also a relevant respiratory stressor. Coaches may reasonably move sessions to cleaner times or locations, but any persistent breathing symptom, wheeze or exercise-induced cough should be assessed by a clinician rather than trained through.

The body's early response to altitude is itself informative. Resting ventilation rises, resting heart rate is often higher for the first days, sleep can be disturbed and plasma volume may fall, which temporarily raises haemoglobin concentration without any new red cells being made. These early changes are responses to reduced oxygen pressure, not evidence that the lungs have grown or that the blood has already adapted. Distinguishing the first-week response from genuine longer-term adaptation is one of the reasons AKURA insists on repeated, standardised measurement at altitude rather than a single reading taken on the second morning of a camp.

COACH: Use hills for strength, economy and controlled intensity, and describe them honestly. If you take a squad to Ooty or Kodaikanal, expect paces to feel harder at first, reduce intensity for the first days, and record SpO₂, resting HR, sleep and RPE rather than assuming lungs are "growing".

## 10. Haemoglobin Mass

Sawan Barwal's Project 2:09 group normally spends about four months, from November to February, in the Nilgiris around Ooty and Wellington, then comes down to Bengaluru for final preparation. "For Sawan, most of the time he was staying in high altitude," his coach Ajith Markose told The Bridge. Four months of sleeping in thinner air is a large dose, far larger than the weekend hill trips many club runners take. A reader might expect, somewhere, a neat pair of numbers: how many grams of haemoglobin he carried in October, and how many in February. If such measurements were made, they are not in the public record. More broadly, the research behind this book could find no peer-reviewed study of Indian athletes' blood adaptations at Ooty at all. That is a remarkable gap, because the most important blood number for an endurance runner is not the one printed on an ordinary laboratory report.

Haemoglobin is the iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that binds oxygen. Each gram of fully saturated haemoglobin can carry roughly 1.34 mL of oxygen, so the total amount of haemoglobin in the body sets a large part of the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. When the research question is oxygen-carrying capacity, haemoglobin mass, the total grams of haemoglobin circulating in the body, is more informative than haemoglobin concentration alone.

### Concentration is a ratio, mass is an amount

A routine blood test reports haemoglobin concentration, usually in grams per decilitre. That number is a ratio: haemoglobin divided by the volume of blood it is dissolved in. Concentration can rise because red-cell mass increases, because plasma volume falls, or both. It can fall because red-cell mass decreases, because plasma volume expands, or both. A single concentration value cannot tell which of these happened.

|Scenario|Hb mass|Plasma volume|Hb concentration|
|Red-cell production after altitude|Up|Unchanged|Up|
|Dehydration after a hot long run|Unchanged|Down|Up (apparent)|
|Heat acclimation|Usually unchanged (short blocks)|Up|Down (apparent)|
|Altitude plus heat block|Up|Up|Could be unchanged|
|Iron deficiency, blood loss|Down|Variable|Usually down|

The third and fourth rows are especially important for AKURA's heat-based research. An athlete returning from a Chennai summer training block may show a lower haemoglobin concentration, not because oxygen-carrying capacity has worsened but because plasma volume has expanded. Without a measure of mass or volume, a coach could misread a positive adaptation as a problem.

KEY: Haemoglobin concentration is a ratio that changes with plasma volume. Haemoglobin mass is the total amount, and is the better marker of oxygen-carrying capacity.

MYTH: A higher haemoglobin value on a blood report always means better endurance capacity. || A higher concentration can simply reflect dehydration or reduced plasma volume. Total haemoglobin mass, interpreted with performance, is the more meaningful variable.

### How haemoglobin mass is measured

In research and elite sport, haemoglobin mass is commonly measured with the optimised carbon-monoxide rebreathing method, in which the athlete breathes a small, controlled dose of carbon monoxide and the change in carboxyhaemoglobin is used to calculate total haemoglobin. The method, developed and refined by Schmidt and Prommer and others, has good reliability in experienced hands. It must be performed by trained staff using established safety procedures; it is not a do-it-yourself field test.

Typical haemoglobin mass is higher in endurance-trained athletes than in untrained people when expressed per kilogram of body mass, and it differs between men and women and among individuals. Values should be interpreted relative to the athlete's own baseline rather than a single universal norm.

### Altitude and the 1% rule

Altitude exposure can stimulate erythropoietin (EPO) and, over weeks, erythropoiesis, the production of new red blood cells. A meta-analysis by Gore and colleagues, published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in 2013, pooled altitude studies that used carbon-monoxide rebreathing and estimated an average haemoglobin-mass increase of about 1.1% per 100 hours of exposure. The same analysis also showed substantial individual variability, and the authors estimated a wide prediction interval for any individual athlete's response.

Therefore "1% per 100 hours" should be treated as a research estimate from particular protocols, typically natural or simulated altitude of moderate elevation with sufficient daily hours and adequate iron, not as an athlete guarantee. Some athletes gain more, some gain little or nothing, and measurement error around a single test is not trivial. For an Indian squad considering a camp in Ooty or Kodaikanal, the practical translation is to calculate the expected hours of exposure, check iron status beforehand under medical supervision, and measure rather than assume the response.

FIELD: Planning an altitude Hb-mass study ; record baseline performance test and, if available, Hb mass by CO rebreathing ; obtain clinician-supervised iron studies 4–6 weeks before ; log daily hours at altitude, sleep altitude and training load ; retest Hb mass within a few days of descent ; repeat the performance test under standardised conditions ; interpret change against the method's known measurement error.

It is worth adding a simple calculation to show why mass matters. If an athlete has 900 g of haemoglobin and each gram, fully saturated, binds about 1.34 mL of oxygen, the blood can carry roughly 1.2 litres of oxygen at any moment. A 3% increase in haemoglobin mass would add roughly 36 mL of carrying capacity, regardless of whether the concentration on a lab report moved. These figures are illustrative, not targets, but they explain why researchers prefer to measure the total amount directly when they want to understand oxygen transport, and why concentration alone can hide or exaggerate genuine change.

COACH: Treat any Hb-mass gain as a potential that must still be converted into performance. Pair every blood measurement with a standard performance test, and resist announcing a camp as "successful" until both have been checked.

## 11. Plasma Volume

It is May in Chennai, and the long run starts at 4:45 a.m. to beat the sun. It does not beat the humidity. By the tenth kilometre along the East Coast Road, the runners in a small club group have stopped talking. By the twentieth, their shirts are so heavy that one of them wrings his out beside a tea stall. Back at the car, the coach, who has started carrying a bathroom scale in the boot, weighs everyone. One runner is 1.8 kg lighter than when he started, despite drinking at every stop. Most of that loss is water that left his body as sweat, and some of it was drawn from the liquid part of his blood. Three weeks later, running the same route at the same pace, the same runner finishes with a lower heart rate and says the run felt easier. Nothing about his heart or lungs has visibly changed. Part of the explanation may be flowing through his veins.

Blood is not only red cells. Roughly half or more of it, depending on the individual, is plasma, the liquid portion of blood made mostly of water, together with proteins, electrolytes and other solutes. Plasma is easy to overlook because it does not carry much oxygen itself, yet it plays a central role in endurance performance and, for athletes training in India's heat, may be one of the most responsive parts of the whole system.

### What plasma does for the endurance athlete

Expansion of plasma volume increases total blood volume. A larger blood volume can improve venous return, the flow of blood back to the heart, which helps fill the ventricles more completely before each beat. Better filling supports stroke volume, and a better-maintained stroke volume supports cardiovascular stability during long efforts. Plasma volume also contributes to thermoregulation: there is more fluid available both to send blood to the skin for cooling and to sustain sweating, without starving the working muscles or the heart.

Heat acclimation is particularly relevant because sweating and repeated thermal stress stimulate fluid-regulatory adaptations. Repeated exposure to exercise in the heat promotes retention of sodium and water and an increase in plasma proteins that help hold fluid within the circulation. Endurance training in cooler conditions also expands plasma volume, typically within days of starting a new training block, which is one reason heart rate at a fixed pace can fall quickly early in a training cycle.

KEY: Plasma volume supports venous return, stroke volume, cardiovascular stability and cooling. Heat acclimation and endurance training can both expand it, but by different amounts in different people.

### Evidence, and its limits

Systematic reviews show that heat acclimation can change plasma volume and cardiovascular responses, but the size and speed of adaptation vary across protocols and individuals. Reviews such as that by Périard, Racinais and Sawka describe plasma expansion as one of the earlier heat adaptations, often apparent within the first week of a structured protocol, while also noting that some of it may be transient if heat exposure stops. Protocol details such as daily duration, core temperature achieved, hydration practice and training status strongly influence what is observed.

This matters for how AKURA frames its heat hypotheses. It is reasonable, on current evidence, to hypothesise that a controlled heat-acclimation block may expand plasma volume in many athletes. It is not reasonable to promise a specific percentage to any individual, or to assume that plasma expansion automatically produces a faster race in cool conditions. That is a separate research question that needs its own performance test.

MYTH: A drop in haemoglobin concentration after heat training means the athlete has become anaemic. || It may partly reflect plasma expansion diluting the red cells. Anaemia is a clinical diagnosis that requires proper medical assessment, including measures of red-cell mass or iron status where appropriate.

### Measuring concentration and volume separately

This creates a central AKURA principle: measure concentration and volume separately whenever the research question requires it. A lower haemoglobin concentration after heat adaptation does not automatically mean worse oxygen-carrying capacity; it may partly reflect plasma expansion.

|Approach|What it gives|Practical note|
|CO rebreathing with Hb and Hct|Hb mass, blood and plasma volume|Specialist equipment and trained staff|
|Change in Hb and Hct (Dill–Costill method)|Estimated percentage change in plasma volume|Needs standardised posture and timing|
|Body mass and urine colour or specific gravity|Hydration context only|Does not measure plasma volume|
|HR at fixed pace and conditions|Functional response|Indirect, many confounders|

The Dill and Costill approach estimates the relative change in plasma volume from paired haemoglobin and haematocrit values. It is a practical research tool but is sensitive to posture, time of day and recent exercise, so blood samples must be taken under identical conditions. In a typical Indian club setting without laboratory access, the coach can still collect the functional evidence carefully: standardised heart rate at fixed pace, body mass, sweat observations and perceived effort, all logged with temperature and humidity.

There is also a practical interpretation problem in the days after a race or hard session. Prolonged exercise, especially in heat, can shift fluid out of the circulation and reduce plasma volume acutely, while recovery and training over subsequent days can expand it. Blood sampled on a Monday after a Sunday long run is not directly comparable with blood sampled after two easy days. For AKURA studies, the timing of the last hard session, fluid intake and even the posture in which the athlete waited before sampling should be recorded, because each of these can move plasma volume enough to change the apparent result.

COACH: If you run a heat block, schedule the post-test within a few days of finishing, because some plasma adaptations can fade without continued exposure. Keep hydration, caffeine and posture before blood tests the same at baseline and follow-up.

CHECK: For your next heat-training block, which marker will you use for concentration, which for volume, and which for performance?

## 12. Red Blood Cells and Erythropoiesis

The blood report arrives on a phone in a sports hostel: a single page of numbers, a few of them flagged in red. The athlete is a seventeen-year-old middle-distance runner who has felt tired for weeks. Her haemoglobin is low. She is far from unusual. A venous-blood survey across eight Indian states found anaemia in 44% of adolescent girls and 41% of adult women. A 2025 study of 104 female athletes in Tamil Nadu, volleyball and ball badminton players rather than runners, found that about 53.8% were anaemic. For a distance runner, whose sport depends on carrying oxygen, figures like these should stop a coach cold. Yet the response in many squads is either to ignore the red flags or to hand out iron tablets without a doctor's advice. Neither approach respects how the body actually builds blood. Red cells are made slowly, from raw materials, in response to signals, and every one of those steps can fail.

Red blood cells are the delivery vehicles of the endurance system. They contain haemoglobin and transport oxygen from the lungs to the tissues, and help carry carbon dioxide back. They are produced in the bone marrow through erythropoiesis and, in healthy adults, circulate for roughly four months before being removed and recycled. That slow turnover is the first thing a coach must understand: changing the red-cell population takes weeks, not days.

### How the body decides to make more

At altitude, reduced oxygen availability can increase erythropoietin signalling. Specialised cells in the kidney sense reduced oxygen delivery and increase production of the hormone erythropoietin, often within hours of arrival. EPO then stimulates the bone marrow to produce more red-cell precursors. Young red cells, called reticulocytes, appear in the blood over the following days, and a measurable increase in total red-cell and haemoglobin mass generally requires weeks of adequate exposure.

The response is not instantaneous and is affected by exposure dose, iron status, sleep, individual biology and the exact altitude protocol. Exposure dose includes both the altitude itself and the number of hours per day spent there, which is why the "live high, train low" model studied by Levine and Stray-Gundersen and others emphasises where the athlete sleeps as well as where the athlete trains. Illness, inflammation, inadequate energy intake and heavy training stress can all blunt the response.

KEY: Erythropoiesis is a slow, dose-dependent process. A rise in EPO is only the first step; building new red cells needs time, iron, energy, sleep and a sufficient hypoxic stimulus.

### Iron: the raw material

Every new haemoglobin molecule needs iron. An athlete with insufficient iron stores may not respond as expected to an altitude intervention, and may simply become more fatigued. This is one reason ferritin and broader blood assessment may be relevant in a supervised sports-medicine programme. Endurance athletes, particularly female athletes, athletes with low energy availability and those following predominantly vegetarian diets, may be at greater risk of low iron status, although risk is individual and should be assessed rather than presumed.

Iron supplementation is not a harmless default. Excess iron can cause harm, and supplementation decisions, dose and duration belong with a qualified doctor or sports physician, based on proper blood tests. Ferritin in particular can rise with inflammation or recent hard exercise, so timing of blood draws and clinical interpretation matter.

MYTH: Taking iron tablets before a camp will guarantee more red blood cells. || Iron only helps if stores are actually insufficient, and the decision to supplement should be made by a clinician after testing. Iron is a necessary raw material, not a performance booster on its own.

### Blood data is not a performance score

The coaching mistake is to treat a blood marker as a performance score. A higher red-cell count, haemoglobin or haematocrit is not the same as a faster 10 km. Blood markers fluctuate with hydration, posture, recent training and laboratory method. The correct approach is to connect blood data with performance and with longitudinal change in the same athlete.

|Marker|What it may indicate|Interpret with caution because|
|Haemoglobin concentration|Oxygen-carrying ratio|Changes with plasma volume|
|Haematocrit|Proportion of blood that is cells|Changes with hydration and posture|
|Reticulocytes|Recent red-cell production|Short-lived, method-dependent|
|Ferritin|Iron stores (approximate)|Rises with inflammation|
|Hb mass|Total oxygen-carrying capacity|Needs specialist method|

It is also worth stating plainly: artificially raising red-cell mass through EPO injection, blood transfusion or similar methods is prohibited in sport and carries serious health risks. AKURA's interest in erythropoiesis is entirely about understanding legitimate, natural adaptation to training and environment.

FIELD: Supervised blood-monitoring routine ; schedule tests at the same time of day after a rest or easy day ; keep the athlete seated for a standardised period before sampling ; record hydration, menstrual phase where relevant, recent illness and training ; request ferritin, full blood count and any tests advised by the clinician ; store results beside the nearest standardised performance test ; review trends with the medical team, not in isolation.

Individual variation deserves emphasis. Two athletes on the same altitude camp, sleeping at the same elevation and following the same programme, may show very different red-cell responses. Some of this reflects measurement error, some reflects iron status, illness or training stress, and some appears to be genuine biological difference. This is why the phrase "responder" or "non-responder" should be used carefully. An athlete who does not respond on one camp may respond on another when iron, sleep and load are better managed. AEI records each exposure as an individual experiment rather than labelling the athlete permanently.

COACH: Build a relationship with a sports physician before planning altitude or heavy training blocks. Your job is to provide training context and performance data; the clinician's job is to interpret blood results and decide on any treatment.

CHECK: Does your athlete have a baseline ferritin and full blood count taken under standardised conditions, and do you know what the result was compared with their own previous values?

## 13. VO₂max

Put two numbers side by side and they seem to tell a story on their own. In 1991, researchers testing Bengali female athletes on a cycle ergometer found that the long-distance runners among them had the highest relative VO₂max of any group: 43.0 mL/kg/min. In 1995, Bengt Saltin and colleagues reported that Kenyan senior runners averaged 79.9 mL/kg/min when tested at sea level. The gap looks enormous, and it is easy to draw a sweeping conclusion from it about who can and cannot run. That conclusion would be wrong in almost every way. The two studies tested different sexes, different generations and very different levels of training, and pedalling a bicycle is not the same task as carrying a body over the ground; many runners score lower on a cycle than they would running. Neither number says how anyone would perform on race day. VO₂max is a real and important measurement. It is also the most over-interpreted number in the sport.

VO₂max is probably the most famous number in endurance physiology. It describes the maximum rate at which the body can take up and use oxygen during severe exercise, usually expressed in millilitres of oxygen per kilogram of body mass per minute. It is important: elite endurance athletes almost always have high values, and a low VO₂max limits what an athlete can achieve at race pace. But it is not a complete description of running ability, and treating it as the master number leads to poor coaching decisions.

### What VO₂max actually represents

Using the Fick principle from Chapter 8, VO₂max is the product of maximal cardiac output and maximal oxygen extraction. In trained athletes at sea level, much of the evidence suggests that oxygen delivery, especially maximal cardiac output and haemoglobin mass, is a major determinant of VO₂max, while muscle oxidative capacity also contributes. Reviews by Bassett and Howley, and by Joyner and Coyle in the Journal of Physiology in 2008, describe VO₂max as one of three key pillars of endurance performance, alongside the lactate or threshold characteristics and running economy.

VO₂max is measured in a laboratory with gas analysis during an incremental test to exhaustion. Values vary with protocol, equipment, motivation and whether the test is run on a treadmill or another ergometer. Values reported by watches are estimates derived from heart rate and pace; they can be useful for tracking trends in one person but should not be treated as laboratory measurements.

KEY: VO₂max sets the size of the aerobic ceiling. It does not tell you how close to that ceiling the athlete can race, or how much oxygen each kilometre costs.

### Same engine, different results

Two athletes can have similar VO₂max values and different race performances because of running economy, threshold characteristics, body mass, fuel use, biomechanics and pacing. Consider a simplified illustration.

|Variable|Athlete A|Athlete B|
|VO₂max (mL/kg/min)|70|70|
|Threshold as % of VO₂max|75%|85%|
|Oxygen cost at 16 km/h (mL/kg/min)|56|50|
|Likely marathon outcome|Slower|Faster|

The numbers are hypothetical, chosen to show the logic. Athlete B can sustain a higher fraction of the same ceiling and needs less oxygen for the same speed, so Athlete B's sustainable race pace is higher despite an identical VO₂max. This is the same reasoning used in the Joyner and Coyle model, in which performance velocity reflects VO₂max, the fraction of it that can be sustained, and economy together.

Coaching software can add to the confusion. Jack Daniels' VDOT, for example, is a performance-based index derived from race times; its documentation explicitly distinguishes VDOT from laboratory VO₂max and notes that runners with similar VO₂max can produce different race results. VDOT is useful precisely because it folds economy and other factors into a single performance-derived value, but it should be labelled for what it is.

MYTH: The athlete with the highest VO₂max will win the race. || Among well-trained athletes of similar level, VO₂max alone predicts race results poorly. Economy, sustainable fraction of VO₂max, fuelling and pacing often separate them.

### How AEI uses VO₂max

AEI therefore treats VO₂max as one physiological variable, not the master number. Where a valid laboratory test exists, AEI records the value together with the protocol, date, body mass and testing conditions. Where only a field or wearable estimate exists, AEI labels it as an estimate. In both cases, the value sits in the profile beside economy, threshold and the performance anchor, so that a change in VO₂max can be checked against a change in real running.

VO₂max is trainable, especially in less-trained athletes, through a combination of consistent aerobic volume and intervals near maximal aerobic speed. It also has a substantial inherited component, and responses to the same training vary between individuals. For Indian athletes the practical message is not to fixate on a number that may be difficult to measure, but to build the aerobic system through progressive training and to measure it well when the opportunity exists.

There is also the question of how VO₂max is expressed. Relative values, per kilogram of body mass, are most relevant to running because runners carry their own weight. Absolute values, in litres per minute, better reflect the size of the cardiovascular engine. An athlete who loses body mass may see relative VO₂max rise without any change in the heart or blood, while an athlete who gains muscle may see it fall slightly despite becoming stronger and more robust. Neither change should be interpreted without body mass, performance and health in view, and deliberately chasing a higher relative number through weight loss is not a safe coaching strategy.

COACH: If your athlete's VO₂max estimate on a watch changes, check whether pace at a fixed heart rate or a time trial has also changed. If it has not, treat the watch number as noise until real performance confirms it.

CHECK: Do you know whether the VO₂max values you hear about for your athletes are laboratory measurements, field estimates or watch algorithms?

## 14. Running Economy

On 29 March 2025, at The TEN meeting in San Juan Capistrano, California, Gulveer Singh ran 10,000 metres in 27:00.22. That is 25 laps of the track at an average of about 64.8 seconds each, or roughly 2 minutes 42 seconds for every kilometre, held for twenty-seven minutes. It made him, according to the Sports Authority of India, the third-fastest Asian of all time, and it left him just 0.22 seconds outside the 27-minute barrier he had been aiming for. At that speed, the difference between a good race and a great one is not only how much oxygen a runner can take in. It is also how little oxygen each stride costs. A runner who wastes a few percent on every step, by bouncing too high, braking at each landing or tightening the shoulders, pays that tax thousands of times over. Across 10,000 metres, and even more across 42.2 kilometres, the tax adds up to minutes.

Running economy is the oxygen cost of running at a given submaximal speed. If two athletes run at the same pace but one uses less oxygen, the more economical runner has more physiological reserve: more room below the ceiling, less heat produced for the same speed and potentially slower use of carbohydrate. The marathon is not only about how much oxygen you can take in. It is also about how little oxygen you need for the speed you want.

### Measuring economy

In the laboratory, economy is measured by recording steady-state oxygen uptake while the athlete runs at one or more fixed submaximal speeds, usually below the lactate threshold so that aerobic metabolism dominates. It can be expressed as oxygen uptake per kilogram per minute at a given speed, or as oxygen cost per kilogram per kilometre, which allows comparison across speeds. Some researchers also express it as energy cost, taking into account the mix of fuels being used.

|Expression|Units|Why used|
|VO₂ at fixed speed|mL/kg/min at, say, 14 or 16 km/h|Simple, protocol-specific|
|Oxygen cost of transport|mL/kg/km|Compares across speeds|
|Energy cost|kcal or kJ/kg/km|Accounts for fuel mix|

Economy varies considerably even among athletes of similar ability, and small differences of a few percent can matter over 42.2 km. Measurement also has error: day-to-day variation, treadmill versus overground differences and footwear all influence results, so repeated tests should be standardised carefully.

KEY: Economy is how much oxygen the athlete spends to buy a given speed. A more economical runner has more reserve at race pace, even with the same VO₂max.

### What shapes economy

Economy is influenced by biomechanics, body mass, stiffness and elastic return, neuromuscular coordination, footwear, fatigue and training history. Reviews such as that by Saunders and colleagues in Sports Medicine in 2004 describe many of these factors. Tendons and muscles that store and return elastic energy efficiently reduce the metabolic work the muscle must do. Coordinated recruitment reduces wasted co-contraction. Lighter shoes with compliant, resilient midsoles can lower oxygen cost for many runners, although responses differ individually. Body mass matters, but chasing weight loss carries serious health risks, including low energy availability, and is not a safe economy strategy.

Economy is one reason a runner can outperform another athlete despite having a similar or lower VO₂max. It is also a quality that can deteriorate late in a race: as fatigue accumulates, mechanics change, oxygen cost at the same speed may rise and the athlete moves closer to the ceiling without speeding up.

MYTH: Economy is fixed by body type and cannot be trained. || Economy reflects many trainable qualities. Years of running, strength and plyometric work, and technique refinement may improve it, although the size of change varies and some influences such as limb proportions are not trainable.

### Building economy in an Indian context

Many Indian runners train on mixed surfaces: roads, dirt tracks, temple streets, synthetic tracks where available and hills. That variety can be an advantage, developing robust feet and adaptable mechanics. Economy development usually includes consistent volume over years, short relaxed strides and hill sprints, heavy and explosive strength training performed with good technique, and exposure to race-pace running so that the athlete becomes efficient at the speed that matters.

Heat is also relevant. Running in heat increases total physiological strain at the same speed, and some studies show a small rise in oxygen cost in the heat, although the effect is not consistent. A more economical runner produces less metabolic heat for the same pace, which may help in hot races, but that link needs individual testing.

FIELD: Field economy proxy ; on a flat, calm route or track, warm up 15 minutes ; run 2 × 6 minutes at a fixed, well-below-threshold pace with 2 minutes easy between ; record HR over the last 2 minutes of each, cadence and RPE ; note temperature, humidity, shoes and body mass ; repeat monthly with identical pace and shoes ; a falling HR at the same pace and conditions may reflect better economy, plasma volume or fitness, so label it as a proxy.

Economy also interacts with pacing. Because oxygen cost rises with speed, an athlete who runs an uneven marathon, surging on inclines or chasing groups early, spends more oxygen and more carbohydrate than one who runs the same average pace evenly. In crowded Indian city marathons, with congested starts, sharp turns and flyovers, good pacing discipline can protect some of an athlete's economy advantage. AEI therefore treats pacing data from races, including split variation and response to gradients, as useful context when interpreting economy tests, not as a separate matter.

COACH: Protect economy late in races by building strength and by practising race pace when partially fatigued, for example the last kilometres of a long run. Film the athlete fresh and tired; changes in posture and ground contact often reveal where economy is lost.

CHECK: How many of your athlete's weekly sessions develop economy directly, through strength, strides, hills or race-pace running, rather than only aerobic volume?

## 15. Mitochondria and Muscle Oxygen Use

The training numbers Sawan Barwal's camp reported to The Tribune are not glamorous. About 200 kilometres a week. A long run of 40 to 42 kilometres every other week. Months of it, in camps in the Nilgiris, Kannur and Bengaluru. In most distance programmes, a large share of volume like that is run at an easy, conversational effort. Watch any group of distance runners on an ordinary easy morning and you might wonder what the point is. Nobody is racing. Nobody is gasping. Yet this is the kind of running that builds the deepest part of the endurance engine, the part no watch can display. Inside each working muscle fibre, week after week, the machinery that uses oxygen is expanded, and the network of tiny blood vessels that feeds it is extended. Whatever else went into Sawan's 2:11:37 for Asian Games silver in Nagoya, a marathon at that speed is impossible without that invisible machinery, built one unremarkable kilometre at a time.

The heart, lungs and blood deliver oxygen, but oxygen only creates energy when a muscle cell uses it. That happens inside mitochondria, the cellular machinery that supports aerobic ATP production. Mitochondria oxidise fatty acids and the products of carbohydrate breakdown, including pyruvate and lactate, to generate most of the ATP needed for sustained running. If delivery is the supply chain, mitochondria are the factory floor.

### What endurance training does to muscle

Endurance training stimulates mitochondrial adaptations and improves the muscle's ability to use oxygen and oxidise substrates. The classic work of John Holloszy in the 1960s showed that endurance training increases mitochondrial enzyme activity and oxidative capacity in skeletal muscle. Since then, research has described increases in mitochondrial content and function, in capillary density around muscle fibres, and in the enzymes and transporters that handle fat and carbohydrate.

These adaptations increase the muscle's ability to extract oxygen from blood, widening the arteriovenous oxygen difference in the Fick equation. They also mean that, at a given submaximal pace, the trained muscle can meet its energy needs with less disturbance to its internal environment, relying relatively more on fat and producing less lactate accumulation in the blood.

|Adaptation|Effect|Typical stimulus|
|Mitochondrial content and function|More aerobic ATP capacity|Aerobic volume, threshold and interval work|
|Capillary density|Shorter diffusion distance, longer transit time|Consistent aerobic training|
|Fat-oxidation enzymes|Greater fat use at submaximal pace|Aerobic volume, long runs|
|Lactate and glucose transporters|Better lactate exchange and use|Varied intensity training|

KEY: A high-level endurance athlete is not a large oxygen tank but a coordinated system that delivers oxygen and uses it efficiently. Mitochondria and capillaries determine how well the muscle uses what the heart delivers.

### Many roads to the same factory

This distinction helps explain why easy volume, threshold work, long runs and strength training can all contribute through different pathways. Easy volume accumulates time with aerobic signalling in many fibres and supports capillary growth. Threshold work challenges the muscle to sustain a high oxidative rate. Long runs deplete glycogen in working fibres and may amplify some metabolic signals, while also training durability. Strength training contributes mainly through economy, force production and injury resilience rather than through large mitochondrial gains, but it keeps the whole chain working.

The research literature continues to debate which combinations of volume and intensity best stimulate mitochondrial adaptation, and the answer likely depends on training history. For practical coaching, the consistent message is that repeated, well-recovered aerobic work over months and years builds the muscle's aerobic machinery, and that no single session type is sufficient.

MYTH: Only high-intensity intervals build mitochondria, so easy running is wasted time. || High-intensity work is a strong stimulus, but aerobic volume also drives mitochondrial and capillary adaptation, and it can be sustained over far longer periods with less risk. Most successful endurance programmes combine both.

### Avoid chasing one number

The practical implication is to avoid chasing one physiological number at the expense of the system. A programme built entirely around raising VO₂max may neglect muscle endurance and economy; one built entirely around easy volume may leave the threshold underdeveloped. Mitochondrial adaptations are also not directly visible to most coaches. They are inferred from outcomes such as lower heart rate and lactate at a given pace, improved fat use and better late-race durability.

Near-infrared spectroscopy devices that estimate muscle oxygen saturation are increasingly available, but their readings depend on placement, skin and fat thickness and device algorithms. They may be useful for research within the same athlete under standardised conditions, and should not be treated as a direct measure of mitochondrial content.

Recovery is part of the mitochondrial story. The training session provides the signal, but the adaptation is built during the hours and days that follow, supported by adequate energy, carbohydrate and protein intake, and sleep. Athletes with low energy availability, chronic sleep restriction or frequent illness may accumulate training without the expected muscle adaptations. For Indian athletes balancing work or study, long commutes and early morning sessions to avoid heat, protecting sleep and nutrition may be as important for the muscle as adding another session. AEI should record these recovery inputs alongside training load, so that a lack of adaptation can be interpreted fairly.

COACH: Judge muscle adaptation by what the athlete can sustain, not by a single test. Track how pace, heart rate and perceived effort behave in the final third of long runs; better durability there is often the most practical sign that the muscle's aerobic machinery is improving.

CHECK: Over the last eight weeks, has your athlete's training included enough consistent aerobic volume and enough sustained threshold work to stimulate the muscle, or has it relied on one type of session?

## 16. Lactate Is a Messenger

On a school ground in Madurai, a PE teacher blows his whistle after the last of eight fast 400s. Twelve teenagers bend over, hands on knees, faces twisted. Their legs are burning. "Jog it out," he shouts. "Flush out the lactic acid, or you won't be able to walk tomorrow." The runners obediently shuffle a lap. It is advice that has been handed down on Indian grounds for decades, and at first glance it makes perfect sense. The burning is real, the soreness two days later is real, and the substance being blamed has a name that sounds suitably sinister. But almost every part of the explanation is wrong. The molecule in question is not the villain of the piece. It is not what makes the legs sore later in the week, and the body does not need to be rinsed of it like a dirty pan. In fact, trained muscle takes it up and uses it.

For much of the twentieth century, lactate was cast as the villain of endurance sport: a toxic waste product that made legs burn and caused fatigue. That picture is now considered outdated. Lactate is a normal metabolic intermediate, not simply a toxic waste product. It can be produced in active muscle, transported between tissues and used as a fuel. Understanding this changes how a coach thinks about threshold training, lactate testing and the language used with athletes.

### From waste to shuttle

When glucose or glycogen is broken down through glycolysis, the end product pyruvate can either enter the mitochondria for oxidation or be converted to lactate. Lactate is produced continuously, even at rest, and production rises as exercise intensity and glycolytic rate increase. Crucially, lactate does not simply sit in the muscle. It moves out of cells through monocarboxylate transporters and can be taken up by neighbouring oxidative muscle fibres, the heart, the brain and the liver. Oxidative fibres and the heart can use it as a fuel; the liver can convert it back to glucose.

George Brooks and colleagues developed and extended this framework as the lactate shuttle concept, describing lactate as a link between glycolytic and oxidative metabolism and as a signalling molecule that may influence gene expression and adaptation. The details continue to be researched, but the broad idea that lactate is a useful fuel and messenger, not merely waste, is well supported.

KEY: Lactate is produced, moved and used throughout the body. Blood lactate concentration reflects the balance between production and clearance, not simply how much "waste" has been made.

MYTH: Lactate causes muscle soreness the next day and must be flushed out after hard sessions. || Blood lactate returns towards resting levels within roughly an hour after exercise, even without special cooling-down methods. Delayed soreness has other causes, mainly related to unfamiliar or eccentric loading.

### What rising blood lactate means

During increasing exercise intensity, blood lactate concentration rises as production and clearance dynamics change. At low intensities, production and removal are closely matched, so blood lactate stays near resting values. As intensity rises, glycolytic flux increases, more fast-twitch fibres are recruited and clearance may fail to keep pace, so blood lactate begins to accumulate. At higher intensities it rises steeply.

Fatigue during hard exercise is associated with a complex mix of metabolic changes, including accumulation of hydrogen ions and inorganic phosphate, and changes in ion balance within the muscle. The older idea that lactate itself is the direct cause of the burning sensation and fatigue is now regarded as too simple. Blood lactate remains a useful marker of metabolic strain, precisely because it tracks the balance of these processes, but it should be seen as a signal rather than a poison.

Threshold training is therefore not about "removing all lactate". It is about improving the athlete's ability to sustain a high metabolic rate while controlling the accumulation and use of lactate and associated metabolites. Training may improve this through greater mitochondrial capacity, better lactate transport and oxidation, and changes in muscle fibre recruitment and fuel use.

### Testing lactate responsibly

This is why lactate testing can be valuable when standardised, but a single lactate number should not be interpreted without workload, timing and protocol. A reading of 3 mmol/L means little unless you know the pace, the gradient, how long the athlete had been running, when the sample was taken, the analyser used and the athlete's recent diet and training.

|Factor|Why it changes the reading|
|Stage duration|Short stages may not reach steady state|
|Sample timing|Lactate changes quickly after stopping|
|Glycogen status|Low glycogen can lower lactate at a given pace|
|Heat and hydration|Can raise physiological strain at fixed pace|
|Analyser and site|Devices and finger versus earlobe differ|

Portable lactate meters are now affordable for some Indian academies and clubs. They are powerful tools when used carefully, and misleading tools when used casually. Hygiene is essential: sweat contamination, especially in humid conditions, can falsely raise readings, so the sampling site must be cleaned and dried, and sharps must be disposed of safely.

FIELD: Standardised lactate step test ; after a consistent diet and an easy day, warm up 10–15 minutes ; run 5–7 stages of 4–5 minutes, increasing pace by a fixed step each stage ; take a clean, dry earlobe or fingertip sample within about 30 seconds after each stage ; record pace, HR, RPE, temperature and humidity ; stop when RPE is very hard or lactate rises steeply ; repeat on the same route or treadmill with the same analyser.

COACH: Stop describing lactate as poison to athletes. Explain it as a fuel and a messenger that the trained body learns to handle better. It changes how athletes relate to hard sessions and reduces fear of discomfort at threshold.

CHECK: If your athlete produced a lactate reading of 4 mmol/L today, what six pieces of information would you need to interpret it properly?

## 17. Lactate Threshold

Tuesday is threshold day for a running club in Bengaluru, and three members arrive at Cubbon Park with three different ideas of what that means. The first has a laboratory report that puts her threshold at 4 mmol/L of lactate and a pace of 4:35 per kilometre. The second has a watch that announces his threshold pace every morning, a number that seems to move with his mood. The third has only her coach's instruction: run comfortably hard, at the pace you could hold for about an hour in a race. All three are using the same word. They may be describing three different intensities. By the second repetition the first runner is fighting, the second is chatting, and the third is exactly where her coach wanted her. The problem is not that any of them is foolish. It is that one word has been asked to carry too many meanings, and each meaning comes from a different method.

Few words in coaching are used as loosely as "threshold". Athletes talk about "threshold runs", laboratories report "thresholds" and watches estimate "threshold pace", yet these often refer to different things. The term "threshold" can describe several related concepts, including fixed blood-lactate definitions and individual physiological breakpoints, and different laboratories may use different protocols and terminology.

### Many thresholds, one idea

Broadly, two important transitions are commonly described as intensity rises. The first is the intensity at which blood lactate first rises meaningfully above resting or baseline values, sometimes called the first lactate threshold or aerobic threshold. The second is a higher intensity above which lactate can no longer be stabilised and keeps rising over time during constant-pace running, often associated with the maximal lactate steady state, the second threshold, or related concepts such as critical speed.

|Term|Common definition|Caution|
|LT1 or aerobic threshold|First sustained rise above baseline lactate|Detection method varies|
|Fixed 2 mmol/L|Pace at a set concentration|Not individualised|
|Fixed 4 mmol/L (OBLA)|Pace at a set concentration|Individual MLSS may be higher or lower|
|MLSS|Highest pace with stable lactate over about 30 minutes|Time-consuming to determine|
|Critical speed|Boundary derived from time trials at several distances|Model and protocol dependent|

Faude, Kindermann and Meyer, in a 2009 review in Sports Medicine, described the large number of lactate threshold concepts in use and cautioned that they are not interchangeable. Fixed concentrations such as 4 mmol/L are convenient but ignore the fact that individual maximal lactate steady state values differ considerably between athletes. A threshold number is therefore meaningful only alongside the method that produced it.

KEY: "Threshold" is a family of concepts, not one universal number. Every threshold value must be stored with the protocol and method used to find it.

### Threshold as a training domain

For coaching, threshold is best treated as a training intensity domain rather than a magical pace. It should feel controlled-hard rather than maximal. Athletes often describe it as comfortably hard: breathing is deep and rhythmic, speaking is limited to short phrases, and the effort could be held for a sustained period in a race. It supports the ability to sustain a high fraction of aerobic capacity, which, as Chapter 13 showed, is one of the three pillars of endurance performance.

Because the domain has boundaries rather than a single point, sessions such as continuous tempo runs, cruise intervals and longer intervals with short recoveries can all target it. Heat, hills, fatigue and fuelling state all shift the pace at which the domain occurs, so on a humid Chennai evening the correct threshold effort may be noticeably slower than on a cool morning. Coaches who hold athletes to a fixed pace regardless of conditions risk turning a threshold session into a much harder one.

MYTH: Threshold pace is a fixed number you can use all season. || Threshold shifts with training, fatigue, fuel and environment. It is better treated as an effort domain checked by heart rate, RPE and, where available, lactate, and retested regularly.

### Recording threshold in AEI

AEI should record the test protocol alongside the threshold result. A number without the protocol is incomplete data. For each threshold entry, AEI stores the method (lactate step test, fixed concentration, MLSS, critical-speed model or field time trial), the stage duration and step size, the surface or treadmill gradient, the environmental conditions and the analyser or device. Field estimates, such as the pace sustained in a 30-minute time trial, are useful and practical but should be labelled as such, and they are strongly influenced by motivation and pacing skill.

Changes over time are often more valuable than absolute values. A rightward shift of the lactate curve, where the same lactate appears at a faster pace, or a lower heart rate at the same threshold pace, is a meaningful sign of adaptation if the protocol has been kept the same.

FIELD: Field threshold check ; on a flat track or measured loop, warm up 15 minutes with strides ; run a 30-minute evenly paced effort as hard as can be sustained ; record average pace and HR for the final 20 minutes, RPE, temperature and humidity ; cool down 10 minutes ; repeat every 6–8 weeks in similar conditions ; store as "field threshold estimate" with the protocol.

The relationship between threshold and heat deserves particular attention in India. Heat increases cardiovascular strain and, for many athletes, carbohydrate use at a given pace, so blood lactate and heart rate at a fixed speed may be higher on a hot day. A threshold test performed on a cool December morning in Chennai will not give the same result as one performed in May. Where possible, testing should be scheduled at a consistent time of day and season, or the conditions should be recorded carefully enough that the difference can be accounted for when results are compared.

COACH: Prescribe threshold by a combination of pace range, heart-rate range and perceived effort, and let conditions decide which one leads on the day. In heat, let effort and heart rate lead and allow the pace to slow.

CHECK: Which threshold definition have you been using for your athletes, and would another coach or laboratory reproduce the same number from your notes?

## 18. Fuel, Glycogen and Fat Oxidation

At the Rio Olympics in August 2016, O. P. Jaisha, India's national record holder in the marathon, collapsed after crossing the finish line of the women's race in 89th place. Afterwards she said Indian officials had not provided her with refreshments along the course. "All the [other] countries had their stalls at every two kilometres, but our country's stall was empty," she was quoted as saying by Scroll. The Athletics Federation of India disputed her account, and her teammate Kavita Raut said she herself had had enough water. What exactly happened at those drinks tables may never be settled. But the episode put a public spotlight on a question every marathoner eventually faces. Fitness gets a runner to the start line. What she has stored in her muscles, what she drinks and what she eats along the way decide how much of that fitness survives to the finish. A marathon is a fuel problem as much as a fitness problem.

The marathon is where physiology meets the kitchen. Marathon performance depends heavily on substrate management: which fuels the athlete uses, how much is stored and how well it is replaced during the race. Carbohydrate supports high-intensity work efficiently, while fat oxidation contributes increasingly as intensity and duration change. Training can influence substrate use, but race-day fuelling remains essential.

### Two fuel tanks

The body stores carbohydrate as glycogen in muscle and liver, and as a small amount of glucose in the blood. Glycogen stores are limited, on the order of a few hundred grams in muscle and around a hundred grams in the liver in a well-fed adult, although they vary with body size, training and diet. Fat stores, by contrast, are large even in lean athletes. Carbohydrate yields more ATP per litre of oxygen than fat, so it is the preferred fuel as intensity rises. Fat is abundant but oxidised more slowly and is the dominant fuel mainly at lower intensities.

|Feature|Carbohydrate|Fat|
|Storage size|Limited (muscle and liver glycogen)|Large|
|Energy per litre of O₂|Higher|Lower|
|Use at marathon pace|Major contributor for most runners|Contributes, varies by athlete|
|Main risk|Depletion late in race|Too slow to sustain high pace alone|

As a marathon progresses, muscle glycogen in the working fibres falls. If carbohydrate availability becomes insufficient, the athlete may be unable to hold pace, a phenomenon runners call "hitting the wall". Training can increase the capacity to oxidise fat at a given pace and increase glycogen storage, sparing carbohydrate, but for most runners racing a marathon at a competitive pace, carbohydrate remains central.

KEY: Carbohydrate is the high-performance fuel for racing, but its stores are limited. Fitness improves fat use and glycogen storage; race-day fuelling protects what remains.

### Fitness and fuel need each other

The athlete who starts a marathon with excellent aerobic fitness but poor carbohydrate availability can still fail late. Conversely, high carbohydrate intake cannot rescue inadequate fitness. Current sports nutrition guidance, summarised in reviews by Burke, Hawley, Jeukendrup and colleagues, describes several practical tools: increasing carbohydrate intake for one to two days before a marathon to maximise glycogen stores, a carbohydrate-containing pre-race meal, and carbohydrate intake during the race, commonly in the range of about 30–60 g per hour and up to around 90 g per hour for longer events when using mixtures of glucose and fructose that the gut has been trained to tolerate. Exact amounts should be individualised, ideally with a qualified sports dietitian.

Gut tolerance is trainable. Athletes who try new gels or drinks on race day risk gastrointestinal distress, which is more likely in heat. In the Indian context, familiar carbohydrate foods such as rice, idli, bananas or other locally available options may form part of pre-race meals, provided they have been practised in training and suit the athlete's gut.

MYTH: Fat-adapted runners do not need carbohydrate during a marathon. || Low-carbohydrate adaptation can raise fat oxidation, but studies have shown it may impair the ability to use carbohydrate efficiently at high intensity. For most competitive marathoners, carbohydrate availability remains critical at race pace.

### Fuel meets heat, pacing and hydration

The research model should therefore connect metabolic fitness with practical fuelling, hydration, pacing and environmental stress. Heat increases reliance on carbohydrate at a given pace in many individuals and makes the gut less tolerant, while dehydration reduces performance and increases strain. An aggressive early pace burns glycogen faster and may leave the athlete short in the final 10 km. These factors interact; a fuelling plan that works in a cool Bengaluru long run may fail in a humid Mumbai race.

Athletes should also avoid both extremes of drinking: significant dehydration impairs performance in heat, while drinking large amounts beyond sweat losses in long events can cause dangerous low blood sodium. Individual sweat-rate testing helps set sensible targets, and any athlete with symptoms such as confusion, severe headache or vomiting during or after a race needs urgent medical attention.

FIELD: Race-fuel rehearsal long run ; eat the planned pre-race meal 3–4 hours before ; weigh the athlete nude or in minimal clothing before and after ; run 25–32 km with the final 8–12 km at goal marathon pace ; take the planned carbohydrate every 20–30 minutes at the planned hourly amount ; record fluid intake, gut comfort, RPE, HR and pace drift ; note temperature and humidity ; adjust the plan in small steps.

COACH: Treat fuelling as a trained skill. Rehearse the race-day meal, gel or drink type, timing and quantity in at least three long runs, including one in conditions similar to the race, and record what happened in the athlete's log.

CHECK: Has your athlete practised the exact race-day fuelling plan, in similar heat, at goal marathon pace, and does AEI hold a record of how their pace and heart rate behaved late in that run?
