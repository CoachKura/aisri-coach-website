PART VI — MARATHON ENGINE

PARTINTRO: The marathon is where endurance physiology stops being theory and becomes a public examination. Aerobic power, sustainable intensity, fuel, fluid, heat and muscular resilience must all hold together for two to five hours, and the weakest link decides the outcome. This part treats the marathon as a systems test rather than a longer 5K. It explains why marathon pace is a fraction of aerobic capacity, why that fraction differs between athletes, why pace so often collapses late in the race, and why the final 10 km reveals a quality short races cannot: durability. These ideas prepare the ground for AKURA's argument that an athlete needs distance-specific profiles, not one permanent number.

## 33. The Marathon Is a Systems Test

The marathon is not a 42.195 km version of a 5K. A 5K asks how much oxygen an athlete can use and how much discomfort they can tolerate for a quarter of an hour. A marathon asks a different, broader question: can every system that supports running continue to cooperate for hours, while fuel stores fall, body water is lost, heat accumulates and muscle fibres fatigue? It is a long-duration systems test involving aerobic power, sustainable intensity, glycogen management, hydration, thermoregulation, musculoskeletal resilience and pacing. An athlete can be strong in five of these and still have a poor race because the sixth failed.

### Seven systems, one finish line

It helps to name the systems explicitly, because each can be measured and each can be trained. Aerobic power sets the ceiling. Sustainable intensity, usually discussed through lactate threshold and fractional utilisation, determines how close to that ceiling the athlete can run for hours. Glycogen management determines whether carbohydrate lasts to the finish. Hydration influences plasma volume and cardiovascular strain. Thermoregulation decides how much blood must be diverted to the skin for cooling. Musculoskeletal resilience governs whether the legs can keep producing the same force with each step. Pacing is the decision-making system that spends all of these resources, wisely or badly.

|System|What it contributes|How it fails late|
|Aerobic power|Upper limit of oxygen use|Rarely the late limiter itself|
|Sustainable intensity|Fraction of capacity held for hours|Pace set above true sustainable level|
|Fuel (glycogen)|Carbohydrate for high-intensity running|Depletion, rising effort, slowing|
|Fluid balance|Plasma volume, sweat capacity|Rising heart rate, heat strain|
|Thermoregulation|Heat dissipation|Core temperature rises, pace falls|
|Muscle and tendon|Force production each stride|Mechanics change, cost rises|
|Pacing|Allocation of all resources|Early overspending|

KEY: A marathon is limited by the weakest system on the day, not by the strongest number on the athlete's test report.

### Why the last 10 km tells the truth

The final 10 km exposes weaknesses that may not appear in a short race. Cardiac drift, dehydration, glycogen depletion, heat accumulation and muscular fatigue can all increase the cost of running. The athlete may be holding the same pace on the watch, yet the internal price of that pace is rising: heart rate creeps up, breathing deepens, perceived effort climbs, and stride mechanics become less efficient. When the internal cost exceeds what the athlete can pay, pace falls.

This is why two runners with similar 10K times can finish a marathon ten minutes apart. The shorter race measured mainly their aerobic capacity and threshold; the marathon measured their ability to keep those qualities stable as fatigue accumulated.

MYTH: A fast 10K runner will automatically run a fast marathon. || Short-race speed predicts marathon potential only when long-run conditioning, fuelling, heat tolerance and durability are also developed; the marathon adds systems that a 10K barely tests.

### Central physiology and peripheral fatigue

A good marathon model therefore tracks both central physiology and peripheral fatigue. Central measures include heart rate, cardiac drift, breathing and core temperature, reflecting the heart, lungs and circulation. Peripheral measures describe what happens in the working muscles and tendons: local fatigue, glycogen depletion within fibres, and the gradual change in ground contact and stride that increases the oxygen cost of each kilometre.

In the Indian context, the marathon systems test is often run in warm, humid conditions, where fluid and heat systems are stressed earlier than in cool European races. A Mumbai, Chennai or Hyderabad marathon may therefore reward a different balance of qualities than a cool-weather race, and an athlete's profile should be interpreted with the environment recorded.

### Measuring the systems test

If the marathon is a systems test, then preparation should include system checks. Not every athlete needs a laboratory. A coach can learn a great deal from simple, repeatable observations: the heart-rate drift during a steady 25 km run, the change in body mass across a long run, the pace of the last 5 km of a marathon-specific session, and the athlete's notes on gut comfort and leg feel. Recorded consistently, these observations show which system is lagging behind the others.

The aim is not to eliminate every weakness before race day; that is rarely possible. It is to know which systems are most likely to fail, so that pacing, fuelling and cooling strategies can be designed around them. An athlete with modest heat tolerance may choose a cooler race, start more conservatively or practise cooling strategies. An athlete with a sensitive gut may rehearse fuelling repeatedly. Knowing the weak link turns anxiety into a plan.

COACH: After each marathon, ask the athlete to review the race system by system rather than simply saying “I hit the wall”. Was the start too fast, was fuel taken as planned, how much body mass was lost, how hot was it, and when did the legs start to feel different? This turns one disappointing race into a map of the next training block.

CHECK: For your last marathon, which of the seven systems do you believe failed first, and what evidence (splits, heart rate, fuel log, weather, body mass) supports that belief?

## 34. Fractional VO₂max and Marathon Pace

Every runner has a maximal aerobic capacity, but nobody races a marathon at it. Marathon pace is typically below maximal aerobic speed, run at a fraction of VO₂max that can be sustained for hours. That fraction is not fixed. It depends on fitness, durability, fuel, environmental conditions and individual physiology. Understanding fractional utilisation helps coaches move beyond the simple idea that a bigger VO₂max always means a faster marathon.

### The three-part performance model

A widely cited framework, summarised by Joyner and Coyle in 2008, describes endurance performance as the product of three main factors: VO₂max, the fraction of VO₂max that can be sustained (closely related to lactate threshold), and running economy, which converts oxygen use into speed. The oxygen uptake an athlete can sustain, combined with economy, gives a performance velocity. A runner with a moderate VO₂max can outperform a runner with a higher VO₂max if they sustain a higher fraction or run more economically.

|Factor|Question it answers|Typical assessment|
|VO₂max|How large is the aerobic ceiling?|Graded laboratory test|
|Fractional utilisation|How much of it can be held for hours?|Threshold testing, race analysis|
|Running economy|How much oxygen does each speed cost?|Submaximal steady-state test|
|Durability|Do these values hold as fatigue builds?|Pre- and post-long-run testing|

Well-trained marathon runners are often described as racing at a high fraction of their VO₂max, commonly quoted around three-quarters or more for trained athletes and higher in elites, but the exact figure varies widely by individual, conditions and test method. The point for coaches is not to memorise a percentage but to understand that this fraction is trainable and individual.

KEY: Marathon speed is not VO₂max alone; it is VO₂max multiplied by the fraction you can sustain and translated through economy, and all three can change with training.

### Same pace, different internal effort

The same nominal pace can represent different internal effort for different athletes. A 4:15 min/km pace might be a comfortable steady effort for one runner and close to threshold for another. Even for the same athlete, that pace represents a different internal load at 7 a.m. in a cool Ooty morning than at 7 a.m. on a humid Chennai beach road. Heat, humidity, hills, wind, sleep and accumulated training fatigue all shift the fraction of capacity required.

This is why pace prescriptions should be anchored to current fitness and adjusted for conditions. A pace table is a starting point; heart rate, perceived effort and environmental data tell the coach whether the prescription is being expressed as intended.

MYTH: My marathon pace is a fixed number I should hold regardless of the weather. || Marathon pace is an effort level expressed as a speed; in heat and humidity the same effort produces a slower pace, and holding the cool-weather pace raises the internal cost.

### Performance-based anchors

VDOT, developed by Jack Daniels and presented in Daniels' Running Formula, is an established example of converting recent race performance into training intensities rather than treating VO₂max alone as the pace engine. Because it starts from what the athlete has actually run, it automatically includes the combined effect of VO₂max, fractional utilisation and economy as expressed in that race. This is a strength that AKURA respects and discusses in Part VII.

What a performance anchor cannot show on its own is which of those factors is responsible for the result. Two runners with the same race time may reach it with very different physiology, and they may need different training to improve. AKURA's interest is in adding that explanatory layer without discarding the clarity of performance-based prescription.

FIELD: Marathon-effort calibration run ; choose a flat, measured route ; warm up 15 min easy ; run 3 × 5 km at goal marathon pace with 2 min easy jog between ; record pace, average heart rate, RPE, temperature and humidity for each block ; compare heart rate and RPE drift between blocks ; repeat in cooler and hotter conditions to see how the internal cost of the same pace changes.

### Training the fraction

Fractional utilisation responds to training that develops sustained aerobic work: consistent easy volume, threshold sessions, and marathon-specific runs that hold goal effort for long periods. Improvements in mitochondrial density, capillary supply and the ability to use fat at moderate intensities help an athlete run faster before carbohydrate use and lactate accumulation rise steeply. These adaptations take months and years, which is one reason experienced marathon runners often improve their marathon times long after their 5K times have plateaued.

For coaches, this means marathon potential cannot be judged from a VO₂max test alone. A young athlete with a high laboratory value but little aerobic history may need several years of patient development before that ceiling becomes a fast marathon. An older athlete with a moderate value but deep aerobic conditioning may already be using a large share of their capacity.

COACH: When an athlete's marathon pace feels unexpectedly hard, check conditions before questioning fitness. Record temperature, humidity, sleep and the previous week's load; if these are unfavourable, the solution may be adjusting pace to effort rather than concluding that fitness has declined.

CHECK: What evidence do you have that your current marathon pace reflects your present fitness and today's conditions, rather than an old race or a hoped-for target?

## 35. Why Marathon Pace Fails Late

Many runners describe the marathon as “going well until 32 km”. The collapse feels sudden, but many late-race collapses begin long before the athlete feels the problem. By the time the legs refuse and pace falls by 20 or 30 seconds per kilometre, the causes have usually been accumulating for more than an hour. Understanding these hidden costs changes how athletes pace, fuel and prepare.

### The hidden costs of the first half

Starting too fast raises carbohydrate use and thermal strain. At higher intensities the body relies more heavily on carbohydrate, so even a modestly aggressive first half draws down glycogen faster than planned. Higher intensity also produces more heat, which must be dissipated through sweating and skin blood flow. Inadequate fuelling reduces available substrate. Dehydration reduces plasma volume and can increase cardiovascular strain, so heart rate climbs for the same pace. Muscular fatigue changes mechanics: ground contact lengthens, the stride becomes less springy, and the oxygen cost of running rises.

None of these changes is dramatic on its own at 15 km. Together they create a debt that is repaid, with interest, in the final 10 km.

KEY: Marathon pacing is not merely a stopwatch problem. It is a physiological budgeting problem.

### The physiological budget

It is useful to think of a marathon as a fixed budget that can be spent in different ways. Every second gained early by running above sustainable pace is purchased with glycogen, fluid, heat tolerance and muscular resilience that will be needed later.

|Budget line|What spends it|How to protect it|
|Pacing|Fast start, surges, hills taken too hard|Even or slightly negative split by effort|
|Fuel|High intensity, low intake, poor gut tolerance|Planned carbohydrate intake, practised in training|
|Fluid|Sweat loss, heat, humidity|Individual drinking plan based on sweat testing|
|Heat|Air temperature, humidity, sun, intensity|Heat acclimation, cooling, pace adjustment|
|Mechanics|Accumulated muscle damage, downhills|Long runs, strength work, durable footwear choice|

### Fuel and fluid, practised not improvised

Carbohydrate intake during long events is well supported by sports-nutrition research, and guidance has become more individual. A commonly used summary, associated with work by Asker Jeukendrup, links recommended intake to event duration and the athlete's trained gut tolerance.

|Event duration|Commonly recommended carbohydrate|Notes|
|Under about 1 hour|Little or none needed; mouth rinse may help|Performance rarely fuel-limited|
|About 1–2 hours|Up to about 30 g per hour|Simple sources are usually sufficient|
|About 2–2.5 hours|Up to about 60 g per hour|Gut training improves tolerance|
|Beyond about 2.5 hours|Up to about 90 g per hour|Usually multiple transportable carbohydrates|

These are ranges, not targets to be reached on race day for the first time. The gut adapts to regular carbohydrate intake during training, and an athlete who has never taken 60 g per hour in a long run should not attempt it first in a race. Fluid should be individual too: the aim is to limit excessive dehydration without overdrinking, which carries its own risk of hyponatraemia. Sweat rates in Indian summer and humid coastal conditions can be substantial, so pre- and post-run body mass checks are valuable.

MYTH: Hitting the wall is purely a matter of mental toughness. || Late-race slowing usually reflects measurable causes such as early overpacing, carbohydrate depletion, dehydration, heat strain and muscular fatigue; mental skills matter, but they cannot replace an empty fuel tank.

FIELD: Fuel and fluid rehearsal long run ; weigh the athlete nude or in minimal clothing before the run ; run 28–32 km including the final 8–10 km at marathon effort ; take carbohydrate on the race schedule and log grams per hour ; log all fluid consumed ; weigh again after towelling dry ; calculate body-mass change adjusted for fluid intake ; note gut comfort, heart rate drift and pace in the final 10 km.

### Durability as its own quality

The late-race story points to a quality that short-distance speed does not capture: the ability to maintain performance as exercise duration and fatigue increase. Recent sports-science work, including discussion by Maunder and colleagues in 2021 and by Andrew Jones and colleagues in studies of runners at sub-two-hour marathon pace, has highlighted durability or physiological resilience as an important addition to the traditional three-factor model. Research in this area is developing, and measurement protocols are still being standardised.

AEI should eventually estimate an athlete's durability separately from short-distance speed. A runner whose threshold and economy hold steady after two hours is a very different marathon prospect from one whose values degrade quickly, even if both share the same 10K time.

A practical sign of early overspending is a heart rate that rises faster than expected during the first half despite steady pace, or a perceived effort that already feels like threshold at 15 km. Athletes can be taught to recognise these signals and to adjust immediately, because a small correction at 15 km is far cheaper than a forced slowdown at 35 km.

COACH: Use the first 10 km of every marathon as a discipline exercise. Agree a target effort and a ceiling heart rate before the start, and treat any early pace faster than plan as a withdrawal from the final 10 km budget rather than “time in the bank”.

CHECK: In your last long race, where did you spend your budget: pacing, fuel, fluid, heat or mechanics? Which line would you protect first next time?

## 36. The Last 10K

In a well-paced marathon, the last 10 km is where training becomes visible. Up to 30 km, many athletes look similar; after it, their paths separate. The final 10 km is where the interaction of fitness and durability becomes visible, and it provides coaches with some of the most honest information they will ever collect about an athlete.

### Speed without durability

An athlete with excellent 5K speed but insufficient long-run conditioning may possess a high short-distance performance index but a weaker marathon profile. Their aerobic capacity and threshold are good, but these qualities are not yet protected against the fatigue of hours of running. By contrast, a runner with a modest 5K time but years of consistent volume may lose very little pace in the final 10 km and finish far higher up the results than their short-distance times would suggest.

Neither athlete is simply “better”. They have different profiles, and they need different training. The first needs long runs, marathon-specific sessions and fuelling practice; the second may benefit from more speed and threshold work to lift the ceiling that their durability protects.

KEY: The final 10 km separates two qualities that short races blend together: how fast an athlete can run, and how well that speed survives fatigue.

### Distance-specific expression

This suggests a useful AEI concept: distance-specific expression. The athlete should not receive one permanent number for all distances. A 5K profile, 10K profile, half-marathon profile and marathon profile can reveal where endurance breaks down. If an athlete's 5K and 10K performances imply a certain level, but the half-marathon and marathon imply progressively lower levels, the gap points towards durability, fuelling or heat tolerance rather than aerobic capacity.

|Profile|Mainly tests|What a relative weakness may suggest|
|5K|Aerobic power, high-intensity tolerance|Limited top-end aerobic capacity|
|10K|Aerobic power, threshold|Threshold development needed|
|Half marathon|Threshold, early durability|Sustained-intensity conditioning|
|Marathon|Fractional utilisation, fuel, heat, durability|Long-run, fuelling or thermal limits|

The difference between these profiles is itself useful information. A flat profile across distances suggests balanced development. A profile that falls steeply with distance suggests the athlete has speed to spare but lacks endurance-specific resilience. A profile that is strong at the long end but weaker at 5K suggests a durable athlete who may need to raise the ceiling.

MYTH: One performance number should describe an athlete across every distance. || Race-equivalence models are useful guides, but individual athletes often deviate from them; the pattern of deviation across distances is diagnostic information in its own right.

### Reading the final 10 km

Coaches can analyse the last 10 km with simple tools. Compare the average pace of 30–40 km with the average of 10–30 km. Compare heart rate at the same pace in the early and late race. Look at whether the athlete slowed gradually or suddenly; a gradual fade often reflects heat or cumulative fatigue, while a sudden drop may point to glycogen depletion or cramping. Record conditions, fuel taken and any gastrointestinal problems alongside the numbers.

FIELD: Last-10K durability analysis ; export race splits and heart rate ; calculate mean pace for 10–30 km and for 30–40 km ; calculate percentage pace change ; compare mean heart rate for the same segments ; annotate temperature, humidity, fuel intake and any stops ; repeat after every marathon and key long run to build a personal durability trend.

The last 10 km also carries emotional and tactical weight. Athletes who have practised finishing long runs at marathon effort know how that fatigue feels and are less likely to panic when it arrives. Those who have only ever run long runs at easy pace may interpret normal late-race discomfort as disaster and slow more than their physiology requires. Specific preparation therefore trains both body and judgement.

Over a career, the pattern of final-10 km performance becomes one of the most useful records an athlete can keep. It shows whether durability is improving from one marathon cycle to the next, independent of course, weather and finishing time. In AKURA's terms, it is an early, practical estimate of the AEI-D Durability domain.

COACH: Build at least two long runs per block that finish with the final 8–10 km at marathon effort. They rehearse the exact situation that decides races, and the pace and heart-rate change in those final kilometres is an early indicator of marathon readiness long before race day.

CHECK: If you plotted your 5K, 10K, half-marathon and marathon performances as equivalent levels, where would the line drop, and what would that drop tell you about your next training priority?

PART VII — VDOT AND AEI

PARTINTRO: Good coaching frameworks turn complexity into decisions. Jack Daniels' VDOT is one of the clearest examples in running: it converts a recent performance into a measure of current ability and a set of training intensities. This part begins by describing what VDOT does and why it has earned its place. It then explains why AKURA proposes a different, broader layer, the AKURA Endurance Index, which asks what physiological systems support performance, how the athlete responds to environment and training, and how confident we are in each measurement. AEI is presented as a research prototype that sits beside, not above, established tools, with separate Fitness, Readiness and Trend views.

## 37. What VDOT Does

Any honest discussion of a new endurance index must start by acknowledging what already works. VDOT, developed by the American coach and exercise physiologist Jack Daniels and set out in Daniels' Running Formula, is an established running-performance framework that represents current running ability and provides training paces from recent or estimated race performance. It has been used by coaches and runners around the world for decades, and its enduring popularity reflects a real achievement: it makes training intensity simple, consistent and actionable.

### Performance first

The central idea of VDOT is that a recent race result already contains a great deal of physiological information. A runner's 10K time reflects their aerobic capacity, the fraction of it they can sustain, and their running economy, all expressed together under race conditions. Rather than requiring laboratory testing, VDOT uses that performance to estimate a single value of current running ability. Resources associated with Daniels' methods describe VDOT as a measure of current running ability and distinguish it from laboratory-measured VO₂max. Two runners can have similar VO₂max values yet different VDOT values because economy, threshold and other factors differ.

KEY: VDOT is a measure of current running ability derived from performance, not a laboratory measurement of VO₂max.

### From ability to training intensity

VDOT is valuable because it translates performance into actionable training intensity. From one value, a runner receives guidance for different kinds of training and estimates of equivalent performances at other distances. Daniels organises training into clearly defined categories, each with a distinct purpose.

|Category|Broad purpose in Daniels' system|
|Easy (E)|Aerobic development, recovery, volume|
|Marathon (M)|Marathon-specific pace and endurance|
|Threshold (T)|Raise sustainable high intensity|
|Interval (I)|Stress aerobic power|
|Repetition (R)|Speed, economy and mechanics|

This book deliberately does not reproduce VDOT pace tables. Coaches and athletes who wish to use VDOT should consult Daniels' Running Formula and its official resources directly, where the method and its assumptions are explained by its author.

MYTH: VDOT is just another name for VO₂max. || VDOT is estimated from race performance and reflects current running ability, including the effects of economy and sustainable intensity; laboratory VO₂max measures maximal oxygen uptake only.

### What AKURA learns from VDOT

The clarity of VDOT offers several lessons. First, a framework should begin with something athletes and coaches can observe: real performance. Second, it should produce decisions, not just descriptions; a number that does not change the next session has limited coaching value. Third, it should be updated as the athlete changes, rather than fixed for a season. Fourth, it should be simple enough to use daily on the track or road.

AKURA should learn from this clarity rather than copy the same purpose. VDOT already does its job well. There is little value in building a second system that merely produces training paces from race times. AKURA's research question is different, and Chapter 38 explains why.

### Using VDOT in the Indian context

Performance-based systems assume that the race used as an anchor was a fair expression of ability. In India, many races are run in heat and humidity, on courses with variable measurement, and sometimes with crowded starts. A coach using VDOT or any similar framework should prefer recent, well-measured, reasonably controlled performances, and should interpret results from very hot races cautiously. This is not a weakness of VDOT; it is a reminder that every input needs context.

### The value of simplicity

Part of VDOT's success is that it is easy to explain. A runner with no laboratory access can enter a recent race result and immediately understand how hard their easy runs, threshold sessions and intervals should be. Coaches working with large groups can assign intensities consistently. As the athlete improves and races faster, the value rises and the training intensities progress with it. This self-correcting link between performance and prescription is one of the framework's most practical features.

Like any model, VDOT relies on assumptions. Its race equivalences represent typical relationships between distances, and individual athletes may be relatively stronger at short or long distances. Daniels himself emphasises that training should be adjusted to the individual and to conditions. Recognising these assumptions is not a criticism; it is how every model should be used, with judgement.

COACH: If your athlete already trains with VDOT, keep it. Use AKURA's measurements to explain why their VDOT is moving or not moving, for example whether an improvement came with better economy, better heat tolerance or simply a cooler race day.

CHECK: Which recent race would you use as your performance anchor today, and was it run in conditions fair enough to represent your current ability?

## 38. Why AKURA Needs a Different Layer

If VDOT works, why propose anything else? The answer lies in the question each framework is designed to answer. VDOT begins primarily with performance: what can the athlete run now, and how should they train as a result? AKURA's proposed Endurance Index begins with a broader research question: what is the athlete's current performance expression, what physiological systems support it, how is the athlete responding to environment and training, and what is the confidence in each measurement?

### Different questions, different tools

A performance-based system is intentionally efficient. It compresses many physiological factors into one ability value and does not need to know which factor produced the result. For daily training prescription, that compression is a strength. For research and long-term athlete development, particularly in an environment as varied as India's, it can hide the very information a coach needs. When an athlete's race times stagnate, the coach wants to know whether the limitation lies in aerobic capacity, economy, threshold, heat tolerance, recovery or durability. A single ability value cannot distinguish between these.

|Feature|VDOT (Daniels)|AEI (AKURA prototype)|
|Primary input|Recent race performance|Performance plus physiology, environment, recovery|
|Main output|Current ability and training paces|Domain profile, fitness, readiness, trend|
|Status|Established, widely used|Research prototype, not validated|
|Environment|Handled through coach judgement|Logged as explicit data|
|Confidence|Implicit in quality of race used|Attached to each measurement|

KEY: VDOT answers “what can this athlete run and how should they train?”; AEI asks “why can they run it, how are they responding, and how sure are we?”

### Beside, not instead of

AEI is therefore not proposed as a replacement for VDOT. It is a research framework that could sit above or beside pace-based systems. An athlete might continue to receive training paces from an established method while AEI tracks whether their economy is improving, whether a heat block changed their cardiovascular response, and whether their durability supports a marathon attempt. In that arrangement the two layers complement each other: one prescribes, the other explains and monitors.

MYTH: A new index must replace older methods to be useful. || Frameworks answering different questions can coexist; AEI is designed to add explanatory and monitoring information, while established pace systems continue to guide daily intensity.

### A conservative first version

The first version should be deliberately conservative: fewer validated claims, more transparent data. It is tempting to produce a striking single score, a 0–100 value on a colourful dashboard. But any number that combines measurements with unvalidated weights may look precise while being scientifically fragile. AEI v0.1 therefore prioritises three principles: keep raw measurements visible; report domains separately until a combined model is validated; and attach a confidence level to every value.

For Indian athletes, the additional layer is particularly relevant. Training may alternate between hot plains, humid coasts and hill stations such as Ooty or Kodaikanal. Travel, exams, work shifts and irregular access to laboratory testing all influence the data. A framework that records environment and confidence explicitly is better suited to this reality than one that assumes stable conditions.

### What the extra layer must earn

An additional layer adds complexity, and complexity has a cost. Athletes must record more data, coaches must interpret more information, and there are more opportunities for error. AEI must therefore earn its place by improving decisions: identifying the limiting system earlier, showing whether an intervention such as a heat block produced a response, flagging when readiness is too low for a key session, or predicting marathon durability more accurately than short-race times alone.

If, after careful study, a domain does not improve decisions, it should be simplified or removed. This is the discipline that separates a research framework from a marketing score. AKURA's aim is not to build the most elaborate index possible, but the most useful one that the evidence supports.

### A practical example

Consider two Indian club runners, each with the same recent 10K time. A performance-based system would give them similar training intensities, and that is a sensible starting point. AEI might then reveal that the first runner's easy pace at a fixed heart rate deteriorates sharply in humid conditions, while the second runner's pace holds steady but their long-run durability is poor. The same pace prescription remains useful for both; what changes is the emphasis of their next training block, heat preparation for one and marathon-specific endurance for the other.

COACH: Before adding any new metric to an athlete's profile, write down the decision it could change. If you cannot name a decision, record the metric for research but do not let it influence training yet.

CHECK: When your performance stalls, which question would help you more today: “what pace should I train at?” or “which system is limiting me?” Which tool do you currently use to answer each?

## 39. AEI Architecture

A framework needs structure before it needs numbers. The proposed AEI architecture has two levels, reflecting a distinction that experienced coaches already make intuitively: what an athlete is capable of, and what they can express today. Separating these levels prevents two common errors, over-reacting to a single bad day and ignoring warning signs because the athlete is fit.

### Level one: AEI-Fitness

AEI-Fitness describes relatively stable performance capacity: recent race performance, aerobic markers, threshold characteristics, economy and endurance durability. These qualities change over weeks and months in response to training, not overnight. Fitness is the athlete's current engine.

### Level two: AEI-Readiness

AEI-Readiness describes today's ability to express that fitness: sleep, HRV trend, resting heart rate, fatigue, recent load, soreness, illness flags and environmental stress. These signals can change daily. A fit athlete who has slept badly after an overnight train journey, trained in 38 °C heat for three days and developed a sore throat may be unable to express their fitness in a hard session, even though their engine is unchanged.

|Level|Time scale|Main inputs|Coaching question|
|AEI-Fitness|Weeks to months|Races, threshold, economy, aerobic markers, durability|What can this athlete do?|
|AEI-Readiness|Days|Sleep, HRV trend, RHR, fatigue, soreness, load, illness, heat|How much can they express today?|

KEY: Fitness is the engine; readiness is whether the engine can be used fully today. AEI keeps them separate so that one does not distort the other.

### The closed loop

The conceptual loop is: performance + physiology + environment + recovery → AEI → training prescription → actual session → new data → updated AEI. Each training session is therefore both an intervention and a measurement. The planned session reflects the current AEI; the executed session, with its pace, heart rate, perceived effort, conditions and completion status, becomes new evidence that updates the profile.

1) Collect performance, physiological, environmental and recovery data.
2) Update domain values and confidence levels in AEI.
3) Generate a training prescription, reviewed by the coach.
4) Record what the athlete actually did and how it felt.
5) Compare planned and actual responses, then update AEI.

This loop is what separates a research system from a static test report. A test tells the coach where the athlete was; a loop shows how the athlete is changing and whether training is working.

### No weights before validation

No component should be given a final weight until validated on real athlete data. It would be easy to declare that performance counts for 40%, economy for 15% and recovery for 10%, and to produce a neat total. But such weights would be guesses, and guesses presented as numbers can mislead coaches and athletes. Until longitudinal data show which components genuinely predict performance and injury risk in the populations AKURA serves, each component should be reported separately with its confidence.

MYTH: A single combined score is always more useful than several separate numbers. || A combined score is only as good as its weights; with unvalidated weights, separate domain values with clear confidence levels give coaches more honest and more usable information.

FIELD: Setting up the AEI loop for one athlete ; record a recent standardised race or time trial as the performance anchor ; record easy pace at a fixed heart rate on a flat route ; log daily sleep, morning resting heart rate, HRV if available, fatigue and soreness ; log temperature and humidity for every session ; after each key session, record planned versus actual pace, heart rate and RPE ; review the loop weekly with the coach.

### Why two levels matter in practice

In the proposed AKURA ecosystem, the readiness layer also connects to AISRi, the daily training-decision layer, while AEI-Fitness informs longer-term planning. Keeping the interfaces separate makes it clear which information is driving which decision. Many training errors come from mixing the two levels. An athlete who feels flat for three days may conclude they have lost fitness and train harder to compensate, deepening their fatigue. Another athlete may feel excellent one morning after a rest day and attempt a session far beyond their current fitness. By keeping fitness and readiness in separate views, AEI encourages the right response to each: patience with readiness fluctuations, and evidence before revising fitness.

COACH: Keep a simple rule when the two levels disagree. If fitness is high and readiness is low, protect the athlete and modify the day. If readiness is high but fitness is low, do not mistake a good day for a breakthrough; progress the plan as designed.

CHECK: Can you describe your current fitness and your readiness today as two separate statements? Which one are you more likely to confuse with the other?

## 40. Proposed AEI Domains

The two-level architecture needs content. A research prototype can store ten domains, each describing one part of the endurance system that earlier chapters of this book explained: performance, aerobic capacity, threshold, economy, oxygen transport, cardiovascular response, recovery, heat adaptation, altitude response and durability. Together they form a map of the athlete rather than a single verdict.

### The ten domains

|Domain|Primary examples|Interpretation|Confidence note|
|AEI-P Performance|5K, 10K, half-marathon, marathon results|Current race expression|High when recent and standardised|
|AEI-A Aerobic|VO₂-related tests, submaximal HR–pace|Aerobic capacity|Protocol dependent|
|AEI-T Threshold|Lactate or threshold pace|Sustainable high intensity|Protocol dependent|
|AEI-E Economy|O₂ cost at a fixed speed|Movement efficiency|Needs repeatability|
|AEI-O Oxygen Transport|Hb mass, Hb concentration|O₂ transport context|Hb mass needs specialist testing|
|AEI-C Cardiovascular|HR, stroke-volume-related measures|Circulatory response|Context dependent|
|AEI-R Recovery|Sleep, HRV trend, RHR, fatigue|Today's expression capacity|Baseline dependent|
|AEI-H Heat Adaptation|Heat exposure, thermal response|Heat adaptation|Environment must be logged|
|AEI-X Altitude Response|Sleeping altitude, Hb mass response|Hypoxic adaptation|Dose dependent|
|AEI-D Durability|Long-run degradation|Marathon-specific resilience|Needs standardised protocol|

KEY: The ten AEI domains are a structured way to store evidence about an athlete; they are not yet validated scores.

### Domains, not scores

These are domains, not yet validated scores. Each should have raw measurements, normalisation rules, confidence and missing-data handling. Raw measurements must remain visible so that a coach can always trace a conclusion back to the test that produced it. Normalisation rules describe how a value is compared, whether against the athlete's own history or, where appropriate, a reference population. Confidence reflects how reliable the measurement is: a laboratory economy test repeated twice under controlled conditions deserves more confidence than a single estimate from a wrist device. Missing-data handling defines what happens when a domain has no recent measurement, so that absence of data is never mistaken for a low value.

Some domains overlap. Aerobic capacity, threshold and economy all contribute to performance, and recovery influences how every other domain is expressed on the day. Oxygen transport and altitude response are related but distinct: one describes the current state of the blood, the other how it changed after hypoxic exposure. Heat adaptation connects to the cardiovascular domain through plasma volume and heart-rate responses. Recording these domains separately allows the research programme to study their relationships rather than assuming them.

### Not every athlete needs every domain

A club runner in Coimbatore may have reliable performance, recovery and heat data but no laboratory tests. A national-camp athlete may have regular haemoglobin mass and lactate testing. AEI should work in both cases by reporting what is known, with confidence, and clearly marking what is not. The minimum useful profile is often performance, recovery, a simple submaximal HR–pace marker and durability from long runs; laboratory domains add depth when available.

MYTH: A polished 0–100 score is more scientific than a table of separate values. || A beautiful 0–100 score can be scientifically misleading if its weights are arbitrary; transparent domain values with stated confidence are more honest until a combined model has been validated.

FIELD: Building a first domain profile ; list the ten domains ; for each, write the most recent measurement, date and method ; mark confidence as high, moderate or low ; mark missing domains clearly as not measured ; choose the two missing domains that would most change coaching decisions ; plan how and when to measure them.

Confidence deserves its own display. A domain value measured last week in a laboratory and one estimated six months ago from a single field session should not look identical on a dashboard. Simple labels, such as high, moderate and low confidence, together with the date of the last measurement, allow coaches to weigh each domain appropriately.

### How domains link back to the book

Each domain corresponds to physiology explained earlier. AEI-A and AEI-T draw on the chapters on VO₂max, lactate and threshold. AEI-E reflects running economy. AEI-O and AEI-X connect to haemoglobin mass, red-cell adaptation and altitude. AEI-C and AEI-H link to the heart, plasma volume and heat acclimation. AEI-R reflects recovery monitoring, and AEI-D draws on the marathon chapters in Part VI. This mapping keeps AEI grounded in established physiology rather than invented categories, and it makes clear which scientific literature each domain should be validated against.

When a domain changes, the coach should be able to ask which physiological mechanism might explain the change and which intervention preceded it. A domain that cannot be linked to a mechanism, a measurement and a decision is not yet ready to be part of the index.

COACH: Resist the pressure to fill every domain. An honest profile with four well-measured domains is more useful than a complete profile with six guessed values. Ask the athlete to help keep the raw data clean, because they are the source of most daily inputs.

CHECK: Which AEI domains can you measure reliably today, which require help from a laboratory or clinician, and which missing domain matters most for your next goal race?

## 41. AEI-Fitness

AEI-Fitness should answer one question: “What can this athlete currently do under standardised conditions?” It is the slow-moving, evidence-heavy layer of the index, the description of the engine that training has built. Getting it right matters because fitness is the foundation for goal-setting, race selection and long-term planning.

### The performance anchor

The strongest anchor is recent performance under reasonably controlled conditions. Just as VDOT begins with performance, AEI-Fitness gives recent, well-measured results the greatest influence. A certified-course half marathon on a cool morning, or a carefully run time trial on a measured track, provides a clear expression of current capacity. A hot, crowded race or an estimated course distance provides weaker evidence and should carry lower confidence.

### Supporting measures

Supporting measures can include threshold pace, VO₂-related testing, running economy, long-run durability and physiological response. These do not replace performance; they help explain it and fill the gaps between races. An athlete who races only three or four times a year can still show fitness progression through a stable submaximal marker, such as easy pace at a fixed heart rate on the same route in similar conditions, or through a repeated threshold session.

|Input|Role in AEI-Fitness|Update frequency|
|Recent race or time trial|Primary anchor|Every valid race or trial|
|Threshold session or test|Sustainable intensity marker|Every 4–8 weeks|
|Submaximal HR–pace|Aerobic trend marker|Weekly, same route and conditions|
|Running economy test|Efficiency marker|When laboratory access permits|
|Long-run durability|Marathon-specific resilience|Key long runs|

KEY: AEI-Fitness is anchored in real performance and supported by physiological markers; it describes capacity, not how the athlete feels today.

### Slow to change, by design

Fitness should update more slowly than daily readiness. A single bad night's sleep should not erase months of training adaptation. Conversely, a single breakthrough race should not permanently redefine the athlete without confirmation. A new personal best in ideal weather with a strong pacing group may overstate typical capacity; a disappointing race in 34 °C heat may understate it. AEI-Fitness should respond to consistent evidence across several data points, and it should record the conditions of each so that environmental effects are not mistaken for changes in fitness.

|Feature|Fitness|Readiness|
|Question|What can the athlete do?|How much can they express today?|
|Time scale|Weeks to months|Hours to days|
|Primary evidence|Races, tests, long-run durability|Sleep, HRV trend, RHR, soreness, load|
|Typical change|Gradual, training-driven|Rapid, recovery- and life-driven|
|Main use|Goals, race choice, training phase|Daily session decision|

MYTH: One brilliant race proves a new fitness level. || A single result may reflect ideal conditions, pacing or course; fitness should be confirmed by consistent evidence from races, tests and training markers.

FIELD: Standardised fitness check ; select a flat measured loop or 400 m track ; record temperature, humidity and time of day ; warm up 15 min easy ; run 20 min at a fixed heart rate near the top of the easy zone and record average pace ; after 5 min recovery, complete a controlled 3 km or 5 km time trial ; repeat every 4–6 weeks under similar conditions and compare.

Fitness also has a direction. An athlete returning from injury may have a modest current anchor but a steeply rising trend, while a veteran may hold a high anchor that is slowly declining. Both facts matter for planning, which is why AEI-Fitness should always be read alongside the trend view described in Chapter 43.

### Fitness is multidimensional

Although AEI-Fitness may one day be summarised in a single figure, it is built from several components. Two athletes with the same performance anchor may have different supporting profiles: one with excellent economy but modest threshold, another with a high aerobic ceiling but poor durability. Keeping these components visible allows training to target the actual limiter. In the prototype stage, AEI-Fitness is best presented as the performance anchor plus a short list of supporting markers, each with its date and confidence.

For Indian athletes who train through long hot seasons, conditions deserve special attention. Fitness markers collected in April and May may look weaker than those collected in December, even when underlying fitness has improved. Recording temperature and humidity with every standardised test allows AKURA to compare like with like and, over time, to model environmental effects directly.

COACH: Update your athlete's fitness picture after patterns, not events. When a surprising result appears, ask whether conditions, pacing or measurement explain it, and look for a second confirming data point before changing training zones or goals.

CHECK: What is your current performance anchor, how old is it, and what supporting marker would confirm that your fitness has really changed since then?

## 42. AEI-Readiness

Readiness asks a different question: “How well can the athlete express current fitness today?” The engine may be strong, but on a given morning it may be cold, under-fuelled, overheated or strained. AEI-Readiness is the daily layer that helps the coach decide whether the planned session should go ahead, be modified or be replaced.

### Inputs, not verdicts

A readiness layer can use HRV trend, resting heart rate, sleep quantity and quality, subjective fatigue, muscle soreness, recent training load and environmental strain. No single metric should dominate. Heart-rate variability is a useful, context-dependent signal of autonomic state, and research such as that by Plews and colleagues has shown the value of interpreting it as a rolling trend rather than a single morning value. Resting heart rate, sleep and subjective wellbeing add further perspectives. Simple self-reported measures of fatigue and soreness are often as informative as device data and should never be dismissed.

Environmental strain belongs in readiness because it changes the cost of the day's session. A threshold workout planned for 6 a.m. may be appropriate in cool conditions and inappropriate in a humid heatwave, even for a well-recovered athlete.

|Signal|What it may reflect|Common confounder|
|HRV trend|Autonomic balance and recovery|Alcohol, illness, stress, measurement timing|
|Resting heart rate|Fatigue, illness, heat, dehydration|Caffeine, poor sleep, measurement posture|
|Sleep|Recovery opportunity|Travel, shift work, exams, family demands|
|Fatigue and soreness|Perceived recovery|Mood, motivation, life stress|
|Recent load|Accumulated training stress|Unrecorded activity, heat|
|Environmental strain|Cost of today's session|Microclimate, time of day|

KEY: Readiness is a confidence-adjusted judgement built from several signals; no single wearable metric is a diagnosis.

### From signals to decisions

The practical output is not “train hard” or “rest” from one number. It is a confidence-adjusted recommendation: planned session, modified session, easy session or recovery, with coach review when risk signals conflict. When most signals agree that the athlete is well recovered, the planned session proceeds. When some signals are unfavourable, the session can be modified, for example by reducing volume, moving it to a cooler time or converting intervals to steady running. When several signals are poor, an easy session or recovery day is preferable. When signals conflict, or when an illness flag appears, a human review is required.

1) Planned session: signals broadly favourable, no risk flags.
2) Modified session: one or two unfavourable signals or high heat strain.
3) Easy session: several unfavourable signals, no illness flags.
4) Recovery or coach review: illness symptoms, conflicting risk signals or sharp unexplained change.

MYTH: A high readiness score means the athlete is safe to train hard. || Readiness scores cannot detect every problem; symptoms such as chest pain, fever, dizziness or signs of heat illness always override any score and require appropriate medical attention.

FIELD: Morning readiness routine ; on waking, before caffeine, record resting heart rate and HRV in the same posture ; record sleep duration and a 1–5 sleep quality rating ; rate fatigue, soreness and mood 1–5 ; note any illness symptoms ; check forecast temperature and humidity for the session time ; compare with the athlete's 7-day and 28-day norms rather than with other athletes.

Recording the decision itself is equally important. When the coach modifies a session, the reason and the outcome should be logged. Over months, these records show whether readiness-based modifications were justified, and they become part of the data that will eventually be used to validate or revise the readiness model.

### Readiness in real Indian life

Readiness is shaped by more than training. Long commutes, night shifts, examination seasons, family responsibilities, festival travel and poor sleep in hot, humid bedrooms all influence how an athlete arrives at a session. A readiness system designed for Indian athletes should make it easy to record these factors, because they often explain changes that physiological data alone cannot. The coach's knowledge of the athlete's life remains one of the most important readiness inputs.

Readiness data should also be interpreted against each athlete's own baseline. One athlete's normal resting heart rate may be another's warning sign. For this reason, the first few weeks of monitoring are best treated as a baseline-building period, during which recommendations rely more heavily on coach judgement.

COACH: Teach athletes that modifying a session is a skill, not a failure. A hard session moved by one day or shortened by a third on a poor readiness morning usually costs nothing in fitness, while forcing it can cost a week.

CHECK: Which readiness signal do you trust most for yourself, and have you ever checked whether it actually predicts your good and bad training days?

## 43. AEI Trend

A single measurement is a photograph; a trend is a film. A trend is often more informative than a point, because it shows direction, speed of change and response to interventions. Many of the most important coaching questions, whether a heat block worked, whether fitness is still improving, whether fatigue is accumulating, can only be answered by looking across time.

### When the trend and the race disagree

A runner's pace at a fixed heart rate can improve gradually while race performance remains unchanged because durability or pacing is limiting. Without a trend view, the coach might conclude that training has failed. With it, the coach can see that aerobic fitness is rising and look for the real limiter elsewhere, perhaps in the final 10 km of races, in fuelling, or in pacing discipline. The trend therefore protects good training from being abandoned because of one unrepresentative result.

KEY: Trends reveal whether an athlete is changing, and in which system, even when race results have not yet caught up.

### Rolling windows

AEI Trend should therefore display rolling changes rather than only today's score. Useful views include 7-day, 28-day and 90-day trends, with annotations for races, illness, travel, heat blocks and altitude exposure.

|Window|Best for|Typical signals|Caution|
|7-day|Short-term readiness and fatigue|HRV trend, RHR, sleep, soreness, load|Noisy; sensitive to single events|
|28-day|Training-block response|Submaximal HR–pace, threshold, load pattern|Affected by heat or travel within block|
|90-day|Fitness and adaptation|Race anchors, economy, durability, Hb markers|Slow to reveal sudden problems|

Annotations are essential. A falling 28-day HR–pace trend might mean improved fitness, or it might mean the weather cooled. A rising resting heart rate might signal fatigue, or it might coincide with a week of travel to a hot city. By marking races, illness, travel, heat blocks, altitude camps, exams, work shifts and equipment changes, the coach can separate cause from coincidence.

MYTH: A single green number on a dashboard tells you the athlete is on track. || A single number hides direction and context; rolling trends with annotations show what is changing, how fast, and what might be causing it.

### A dashboard that asks questions

A research dashboard should make cause-and-effect questions visible rather than hiding them behind a single green number. Did easy pace at a fixed heart rate improve after the Chennai heat block? Did resting heart rate settle after the athlete returned from Kodaikanal? Did long-run durability improve over the last 90 days while 5K speed stayed flat? A well-designed trend view invites these questions and allows the coach and athlete to test their hypotheses against the data.

For AKURA, trend data are also the raw material for validation. Only by collecting consistent, annotated measurements over months can the research programme learn which domains truly predict performance, which interventions produce meaningful responses, and how Indian athletes adapt to the specific environments in which they train.

FIELD: Monthly trend review ; export 90 days of training, readiness and race data ; plot submaximal HR–pace, resting heart rate, HRV trend and weekly load ; mark races, illness, travel, heat and altitude exposure on the timeline ; identify one improving, one stable and one declining signal ; write a hypothesis explaining each ; agree one training adjustment and one measurement to test it next month.

Trend windows should also match the question. A coach deciding whether to hold tomorrow's intervals needs the 7-day view. A coach evaluating a four-week heat block needs the 28-day view with the block clearly annotated. A coach planning the next marathon cycle needs the 90-day view and the history of previous cycles. Showing all three windows side by side prevents short-term noise from being mistaken for long-term change, and prevents slow long-term decline from being hidden by a good week.

COACH: Review trends with the athlete, not just for them. When athletes see their own HR–pace line improving through a hard humid summer, they understand their training, and understanding improves adherence far more than any score.

CHECK: Looking at your last 90 days, which signal has changed most, what annotation might explain it, and what measurement would confirm or reject that explanation?
