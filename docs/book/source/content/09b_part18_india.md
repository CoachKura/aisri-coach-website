PART XVIII — INDIA'S ELITE RUNNERS: AN AEI ANALYSIS

PARTINTRO: This Part turns the AKURA lens on India's own best distance runners. It maps where they come from, profiles the men and women who have carried the national records from 1500 m to the marathon, tracks how those records have moved or stood still, and measures how far they sit from world level. It then asks what the ten AEI domains can and cannot say about them from public information alone, and ends with a practical roadmap. Every fact comes from verified public sources; where the evidence runs out, the text says so and offers hypotheses rather than conclusions.

## STORY: The Soldiers' Road

It is a little after dawn at an Army recruitment rally somewhere in the Deccan. The ground is dry and the air still cool. Several hundred young men in vests and cheap canvas shoes stand in lines, waiting for the 1.6 km run that will decide whether they get a uniform. Most of them are farmers' sons. Almost none of them have ever heard of a lactate threshold, a steeplechase barrier or a place called Colorado Springs. They are here for a salary, a pension, a bit of respect back in the village.

Somewhere in a line like this, in the years that follow, India's distance-running future is being quietly sorted.

That scene is imagined. What follows is not.

### A boy from Beed

Avinash Sable was born on 13 September 1994 in Mandwa village, in the Beed district of Maharashtra. His parents were farmers. As a boy he is reported to have run about 6 km to school. After Class 12 he joined the Army, in 5 Mahar Regiment.

He did not join to become a runner. His postings took him to the Siachen glacier, to Sikkim, and to the Rajasthan desert, where temperatures have been reported at up to 50 °C. He came to competitive running only in 2015, through Army cross country. Around 2017, an Army coach named Amrish Kumar saw him and moved him into the 3000 m steeplechase, an event he had never run.

Within two years he was in a World Championships final. He went on to break the national record ten times, win Asian Games gold in 2023 and a Commonwealth Games silver in 2022, and become the first Indian to reach an Olympic steeplechase final, in Paris in 2024.

Pause on the sequence. Farm. Uniform. Glacier. Desert. Cross-country race. A coach who happens to be watching. Nobody designed that path for Avinash Sable. The Army simply put him in the places where it could happen.

### "Only after joining the army"

Four years younger, and far to the north, Gulveer Singh was born on 1 June 1998 in Sirsa village, Aligarh district, Uttar Pradesh, into a farming family. He joined the Army through the sports quota in 2018, in The Grenadiers. He was posted in Arunachal Pradesh, where his duties included patrolling at altitude. He moved from the Army Sports Institute to the national camp. He is now a Junior Commissioned Officer, and he holds the Indian records at 3000 m, 5000 m and 10,000 m.

He has been unusually direct about where his running began.

QUOTE: I truly learned about competitive running only after joining the army. — Gulveer Singh (Olympics.com, quoting an Inspire Institute of Sport interview)

Read that twice. One of the most successful distance runners India has ever produced says that his sport, in any serious sense, began inside a military institution.

### The institute in Pune

The institution has an address. On 1 July 2001 the Army set up the Army Sports Institute in Pune under a programme it called "Mission Olympics". Its Boys Sports Company runs as a joint venture with the Sports Authority of India.

The name turns up again and again in the biographies of India's best distance men. Thonakal Gopi, a Havildar in the Artillery, has been listed by the Army as an ASI Pune athlete. Kheta Ram's pathway ran through it, according to reference sources. Gulveer came through it. Sawan Barwal, who joined the Army in 2019 and now holds the national marathon record, is reported to have trained there under an Army coach earlier in his career.

Look at who these men were before the uniform. Gopi grew up in Sulthan Bathery, in Wayanad in Kerala, about 1,000 m above sea level; his parents farmed rice and ginger, and he joined the Army through the sports quota. Kheta Ram is reported to have grown up in the Barmer desert of Rajasthan and to have run 4 km to school. Nitendra Singh Rawat is from Garur in the hills of Uttarakhand. G. Lakshmanan comes from Sokkurani in Pudukkottai district, Tamil Nadu, "born in a poor family", as The Hindu put it; he first trained with S. Loganathan of the Kavinadu Youth Sports Club, on tar roads and village streets, before the Army took him further. In 2017 he won both the 5000 m and the 10,000 m at the Asian Championships.

Desert, hill, backwater, paddy field. Four corners of the country, one uniform.

### Three soldiers in Rio

In August 2016, all three of India's men in the Olympic marathon were Army runners: Gopi, Kheta Ram and Rawat. All three had qualified at the Mumbai Marathon that January. Before the Games they had trained at altitude in Ooty under Surendra Singh Bhandari, an Army Sports Institute coach. Rawat later credited that camp for his Mumbai run.

QUOTE: I was personally confident after the training we had been put through in the national camp at Ooty by our coach (Surinder Singh) Bhandari. — Nitendra Singh Rawat (Sportskeeda, 2016)

Gopi and Kheta Ram finished 25th and 26th in Rio, one second apart, both in personal bests. It was not a medal. But it was a system result: three men from three states, recruited, housed, paid and coached by the same institution, sent to the same mountain, arriving at the same start line.

The pattern stretches back further than Pune. Shivnath Singh, whose 1978 marathon record stood for 48 years, was an Army man. Hari Chand, who ran the 10,000 m barefoot at the 1976 Olympics and won Asian Games double gold in 1978, joined the Central Reserve Police Force in 1972 and served as an inspector. For half a century, the fastest Indian men have mostly worn a uniform.

### The women's road runs on rails

The women's road looks different. None of the women profiled in this Part came through the Army. Their equivalent has been the Railways, and later the police.

Sudha Singh, born in Raebareli, Uttar Pradesh, in 1986, worked for Central Railway in Mumbai. It was a railway officer who changed her event.

QUOTE: Madhu Shivdutt, my railway officer introduced me to Steeplechase. — Sudha Singh (Mumbai Live)

She won the first women's steeplechase ever held at the Asian Games, in 2010. Lalita Babar, a farmer's daughter from Satara, ran for the Railways and reached the Rio Olympic final. Preeja Sreedharan, from Idukki in Kerala, whose father, a manual labourer, died when she was eight, became a superintendent with Southern Railway and set a 10,000 m national record in 2010 that still stands. Parul Chaudhary took a Western Railway ticket-examiner job in 2015, before the national records and Asian Games medals, and joined the Uttar Pradesh Police as a Deputy Superintendent in 2024. Sunita Rani, the 2002 Asian Games 1500 m champion, joined the Punjab Police through the sports quota.

For these women, the job was the professional contract. It paid the rent, released them to national camps and gave their families a reason to let them keep running.

### The runner without a road

To see what the system gives, look at someone it missed.

Jyoti Gawate, born in 1987 in Parbhani, Maharashtra, became one of India's best women marathoners, with a 2:45:48 in Mumbai in 2019. Until 2015 she was reported to live with her family in a wooden hut. In 2014 she took a job with the Mumbai Police but quit after eight days to protect her training time. Her application to the Railways went unanswered. She trained in Parbhani with her long-time coach, Ravi Raskatla, without access to the national camp, and lived on prize money.

QUOTE: I am still jobless; what more can I do? — Jyoti Gawate (Mid-Day, via shyamgopan.com)

Jyoti's story is the other side of the soldiers' road. The talent was there, and so was the work. The route was not.

### The system as the main character

Put these lives side by side and one thing becomes obvious. The most important figure in Indian distance running is not any single athlete. It is an institution: the Army, the Railways, the police, a Sports Authority of India hostel, a long-serving coach in one town. The villages supply hardy, determined young people in every corner of the country. The institutions decide which of them become international athletes.

That raises harder questions. Where exactly do these pathways reach, and where do they stop? Which states feed which institutions? What does each one actually provide: a salary, a coach, altitude, a race abroad? And how many Jyoti Gawates are running village roads right now, with nobody watching?

To answer those questions, we need a map.

## 90. The Indian Distance Map

Take the night train north out of Pune and, by morning, you could be anywhere on the map of Indian distance running. The Army Sports Institute sits behind you in the cantonment. Ahead lie the villages of Beed and Satara, where children have been reported running kilometres to school; the hostels of Nashik; the sugarcane country around Meerut and Aligarh; the hill districts of Uttarakhand and Himachal; and, far to the south, the Nilgiri mist over Ooty, where half of India's elite seem to have spent a winter. Put a pin in the home village of every Indian who has held a distance record or won an Asian medal in the last twenty years, and a pattern appears. It is not a single region. It is a network of pathways: a uniform, a railway job, a coach who stayed in one town for thirty years, and a mountain camp.

This chapter maps where India's elite distance runners come from and, more importantly, which institutions carried them from village roads to international start lines. The athletes counted here are the ones profiled in Chapters 91 and 92. The map is built only from verified biographies; where a birthplace or employer could not be confirmed, the athlete is placed in the "other" group rather than guessed.

### Pathways, not places

Indian distance running is often described by region: "the Kenya of India" has been claimed for Nashik by its own coach, and hill districts are frequently credited with producing runners. The public evidence suggests a more useful framing. Geography supplies the raw material, usually a rural, physically demanding childhood; institutions supply the conversion. Almost every elite profiled in this Part had both.

### The Army engine

Of the twelve men profiled in the next chapter, ten are, or were, Army soldiers. They include the national record holders in the steeplechase (Avinash Sable), 3000 m, 5000 m and 10,000 m (Gulveer Singh), marathon (Sawan Barwal) and 1500 m (Jinson Johnson). The Army Sports Institute in Pune, set up in July 2001 under the Army's "Mission Olympics" programme, recurs in these biographies again and again.

Two features of this pathway stand out. First, several athletes joined mainly for a secure job and only discovered competitive running after enlisting. Sable came to running through Army cross country in 2015; Gulveer has said that he truly learned competitive running only after joining. Second, the Army recruits from exactly the rural, farming and hill families that also produce hardy young runners, so it acts as a nationwide scouting net even when it is not trying to be one.

KEY: For Indian men, the Army is not one pathway among many; it is the main pathway. Any national talent strategy that ignores the services, or duplicates what they already do, will waste scarce resources.

### Where the women come from

The women's map looks different. No woman profiled here came through the Army. Their equivalent is the Railways, which employed Sudha Singh, Lalita Babar, Preeja Sreedharan and Parul Chaudhary, and the police, which employed Sunita Rani and now Parul. A job is, in effect, the professional contract. Jyoti Gawate, a marathoner from Parbhani who never obtained a job and lived on prize money without national-camp access, shows what happens without one.

Regional clusters matter more for women than for men. Vijender Singh, a SAI coach based in Nashik since 1991, has worked with Kavita Raut, Sanjivani Jadhav and Monika Athare, among others. Satara (Lalita Babar), Beed (Sable) and Buldhana (Poonam Sonune) make rural Maharashtra the densest single region on the women's and men's maps combined.

### The hill districts and the south

Uttarakhand has produced Nitendra Singh Rawat (Garur), Man Singh (Pithoragarh) and Ankita Dhyani, whose village of Maroda sits at about 1,400 m. Himachal Pradesh has produced Sawan Barwal (Mandi) and Seema (Chamba). Western Uttar Pradesh, which is not hilly at all, has produced Gulveer Singh, Parul Chaudhary, Kartik Kumar and K. M. Deeksha. That alone should caution anyone who thinks altitude of birth explains Indian distance talent.

In the south, Kerala supplied Gopi, Jinson Johnson, Preeja Sreedharan and O. P. Jaisha. Tamil Nadu supplied G. Lakshmanan and L. Suriya, both reported to have trained at a youth club in Kavinadu village near Pudukkottai. Tamil Nadu also hosts the most important domestic altitude venue: Ooty, at 2,240 m, where a state high-altitude training centre opened in February 2024 and which a ministry official has described as the preferred choice for middle- and long-distance runners.

COACH: When you scout, look at pathways as well as times. A 16-year-old with a modest 3000 m time who is two years from a services or Railways trial may have a better route to the top than a faster athlete with no job prospect. Help athletes and families understand the sports-quota routes early, and keep training logs that a recruiting coach can read.

### What the map does not show

The map shows who reached the top, not who dropped out. We have no public data on how many talented teenagers from Beed or Chamba never found a coach, a hostel place or a job. Coorg, Dharamshala and Kodaikanal are sometimes mentioned as running regions, but we could not verify any documented elite pathway from them. The map is a record of survivors, and that bias should be kept in mind when drawing lessons from it.

## 91. The Men: From 1500 m to the Marathon

In March 2025, at a track meet called The TEN in San Juan Capistrano, California, an Indian Army soldier finished sixth in a 10,000 m race. The placing barely registered in world athletics news. The time did: 27:00.22. Gulveer Singh, born in 1998 into a farming family in Sirsa village, Aligarh district, had just run the 10,000 m more than a minute faster than the national record that had stood from 2008 until 2024. The Sports Authority of India described him as the third-fastest Asian of all time. Two years earlier the national record had been 28:02.89, set in 2008. Something had changed, and it was not the Aligarh soil.

This chapter profiles India's leading men from 1500 m to the marathon, past and present, and looks for the patterns that link them. All personal bests and dates are from World Athletics profiles, Olympics.com and major news reports. Where a coach or base could not be confirmed, the table says so.

### Pattern 1: the soldier-athlete

Ten of the twelve men in the table served in the Army; Hari Chand served in the CRPF. Only Ajay Kumar Saroj's service affiliation could not be confirmed. The Army provides more than a salary. Sable's postings took him from Siachen to the Rajasthan desert; Gulveer did patrolling duties at altitude in Arunachal Pradesh. These are incidental environmental exposures, not planned training, but they show how physically demanding the pathway is before any coach gets involved.

### Pattern 2: the late discovery

Many of these men did not arrive as polished juniors. Sable joined the Army after Class 12 and took up running through Army cross country in 2015; an Army coach, Amrish Kumar, moved him into the steeplechase around 2017. Within two years he was in a World Championships final. Sawan started as a 1500 m runner at 14 and ran his first marathon at 28. Gopi ran his best marathon at 37. For coaches, this is an encouraging pattern: in Indian men's distance running, the late developer is common, and aerobic development can continue well into the thirties.

KEY: Indian male distance runners often reach elite level late, after enlisting or after years on the track. Talent identification that closes at 17 would have missed several of the country's record holders.

### Pattern 3: the Colorado and Ooty effect

Two altitude hubs dominate the recent men's story. Domestically, the Nilgiris hosted the Rio 2016 marathon group under Surendra Singh Bhandari, Gopi before his 2025 Valencia run, and Sawan's Project 2:09 winters. Internationally, Colorado Springs under the American coach Scott Simmons became the distance hub from 2019, used by Jinson, Sable from 2022 and Gulveer from 2024. Sable also trained at St Moritz before the Paris Olympics.

The timing is striking. The steeplechase record, 1981 to 2018; the 5000 m record, 1992 to 2022; the 10,000 m record, 2008 to 2024; the marathon record, 1978 to 2026. All fell in the era of foreign coaching, planned altitude blocks and racing abroad. Correlation is not proof, and the same era also brought better shoes, better pacing and TOPS funding. But the pattern is consistent across events.

### Pattern 4: records set abroad

Almost every men's national record since 2022 was set at an overseas meet with fast pacing: The TEN and Sound Running in California, Portland, Niigata, Hachioji, Boston indoors, Budapest, the Paris Diamond League and Rotterdam. Indian domestic races rarely offer record pace, deep fields or cool evenings. This matters for selection: an athlete who never races abroad may never discover how fast they can run.

COACH: For a developing senior athlete, plan at least one race a season in conditions built for fast times: cool weather, a deep field and pacing. Treat it as a measurement, not just a competition. A single honest time in good conditions is worth more for planning than several wins in the heat of a domestic meet.

### Pattern 5: fragile bodies at the top

The best men have also paid a physical price. Jinson Johnson, still the 1500 m record holder, described a severe Achilles injury from 2019 and retired in January 2026. Sable injured the ACL and meniscus of his right knee at the Monaco Diamond League in July 2025, missed the Tokyo World Championships and the 2026 Commonwealth Games, and was fourth at the 2026 Asian Games on his comeback. He later reflected that he may have rushed his return. A small elite group means each injury costs the nation a large share of its capacity.

### The marathon depth problem

The men's marathon has more depth than it first appears. In 2025–26, Sawan (2:11:37), Gopi (2:12:23), Man Singh (2:13:25) and A. B. Belliappa (2:14:07) all ran under 2:14:10, and Gopi and Man Singh won Asian Marathon Championships titles in 2017 and 2024. Yet no Indian has ever run under 2:10. In 2019 alone, 293 sub-2:10 performances were recorded worldwide. The gap is not at the top of the Indian list; it is between the top of the Indian list and the world.

MYTH: Indians are track runners, not marathoners. || Shivnath Singh's 2:12:00 in 1978 was a strong mark for its era, and four Indians ran under 2:14:10 in 2025–26. The limiting factor looks more like marathon-specific preparation, racing opportunities and late conversion than any fixed trait.

CHECK: Look at the men's table. Which athlete's pathway most resembles that of a runner you coach? What single element of that pathway, whether altitude, overseas racing or a specialist coach, could you realistically reproduce this year?

## 92. The Women: Steeple to Marathon

At the Rio Olympics in August 2016, a farmer's daughter from Mohi village in Satara district lined up in the final of the 3000 m steeplechase. As a child, Lalita Babar was reported to have run 4 km each way to school and fetched water for a 17-person household in a drought-prone area. At 15 she won Rs 10,000 in prize money in a 10 km road race. A Belarusian coach, Nikolai Snesarev, introduced her to the steeplechase. In the heats in Rio she set a national record of 9:19.76, and in the final she finished tenth, the first Indian to reach an Olympic track final since P. T. Usha in 1984. That record lasted seven years, until a Railways ticket examiner from a sugarcane village near Meerut broke it.

This chapter profiles India's leading women from 1500 m to the marathon. Their story is different from the men's in almost every structural way: different employers, different coaches, different clusters, and records that have either leapt forward or stood still for more than a decade.

### Pattern 1: the job as contract

Where the men have the Army, the women have the Railways and the police. Four of the twelve women in the table held Railways jobs. Parul Chaudhary joined Western Railway in 2015 and became a Deputy Superintendent of Police in Uttar Pradesh in 2024. Sunita Rani joined Punjab Police as a DSP in 2002. The public record contains a stark counter-example in Jyoti Gawate, who reached a 2:45:48 marathon in 2019 while living on prize money, training in Parbhani with her long-time coach and without national-camp access. The job is not a perk. It is what makes full-time training possible.

KEY: For Indian women, a secure job has functioned as the professional contract that allows elite training. The absence of one is a measurable disadvantage, not just a personal hardship.

### Pattern 2: the Snesarev generation

Between 2005 and 2016, one coach shaped an entire generation. Nikolai Snesarev arrived in 2005 to coach Preeja Sreedharan and Kavita Raut; at the 2010 Asian Games in Guangzhou they finished first and second in the 10,000 m, and Sudha Singh won the first women's steeplechase ever held at the Asian Games. He later guided Lalita Babar to the Rio final. Olympics.com has described him as an advocate of natural methods. He left the national set-up in 2019 and died in March 2021 at NIS Patiala, shortly after being re-appointed.

The strength of that generation is also a warning. Three of the seven women's distance records, 10,000 m (2010), marathon (2015) and half marathon (2017), were set by this cohort or its contemporaries and still stand. When a single coach-led group lifts the ceiling and then disperses, the ceiling can stay where it was for a long time.

### Pattern 3: Parul and the new ceiling

The recent women's story is, to a remarkable degree, one athlete. Parul Chaudhary started running barefoot in a school 800 m race in 2011, with no practice, at her father's suggestion. She trained on paths between sugarcane fields. From 2022 to 2026 she set national records at 3000 m (8:57.19, the first Indian woman under nine minutes), 5000 m (15:10.35 and then 15:04.26) and the steeplechase (9:15.31, 9:13.39, 9:12.46 and 9:08.67). She won Asian Games 5000 m gold and steeplechase silver in Hangzhou, and two bronze medals in Nagoya in 2026. She trains with Scott Simmons in Colorado Springs.

Her rise tells us that the ceiling is not fixed. It also tells us how thin the depth is behind her: at the 2026 Asian Games, Ankita Dhyani ran 9:19.24 for fourth, a large personal best, but still more than ten seconds behind.

COACH: If you coach a promising woman at 800 m or 1500 m, do not assume that is her event. Parul moved from 800 m to 5000 m and the steeplechase; Lalita moved from road races to the steeplechase; Jaisha moved from 1500 m to the marathon. Test the athlete across distances every season and keep the door to longer events open.

### Pattern 4: event switching and hills

Event switching is common and often successful among Indian women, and the new generation adds a geographical twist. Ankita Dhyani grew up at about 1,400 m in Pauri Garhwal and, as a girl, ran hill roads alongside young men training for Army fitness tests. Her coach at Bhopal has credited that upbringing with giving her an altitude background. Seema, from Reta village in Chamba, grew up running barefoot while herding cattle, came through a SAI hostel and won 10,000 m bronze at the 2026 Asian Games, only the third Indian woman to medal in the event and the first in 16 years.

### Pattern 5: the stalled long events

The 10,000 m record has stood since 2010 and the marathon since 2015. The closest recent attempts, Parul's 32:02.08 and Seema's 32:02.43 on the track and Seema's 1:11:23 in the half marathon, are close but not through. At the 2026 national marathon championship in New Delhi the winning time was 2:41:15, and no Indian woman met the Asian Games qualifying mark of 2:31:52. The women's marathon is where the gap to the world is largest, as Chapter 94 will show.

## 93. Records Over Time

The 1500 m records move differently from the longer events. Sunita Rani's 4:06.03 from the 2002 Asian Games stood for 19 years, and the two records since have improved it by little more than a second in total. On the men's side, Jinson Johnson has described breaking a 23-year-old 1500 m record at the 2018 Commonwealth Games before setting the current 3:35.24 in Berlin in 2019, which still stands. The middle distances have not yet had their Sable or Gulveer.

### What stagnation tells us

Long flat periods are rarely about a lack of talent. The same villages, the same Army recruitment and the same Railways quotas existed throughout. What the flat periods share is the absence of three things: a coach with a clear long-term method, sustained altitude or specialist training blocks, and access to fast races. When all three were present, for the Snesarev group from 2005 and for the Colorado Springs and Ooty groups from about 2019, records fell. When they disappeared, as after the Snesarev group dispersed, records stood.

There is a second, less comfortable lesson. Some flat periods may reflect the fact that the old records were genuinely strong. Shivnath Singh's 2:12:00 and Preeja Sreedharan's 31:50.47 were not easy targets, and the athletes who approached them later did so with far better equipment and support.

COACH: Look at your own club or state records the same way. List them with their dates. Records that have not moved in more than ten years point to a gap in your system: a missing coach, a missing training environment or a missing race opportunity. Records that are moving fast tell you which parts of your system are working.

### What breakthroughs tell us

Breakthroughs cluster around specific people and environments rather than spreading evenly. Sable and Gulveer account for almost all recent men's track records; Parul for almost all recent women's records. This is a fragile structure. A single injury, as Sable's 2025 knee injury showed, can remove a nation's main record-breaker for a season or more. A healthy system should produce several athletes pressing the same record at once.

## 94. How Far from the World?

At the Tata Mumbai Marathon in January 2026, the first Indian man home, Kartik Karkera, crossed the line in 2:19:55. The overall winner, Tadu Abate of Ethiopia, had already finished in 2:09:55, ten minutes earlier. At the Delhi Half Marathon in 2025, the women's race was won by Kenya's Lilian Rengeruk in 1:07:20; the first Indian woman, Seema, ran 1:11:23, a strong run just outside the national record. These races are held in Indian conditions, on Indian roads, in front of Indian crowds, and the gap is visible to anyone standing at the finish. The question for this chapter is how big that gap really is at the very top, event by event, when India's best-ever marks are set against the best in the world.

### Reading the gaps

Three patterns jump out.

First, men's track events are closest. Gulveer Singh's 27:00.22 is about 3.1% from the world 10,000 m record, and Sable's 8:09.91 is about 3.8% from the steeplechase record. In practical terms, 3% in a 10,000 m is about 49 seconds; at world-record pace that is about three-quarters of a lap. It is a big gap in world terms, but it is one that Indian athletes have been closing quickly.

Second, the gaps widen with distance, especially for women. The women's steeplechase (4.6%) is close to the men's track events, thanks almost entirely to Parul Chaudhary. But the women's 10,000 m (10.2%), 3000 m (10.5%) and marathon (19.1%) are far behind. A 19% gap in the marathon is about 25 minutes.

Third, the marathon is India's widest gap for both sexes. The men's marathon gap (9.1%) is more than twice the men's track gaps.

KEY: India's distance gap to the world is not one number. It is smallest in men's track events and the women's steeplechase, about 3–5%, and largest in the marathons and the women's long track events, about 9–19%.

### Against Asian podiums

World records are the far horizon. Asian podiums are the nearer target, and the 2026 Asian Games give a direct reading. Parul Chaudhary's 9:08.67 won bronze behind the winner Yavi's 9:04.36, a gap of only about 0.8%. Sable won Asian Games steeplechase gold in 2023 and Asian Championships gold in 2025. Gulveer won 10,000 m silver, 1500 m silver and 5000 m bronze at the 2026 Asian Games, and Sawan won marathon silver. Seema took the women's 10,000 m bronze.

In other words, India is already at Asian podium level in several distance events. The step from Asian podium to Olympic final is the steep one. Sable reached the Paris 2024 final and finished 11th in 8:14.18, about 0.9% slower than his own national record, which shows that championship finals are raced differently from paced record attempts.

COACH: Use percentage gaps to set realistic goals. An athlete 10% off the national record needs a multi-year development plan; an athlete 2% off can target it within a season or two. The same arithmetic works for any club runner: express the goal as a percentage improvement, and check that it is plausible over the time available.

### Why the marathon gap is so large

The marathon gap cannot be explained by talent alone, because the same Indian men who are 3–4% from world level on the track are 9% from it over 42.2 km. Several explanations are plausible, and all are hypotheses. Indian runners have tended to move to the marathon late. Marathon-specific volume and long-run durability may be under-developed. Domestic marathons are often run in heat and humidity. And very few Indians race in fast, deep, cool-weather marathons. Sawan's move to about 200 km a week and alternate-week 40–42 km long runs, and his debut in Rotterdam rather than a domestic race, can be read as an attempt to address exactly these factors.

### Limits of the comparison

These gaps compare single best-ever marks, not the depth of a nation's field. They also compare a small number of Indian athletes, some of whom set records once in ideal conditions, with world records set by the best athletes in history. They are a useful map of relative strengths, not a verdict on potential.

## 95. Through the AEI Lens

Imagine a national coach in Bengaluru opening a folder for India's ten best distance runners, hoping to compare them across the ten AEI domains introduced earlier in this book. The Performance page is full: race results, dates, venues, splits. The Altitude page has some entries: Ooty, Colorado Springs, St Moritz, dates of camps. Then the pages go quiet. Aerobic capacity: blank. Threshold: blank. Economy: blank. Haemoglobin mass: blank. Heat tolerance: blank. That is not necessarily because these tests are never done inside Indian camps; it is because almost nothing about them is public, and a coach cannot learn from numbers that nobody can see. For a book built on measurement, that silence is itself a finding.

This chapter applies the AEI domains to what is publicly known about Indian elites. It must be read with a clear warning: public data on the physiology of Indian distance runners are very limited. Everything below that goes beyond race results is a hypothesis, stated so that it can be tested, not a conclusion.

### Domain by domain

|Domain|What is public|Hypothesis|Data to collect|
|Performance|Full race histories|Men's track near world level; marathons lag|Standardised race conditions log|
|Aerobic|One 1991 study of Bengali girls (VO₂max 43.0)|Modern elites far higher; no current data|VO₂max and HR–pace markers each season|
|Threshold|None|Threshold development drives recent 10,000 m gains|Lactate or critical-speed tests|
|Economy|None|May limit marathon more than track|Running economy at marathon pace|
|Oxygen|Anaemia widespread in Indian women|Iron deficiency a key risk for women|Ferritin, Hb, Hb mass|
|Cardiovascular|Screening guidance for marathons|Unknown; screening matters|ECG and echo screening records|
|Recovery|Injury reports (Sable, Jinson, Harmilan)|Small elite, high injury cost|HRV, sleep, load and injury logs|

### Performance and durability

The Performance domain is the only one with rich public data, and Chapter 94 has already read it: men's track gaps of about 3–5%, marathon gaps of 9% for men and 19% for women. The simplest hypothesis consistent with this pattern is a Durability deficit. An athlete whose 10,000 m is within 3% of world level but whose marathon is 9% away is, in AEI terms, losing more over the final third of the race than their speed would predict. Sawan Barwal's alternate-week long runs of 40–42 km, and his stated focus in Nagoya on maintaining the pace to the finish, point to the kind of work that addresses durability.

KEY: For Indian marathoners, the Durability domain is the most likely single limiter. It is also one of the cheapest to measure: the pace and heart-rate decay over the last 10 km of long runs and races.

### Aerobic, threshold and economy

The only peer-reviewed aerobic capacity figure we found for Indian distance runners is from 1991: Bengali female long-distance runners averaged a relative VO₂max of 43.0 ml/kg/min on a cycle ergometer. It is useful as history, not as a description of today's elites. By comparison, one widely cited study found Kenyan runners averaging about 79.9 ml/kg/min at sea level. No public threshold or economy data exist for current Indian elites. The hypothesis worth testing is that recent track gains owe much to threshold development under structured coaching, and that economy at marathon pace may be a hidden limiter.

### Oxygen transport and the women's question

The Oxygen domain raises the most urgent public-health signal. A venous-blood survey across eight Indian states found anaemia in 44% of adolescent girls and 41% of adult women. A 2025 study of 104 Tamil Nadu female athletes, not runners, found about 53.8% were anaemic. If even part of this burden reaches young female distance runners, it could help explain why the women's long events lag further behind world level than the men's. This is a hypothesis, but an easily testable one.

COACH: For every female distance runner you coach, ask for haemoglobin and ferritin at least twice a year, and before and after any altitude block. It is cheap, it is available in almost every town, and an untreated iron deficiency can undo months of good training.

### Heat and altitude

The Altitude domain has the best circumstantial evidence. Ooty (2,240 m) is roughly Iten-equivalent in elevation. Gopi credited it after his Valencia personal best. Sable's coach in Colorado Springs described his adaptation on return to altitude as almost instant. But no peer-reviewed study of Indian athletes' haematology at Ooty was found, so the dose, response and individual variation remain unknown.

The Heat domain is the most surprising gap. India is one of the hottest places in the world to train, and domestic races such as the 2026 TCS World 10K in Bengaluru were run in hot, humid conditions. Yet we found no documented, deliberate heat-acclimation protocol for Indian elites. Heat exposure appears incidental rather than planned. Indian runners may be well heat-habituated but not systematically heat-adapted for specific races.

### Recovery and cardiovascular

In a small elite group, injuries are expensive. Sable's knee, Jinson's Achilles, Harmilan Bains' knee surgery, Priti Lamba's ankle fracture at a water jump and Monika Athare's cartilage injury all removed key athletes from competition. A Recovery domain built on load, sleep, heart-rate variability and injury history is not a luxury here. For the Cardiovascular domain, the Indian Heart Journal has published guidance on preventing sudden cardiac death in Indian marathoners, which argues for structured pre-participation screening.

### What India should start collecting

The single most useful step would be a standard, confidential national athlete data set that follows every senior and junior distance runner. At minimum it would include standardised race conditions, twice-yearly aerobic and threshold tests, iron status, haemoglobin mass around altitude camps, heat-acclimation sessions, long-run pace decay and injury history. The National Centre of Sports Science and Research in New Delhi, inaugurated in April 2025, and its partner university departments could anchor this work.

## 96. A Roadmap for the Next Indian Elite

Somewhere in India today, a 14-year-old is running to school along a road that has never been measured. It might be a hill road in Chamba, a canal path near Meerut, a dusty track in Beed. The runner does not know yet that the national 10,000 m record for women has stood since before they were born, or that no Indian man has ever broken 2:10 for the marathon. Whether that runner ever reaches a start line in a World Championships final depends less on their lungs than on a chain of decisions made by other people: a school coach who notices, a hostel that has a place, a job that pays, a camp at altitude, a race abroad, a test that catches an iron deficiency, a coach who knows when to hold them back. This chapter is about building that chain.

The recommendations below come directly from the evidence in this Part. Each is linked to a pattern observed in Indian elites and to principles set out earlier in this book. They are offered for discussion, not as official policy.

### Six priorities

|Priority|Evidence in this Part|Action|Success looks like|
|Talent pathways|Army and Railways carry most elites; late developers common|Link schools, SAI hostels and services trials; keep doors open to age 25+|More athletes entering from more states|
|Heat and altitude|Altitude used; heat unplanned|Plan altitude blocks and heat acclimation by season|Records set at home and abroad|
|Coaching education|Records fell under strong coach-led groups|Mentor Indian coaches alongside foreign experts|Several coach-led groups producing records|
|Data systems|Almost no public physiology|National AEI-style data set|Every elite with ten-domain profile|
|Anti-doping culture|India in AIU Category A since 2026|Education, testing, transparent support|Fewer violations; Category improves|
|Women's pathway|Long women's events lag most|Jobs, iron screening, marathon development|Women's 10,000 m and marathon records move|

### 1. Talent pathways

The Army and the Railways already act as India's distance academies. The priority is to connect them to the places where talent begins. SAI hostels, state academies such as Madhya Pradesh's in Bhopal, and long-serving local coaches such as Vijender Singh in Nashik should have clear routes to services and Railways trials. Talent identification should stay open into the mid-twenties, because many of India's record holders, including Sable, Sawan and Gopi, reached their peaks late. Khelo India support, set at ₹6.28 lakh per athlete per year, can bridge the gap between a hostel place and a job.

KEY: India does not need a new pathway so much as better links between the ones it already has: school, hostel, services or Railways job, national camp.

### 2. Use heat and altitude deliberately

India has a rare combination: some of the world's hottest training conditions and genuine high-altitude venues at Ooty (2,240 m) and Shilaroo (about 2,450 m), with Shillong planned. The roadmap should treat both as planned tools. Altitude blocks should be timed to competitions and monitored with haemoglobin and, where possible, haemoglobin mass. Heat acclimation should be scheduled before hot championships, using the protocols set out in Parts III and IV, rather than left to chance.

COACH: Write the season plan with two extra lines: where the athlete will sleep at altitude, for how long and when; and when the heat-acclimation block will start before a warm-weather race. If neither is planned, the environment is coaching the athlete instead of you.

### 3. Coaching education

Records fell fastest when a clear method met committed athletes: Snesarev's group from 2005, Amrish Kumar with Sable, Simmons in Colorado Springs, Markose's Project 2:09. The lesson is not to import more foreign coaches but to build Indian coaching depth alongside them. A ministry official has estimated that India is short of roughly 750 coaches. Pairing Indian coaches with every foreign expert, and requiring a written, shared training philosophy for national groups, would help the method outlast any single coach.

### 4. Data systems

Chapter 95 showed how little is publicly known about Indian elites beyond their race results. The roadmap should create a confidential national athlete data set using the ten AEI domains, anchored at the National Centre of Sports Science and Research and its partner universities, with clear consent and data-protection rules. Even simple, standardised field data, collected consistently, would transform the evidence base within five years.

### 5. Anti-doping culture

This must be stated plainly and neutrally. In April 2026 the Athletics Integrity Unit moved India's athletics federation into Category A, its highest doping-risk group, alongside Russia, Belarus, Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria and Ukraine. WADA's 2024 figures show 260 adverse analytical findings from 7,113 Indian tests across all sports. Among the distance runners discussed in this Part, two sustained cases are documented: Sanjivani Jadhav received a two-year ban in 2019 for the masking agent probenecid, and Kartik Kumar accepted a three-year ban from USADA in 2025 for anabolic steroids. A credible system protects clean athletes by combining education, frequent out-of-competition testing, transparent medical support and supplement guidance. Category A rules already require at least three no-notice tests before World Championships and Olympics, including blood-passport and EPO tests for events from 800 m upward.

### 6. The women's pathway

The women's long events show the largest gaps. The roadmap should guarantee secure jobs for leading women, screen iron status routinely, and build a deliberate pathway from the 5000 m and steeplechase into the 10,000 m, half marathon and marathon, with access to fast international races.

### A four-year roadmap

1) Year 1: agree a national AEI-style data set; screen iron status for all national-camp women; schedule altitude and heat blocks in every national group's plan.

2) Year 2: link SAI hostels and state academies to services and Railways trials; begin paired Indian–foreign coaching for each event group.

3) Year 3: send a group of 5000 m and 10,000 m runners into marathon-specific development, with one fast international marathon each year.

4) Year 4: review the data set; publish anonymised findings; set new targets for the 2030s, including an Indian man under 2:10 and new women's 10,000 m and marathon records.
